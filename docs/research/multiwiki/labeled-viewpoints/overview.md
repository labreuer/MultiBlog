## From inline Claude summary

**The comparison layer is the unbuilt part.** Passage-level alignment of rival texts exists only where a coordinate system pre-exists, such as verse numbers in BibleHub and Sefaria or the thesis node in Kialo, or in Xanadu's transpointing windows, which no wiki adopted. Only four designs make perspectives answer the same question: ProCon, Verity, Zondervan's Counterpoints series, and comparison charts. Only Counterpoints has the perspectives respond to each other. The academic record adds two sharp precedents. Wikidata lets contradictory sourced claims coexist but rules that ranks record consensus, never your own view. Cyc's microtheories show that contributors struggle to pick the right context when contexts are first-class.

**Design implications the report draws** for something MultiBlog-shaped: label the text from a declared taxonomy the reader can see whole, give each perspective a fixed slot the way a Talmud page fixes Rashi inside and Tosafot outside, separate "who holds this position" from "who wrote this sentence", and manufacture the missing coordinate system with a quote anchor into a shared factual spine. Your existing quote anchors and side-by-side route are the raw material for that last piece.

## My thoughts

The first question might be: if two people name the same article "institution", is it even about the same thing? Or does one mean something like "prestigious organization" while another means an interconnected set of repertoires? Names are not synonyms; synonyms must be structural.

What I'm thinking is that most differences will be multi-article and may well "peel away" from an existing point of view, perhaps in a disorganized fashion at first (say, differences in three different not-obviously-related articles). But over time, the differences may "pull together", yielding additional clarity. The notion of 'schools of thought' is a guiding idea, here. So, I think the term 'embedding' is good enough to be getting going.

How does an embedding differ from tags? By having composite (non-aggregate) structure. Whether it's two ontologies which have some partial translation to each other or something else, mere atomistic names won't do it. Note that MacIntyre: thin language will probably be helpful, here.
