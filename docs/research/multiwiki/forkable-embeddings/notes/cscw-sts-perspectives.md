# CSCW / STS / organizational-knowledge precedents for forkable "embeddings"

Scope note: this file deliberately does **not** repeat what the prior survey ([`labeled-viewpoints/notes/academic-research.md`](../../labeled-viewpoints/notes/academic-research.md), [`labeled-viewpoints/report.md`](../../labeled-viewpoints/report.md)) already covers — Wikipedia NPOV/conflict studies, Omnipedia/Manypedia/WikiGap/Contropedia, ConsiderIt/Reflect, Cohere/Contested Collective Intelligence, Dispute Finder, Deliberatorium, Polis, Kialo, Everything2, Federated Wiki, Reagle, Tkacz, and the DiSalvo/Mouffe summary. Page numbers are given where the primary text was read in this session; anything taken from an abstract, a search summary or a secondary source is labelled as such.

Feature shorthand used below: **(1)** whole entities or passages assigned to one or more embeddings; **(2)** relationships between entities assigned to an embedding; **(3)** embeddings fork from each other; **(4)** the entity's revision is recorded on entry, and later revisions can be re-affirmed as still fitting. **"Comparing is hard"** is the prior survey's finding that in multi-perspective wikis "forking was too easy and comparing was too hard."

## Q1. Boundary objects, *Sorting Things Out*, and boundary negotiating artifacts: what happens to an object that sits in several communities' structures at once?

### Takeaway
The founding STS answer is that an object can belong to several social worlds, with a different meaning in each, **without any consensus**, provided that it is "weakly structured in common use, and becomes strongly structured in individual-site use." Several of the four kinds Star & Griesemer name are, in effect, comparison substrates (a shared outline filled in differently). Bowker & Star add the costs of that arrangement: *torque* when a thing is held in a structure that does not fit it, *convergence* (a classification and its world gradually remake each other), and the conservatism a revisable classification needs if it is to stay comparable across revisions. Lee (2007) shows the other half: in new, unroutinized collaboration, the artifacts that matter are rival *structuring* artifacts that compete, get derived from one another, and supplant one another. That is forking, observed ethnographically.

### Inferences
- **For (1), membership in several embeddings.** Star & Griesemer's "weakly structured in common use, strongly structured in individual-site use" amounts to a specification: the shared entity (a doc or passage) stays thin, and each embedding adds its own strong structure (relations, framing, commentary) *around* it rather than *in* it. That matches keeping the doc's ydoc canonical while embedding membership is stored in separate rows, much as the anchor tables already are.
- **Coincident boundaries are the missing comparison substrate.** "Same boundaries but different internal contents" is exactly what the prior survey said was lacking: a shared coordinate system that each perspective fills in differently. Two embeddings that include the *same* set of passages, but assert different relations among them, form a coincident-boundary pair, and a comparison view should start by aligning on that shared outline. This supports the survey's recommendation to manufacture a "shared factual spine" and says what shape it should take: a common outline, not a common interpretation.
- **For (2), relations belong to the embedding and need no consensus.** Since "representations … contain at every stage the traces of multiple viewpoints … and incomplete battles," an embedding's relations should record who asserted them and against what; they should not be treated as settled facts to be merged.
- **For (4), recording the revision is ICD-style comparability, and re-affirmation is its release valve.** The ICD shows the price of pinning. To stay comparable across revisions, a classification freezes its numbering and keeps "anachronistic categories" alive. Recording the entity's revision at entry and requiring *explicit re-affirmation* avoids that trap: the embedding stays pinned (comparable) until someone vouches for the new text, and nobody has to freeze the text itself.
- **For (3), forks need an algorithm back to the parent.** The ICD committee kept control of national and insurer variants by issuing *rules for modification* that make "working back from the modified list to the ICD itself" possible. The design translation: a forked embedding should store its fork point, plus a diff against the parent expressed as add, remove, or re-relate operations, and not just a copy. That turns comparison into a computable lineage question, which is the fix for "comparing is hard."
- **Lee's lifecycle maps onto the user's "scattered, then pulls together" observation.** A divergence begins as private self-explanation artifacts (one person's notes across several articles), becomes inclusion artifacts (proposals to a group), and at most becomes a structuring artifact that competes for primacy. An embedding feature could support the early stage (a private or draft embedding that collects scattered passages) before it asks anyone to name a school. The curators' shock ("removing and changing whole concepts") is what happens when a derived structure supplants its parent *invisibly*, which argues for fork-notification and "what did they drop?" views.
- **Torque and convergence are warnings for (1).** Placing an author's passage in an embedding they reject is torque on that author, and a popular embedding may, over time, reshape how the passage is read (convergence). This argues for letting authors see, and perhaps annotate, which embeddings hold their text — an extension of the existing rule that a tag chip is exactly as private as the thing it is on.
- **"Names are not synonyms."** The NIC "injection" case is a clean analogue of the user's "institution" example. A label shared across communities is not evidence of a shared referent, and equivalence "is reached by negotiation." Embedding membership should therefore never be inferred from matching titles.

## Q2. Common information spaces and articulation work

### Takeaway
The CSCW tradition of the common information space (CIS) holds that a shared repository does not by itself produce shared meaning. Meanings are "debated and resolved, at least locally and temporarily," and a CIS is dialectical: *open and malleable* inside a community, but *packaged and closed* (as a boundary object or immutable mobile) whenever it must travel between communities. Later field studies found that large integration efforts *reproduce* fragmentation, and that heterogeneous teams struggle to reconcile "differences in team perspectives."

### Cited Findings
**Bannon & Bødker 1997** — Liam Bannon & Susanne Bødker, "Constructing Common Information Spaces," *ECSCW '97: Proceedings of the Fifth European Conference on CSCW*, Lancaster, pp. 81–96 (verified). [EUSSET PDF](https://dl.eusset.eu/bitstreams/2e165755-c4d2-4765-934a-0cbd66b527a6/download); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-015-7372-6_6).
- On interpretation: "the meaning of the terms or objects are not simply 'given', but require an effort of interpretation on the part of the human actors who inhabit this space" (p. 82). — [PDF](https://dl.eusset.eu/bitstreams/2e165755-c4d2-4765-934a-0cbd66b527a6/download)
- They quote **Schmidt & Bannon 1992** (Kjeld Schmidt & Liam Bannon, "Taking CSCW Seriously: Supporting Articulation Work," *CSCW* journal 1(1–2), 1992 — venue from the citation, not independently opened): "Cooperative work is not facilitated simply by the provision of a shared database, but requires the active construction by the participants of a common information space where the meanings of the shared objects are debated and resolved, at least locally and temporarily. Objects must thus be interpreted and assigned meaning, meanings that are achieved by specific actors on specific occasions of use." A CIS is "constituted and maintained by different actors employing different conceptualizations and multiple decision making strategies." They switched from "shared" to "common" because it "indicates the transient and instrumental aspects of people having information 'in common'" (pp. 84–85). — [PDF](https://dl.eusset.eu/bitstreams/2e165755-c4d2-4765-934a-0cbd66b527a6/download)
- Packaging: in distributed settings there is "a much greater need for refining and 'packaging' information into a meaningful context … and the recipient is also required to expend some effort in order to 'unpack' this information, and hopefully be able to re-create the context of its transmission" (p. 83). — [PDF](https://dl.eusset.eu/bitstreams/2e165755-c4d2-4765-934a-0cbd66b527a6/download)
- The dialectic: "It is this tension between the need for openness and malleability of information on the one hand, and, on the other, the need for some form of closure, to allow for forms of translation and portability between communities, that we believe characterises the nature of common information spaces … CISs are both open and closed." They seek to show "how differing viewpoints, local contingencies and multiple interests are temporarily reconciled … how information items may be supplied with some kind of portable context; how local contexts are re-established based on the unpacking of the information." — [PDF](https://dl.eusset.eu/bitstreams/2e165755-c4d2-4765-934a-0cbd66b527a6/download)
- Design consequence: "A common information space is not just a repository of information constituted once and for all … Designing a common information space entails concern for the possibilities of sharing, looking over shoulders, etc. on the one hand; for the rules of submission of information on the other; … the possible roles of human mediators; … frontstage/backstage concerns, and the potential reworking of rules." — [PDF](https://dl.eusset.eu/bitstreams/2e165755-c4d2-4765-934a-0cbd66b527a6/download)

**Rolland, Hepsø & Monteiro 2006** — "Conceptualizing common information spaces across heterogeneous contexts: mutable mobiles and side-effects of integration," *CSCW 2006* (Banff) (verified). The paper draws on Actor-Network Theory and fieldwork in an international oil and gas company. It develops "a dynamic perspective on CIS emphasizing how CIS is malleable, open and achieved in practice," and argues that "large-scale CIS efforts inherently tend to reproduce fragmentation as an unintended consequence of integrating heterogeneous sources of information" (abstract, via search). — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1180875.1180951)

**Zhang & Sarcevic 2017** — Zhan Zhang & Aleksandra Sarcevic, "Constructing Common Information Spaces across Distributed Emergency Medical Teams," CSCW 2017. The challenges they report include "reconciling differences in team perspectives and information needs, all while having minimal technology support." The analysis uses "Bossen's seven CIS parameters" (Bossen, CSCW 2002). — [PDF](https://webpage.pace.edu/zzhang/papers/CSCW_2017.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Embeddings are CIS "packages."** When a passage joins an embedding it is packaged: pulled out of its home doc's flow and given portable context (why it is in *this* embedding, and with what relations). Bannon & Bødker's warning is that the receiver must "unpack" and re-create context. An embedding view should therefore always offer a jump back into the source doc at the recorded revision, the "portable context." MultiBlog's quote anchors and `?at=` revision links already provide this mechanism.
- **Open inside, closed at the boundary, which argues for (4).** Within a single embedding, members can freely re-edit relations (open and malleable). When the embedding is *compared* with another or *forked*, the comparison needs closure: the pinned revision numbers. Pinning is the "closure/punctuation" that makes the embedding portable, and re-affirmation is the controlled reopening.
- **Integration reproduces fragmentation.** Rolland et al. are a caution against a feature that auto-merges embeddings or computes a single "consensus embedding." Their evidence suggests such merges generate new side-effects and fragments. Keep embeddings side by side; do not reconcile them into one.
- **Rules of submission and human mediators** correspond to embedding governance: who may add passages and relations, and whether an embedding has curators. Bannon & Bødker treat these as first-class design concerns, not afterthoughts.

## Q3. Perspective making and perspective taking (Boland & Tenkasi) and Carlile's boundaries

### Takeaway
Boland & Tenkasi's program *did* build a tool: **Spider**, a cause-mapping system in which each manager draws a directed graph of the factors and causal relations that make up their perspective, and then exchanges and discusses the graph. They tested it informally in several organizations. The headline finding was that exchanging these *structural* representations "almost always raises awareness of differences in perspectives that had gone undetected, sometimes for many years." They also found that people build maps by telling stories, not by abstract analysis. The 1995 *Organization Science* paper added a design agenda of discussion forums and narrative maps; I found no formal evaluation of that agenda. Carlile (2004) supplies the escalation ladder: syntactic → semantic → pragmatic boundaries, requiring transfer → translation → transformation.

That successor is unverified. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/200752033_Designing_Information_Technology_to_Support_Distributed_Cognition)

**Carlile 2004** — Paul R. Carlile, "Transferring, Translating, and Transforming: An Integrative Framework for Managing Knowledge Across Boundaries," *Organization Science* 15(5): 555–568, DOI 10.1287/orsc.1040.0094 (verified). The abstract, verbatim: "A framework is developed that describes three progressively complex boundaries — syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic — and three progressively complex processes — transfer, translation, and transformation. The framework is used to specify the practical and political mismatches that occur when innovation is desired and how this relates to the common knowledge that actors use to share and assess each other's domain-specific knowledge." It uses innovation to "explore the negative consequences of the path-dependent nature of knowledge." Its illustration is "a collaborative engineering tool in the early stages of a vehicle's development." — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v15y2004i5p555-568.html)

**Bechky 2003** (same question, evidence from the shop floor) — Beth A. Bechky, "Sharing Meaning Across Occupational Communities: The Transformation of Understanding on a Production Floor," *Organization Science* 14(3): 312–330 (verified). The study is a year-long ethnography at a semiconductor-equipment maker ("EquipCo"). Knowledge-sharing difficulties were "rooted in differences in their language, the locus of their practice, and their conceptualization of the product." ### Inferences
- **Spider is the closest built precedent for (2).** Its perspective *is* a set of relations between named factors, not a set of labels, which supports the user's claim that "synonyms must be structural." Two people's "institution" nodes can be compared by what they are linked to, not by name. The empirical result (hidden differences surfaced "sometimes for many years" late) suggests that the *act of building relations inside an embedding* is itself the perspective-making step. Embedding authoring should be cheap and exploratory ("discovery mode"), not a form to fill in.
- **Narrative first, structure second.** Map-makers justified each edge with a story. So a relation in an embedding should be able to carry a quote-anchored passage as its warrant ("A causes B, because of this passage"), and MultiBlog's quote anchors provide exactly that. It also suggests embeddings will be seeded from essays (perspective making by narrative) and only later distilled into relations.
- **Carlile's ladder tells you which comparison to show.** At a *syntactic* boundary (same meanings, different vocabulary), a term mapping between embeddings suffices. At a *semantic* boundary (same words, different meanings: the "institution" case), comparison must show each side's relations around the term (Spider-style). At a *pragmatic* boundary (different interests, knowledge "at stake"), no view resolves it, and the tool can only make the stakes and the lineage visible. A comparison UI that treats every divergence as a vocabulary mapping will fail at the upper two levels.
- **"Tales of eye witnesses … each tells us a 'complete' story, but tells a different one"** is a precise statement of the rival-tellings-of-a-history use case. It argues that the unit of comparison is the whole embedding (a complete story), so side-by-side comparison should align whole structures, not individual claims. Individual-claim alignment is what the argument-mapping systems covered in the prior survey do.

- No formal evaluation of Spider (controlled study, adoption numbers) was found. The evidence is the authors' qualitative report.
- The "Theory Garden" successor is unverified.

## Q4. Ontology work as community work (GEON, EML/LTER, metadata friction, community-built ontologies)

### Takeaway
The cyberinfrastructure ethnographies say that building a shared formal vocabulary *surfaces* disagreements that had been held "unproblematically." They also say that the working resolutions are coarsen, agree provisionally, defer, or encode both — and that the promised technical option of *representing* disagreement went unused. An ontology is also never finished: it needs "continuous care," and its success is defined by community uptake rather than completeness. The same standard can carry two incompatible histories at once. Community-archive systems (fluid ontologies, Mukurtu) are the one family that deliberately stores several communities' descriptions of the *same* item side by side.

### Cited Findings
**Ribes & Bowker 2009** — David Ribes & Geoffrey C. - The case is GEON (the GEOscience Network), a cyberinfrastructure project building computational ontologies for earth-science subdisciplines. "Without maintenance … even the most cautiously developed ontology may become useless over time" (pp. 13–14). Success moved from "is the ontology complete?" to "is the routine sufficiently representative of the community?" (p. 17). Workshop participants were "standing-in as representatives of a larger epistemic community" (p. 14). — [PDF](https://ics.uci.edu/~gbowker/publications/Ribes%20Bowker%20-%20Between%20Meaning%20and%20Machine.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Proterozoic/Algonkian is the "institution" problem with data attached.** The two names approximately co-refer, occasionally diverge, and carry different institutional weight. GEON's live options map directly onto embedding features. "Decrease granularity" is a shared parent term that both embeddings can include. "Working agreement" is an embedding scoped to a question rather than to "the whole community." "Rain-check" is an explicitly *open* relation. "Encode both" is the color-legend solution, which is simply two embeddings. The fact that "represent the uncertainty" was promised and **never used** is a caution: do not rely on users filling in uncertainty or confidence fields. Plurality has to be the default structure (separate embeddings), not an annotation on one shared structure.
- **"Momentary agreements can become programmed commitments"** is the strongest argument for (4). Recording the revision at entry makes explicit *which* momentary agreement was programmed in, and re-affirmation turns a silent commitment into a dated, attributable act. It also answers "continuous care": an embedding whose members have many un-re-affirmed newer revisions is visibly stale, and a staleness indicator is cheap to compute from the pinned revisions.
- **Two histories of one standard is feature (2) plus comparison.** Millerand & Bowker show that rival tellings can coexist about the *same* events and artifacts, and that they are not two stages of one story. An embedding that holds relations (A led to B, "success" versus "mixed picture") over the same passages is the natural container. Comparing two such embeddings means showing *where the same entities are related differently*.
- **Mukurtu is the closest deployed precedent for (1).** It gives one item many community records, keeps the original (scholarly) record, and does not rank them. Christen's line about "the one authority record" is the design stance MultiBlog would take by refusing a canonical embedding. What Mukurtu lacks, as far as I found, is (2) relations assigned per community, (3) forking, and (4) revision pinning.
- **Wikidata's uneven depth, with "leaders" driving it,** suggests embeddings will be deep only where a few committed curators exist. Design for a small number of deep embeddings and many shallow, scattered ones, which again matches Lee's self-explanation → structuring lifecycle.
- **Edwards et al.'s "metadata as ephemeral communication, not enduring product"** cuts against over-formalizing embeddings. Relations may serve better as conversation artifacts (Boland & Tenkasi's "occasion for conversation") than as a schema to validate.

## Q5. Trading zones, pidgins and creoles, and interactional expertise: coordinate systems that grow locally

### Takeaway
Galison's trading zone is the model for *local coordination without global agreement*: "Two groups can agree on rules of exchange even if they ascribe utterly different significance to the objects being exchanged." Contact languages grow from jargon to pidgin to creole, and a creole can become a new discipline (biochemistry). Collins, Evans & Gorman separate trading zones that *merge* (inter-language, creole) from those that *stay distinct* (fractionated, via boundary objects or interactional expertise). Their 2017 revision adds "ambassadors" who *represent* another community without translating it. Galison's "intercalated periodization" (secondary sources only) says that ruptures in theory, experiment and instruments do not coincide. That bears directly on modelling Kuhnian shifts as forks.

### Cited Findings
- **Galison 1997**, *Image and Logic: A Material Culture of Microphysics* (University of Chicago Press) (verified via Wikipedia and Galison's publications page). "Two groups can agree on rules of exchange even if they ascribe utterly different significance to the objects being exchanged" (p. 783, as quoted by Wikipedia). — [Wikipedia: Trading zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_zones); [Galison publications](https://galison.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/image-and-logic)
- Galison on contact languages, via a search summary citing *Image and Logic* p. 783: cultures in interaction "frequently establish contact languages, systems of discourse that can vary from the most function-specific jargons, through semispecific pidgins, to full-fledged creoles rich enough to support activities as complex as poetry and metalinguistic reflection." Also (p. 806, secondary): "in any exchange, the two subcultures may altogether disagree about the implications of the equivalencies established, the nature of the information exchanged, or the epistemic status of the coordination." — [search result source list incl. Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_zones) (the p. 806 quote was not read in a primary source)
- **Intercalated periodization** (secondary summary): Galison treats physics as three "autonomous yet interrelated" levels — theory, experiment and instrumentation — "each with its own unique periodization." He rejects Kuhnian "block periodization," expecting "the histories of the different subcultures … each to have their own breaks, with the breaks coinciding only rarely." — [Galison publications page](https://galison.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/image-and-logic); [secondary blog summary](https://lifetheuniverseandrendering.wordpress.com/2014/02/25/6-re-modeling-the-galisonian-model/) (not primary)
- **Collins, Evans & Gorman 2007**, "Trading zones and interactional expertise," *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science* 38: 657–666, DOI 10.1016/j.shpsa.2007.09.003 (verified). A trading zone is "any kind of interdisciplinary partnership in which two or more perspectives are combined and a new, shared language develops." Types are distinguished by whether the collaboration is "co-operative or coerced" and whether the end state is "a heterogeneous or homogeneous culture." "Interactional expertise is a central component of at least one class of trading zone" (abstract via search). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S003936810700060X); [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/53873)
- **Collins, Evans & Gorman, "Trading zones revisited"** (arXiv:1712.06327, December 2017; preprint, publication venue not verified), read in full:
  - The trading zone "was introduced by Peter Galison as a supposed resolution of the problem caused by Kuhn's notion of 'paradigm incommensurability'"; they apply incommensurability "far more generally to 'forms of life' which vary hugely in scale and are embedded within one another." "[W]here there is no problem of translation there is merely unproblematic 'trade'."
  - Galison's paradigm case is "the invention of biochemistry which grew out of the invention of a new language which captured the appropriate parts of the language of chemistry and the language of biology."
  - The 2×2 grid: *inter-language* (collaborative, homogeneous; biochemistry, nanoscience), *fractionated* (collaborative, heterogeneous; boundary object, interactional expertise), *subversive* (coerced, homogeneous), *enforced* (coerced, heterogeneous). In the fractionated cell "the parties … rather than attempting a merger, continue to maintain the difference between their forms-of-life even while they cooperate … sharing only a fraction of their respective forms-of-life."
  - **Ambassadorial model**: selected individuals learn the other group's language and "can then represent the thinking of the other group within the home group. Representing is not the same as translating; translation is always incomplete if not impossible across deep cultural divides."
  - A caution in their footnote: "The term 'boundary object' is often used rather loosely and care should be taken to make sure that real explanatory work is being done."
  — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.06327)
- The book-length follow-up is M. E. Gorman (ed.), *Trading Zones and Interactional Expertise: Creating New Kinds of Collaboration*, MIT Press, 2010 (JSTOR listing verified). It includes Galison's "Trading with the Enemy," which returned 403 and was not read. — [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5hhhrw)
- **Kellogg, Orlikowski & Yates 2006**, "Life in the Trading Zone: Structuring Coordination Across Boundaries in Postbureaucratic Organizations," *Organization Science* 17(1): 22–44 (verified). The abstract, verbatim: members "engage in a number of cross-boundary coordination practices that make their work visible and legible to each other, and that enable ongoing revision and alignment … these coordination practices do not eliminate jurisdictional conflicts, and often generate problematic consequences such as the privileging of speed over quality, suppression of difference, loss of comprehension, misinterpretation and ambiguity, rework, and temporal pressure." — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v17y2006i1p22-44.html)

### Inferences
- **Trading zones license the embedding model's core premise.** Two embeddings can include the *same* passage (agree on "rules of exchange") while ascribing "utterly different significance" to it. The system should never force a shared definition before inclusion.
- **Fractionated versus inter-language is (3) versus merge.** Most embedding pairs will stay *fractionated*: sharing some entities, keeping distinct forms of life. A few may *creolize* into a new school (biochemistry), which is the user's "pull together into a coherent school." The tool should support both and not treat merging as the goal. Creolization could appear in the data as a new embedding whose members are drawn from two parents: a merge-fork with *two* recorded parents (an "embedding DAG," not a tree).
- **Pidgin → creole is the lifecycle of a shared vocabulary between embeddings.** Early on, only a few mapped terms (jargon or pidgin: "their X ≈ our Y, for purposes of this question"). Later, a richer mapped vocabulary. That argues for a *partial, scoped* cross-embedding term mapping ("these two nodes co-refer for purposes of question Q"), matching Ribes & Bowker's "working agreement," rather than global synonymy. It also operationalizes "synonyms must be structural": a mapping is justified by relational context, not by name.
- **Ambassadors: "representing is not translating."** A comparison feature should let a member of embedding A write *A's view of B*, labelled as such — a representation, not a merged translation. That is a different object from either embedding, and it resembles Reflect's restatement primitive (covered in the prior survey) at the level of whole embeddings.
- **Intercalation and Kuhn.** If Galison is right that theory, experiment and instrument break at different times, then a "paradigm shift" is not a single fork event. It is a staggered series of forks across different kinds of entities (concept articles, method articles, instrument or data articles), which again fits the user's "scattered divergences that later pull together." Temporal views of a discipline's trajectory should let each *layer* of an embedding have its own fork points, rather than forking the whole embedding at one date.
- **Kellogg et al.'s costs** ("suppression of difference, loss of comprehension") are what to expect when coordination is optimized for speed. A comparison tool that summarizes differences too aggressively (for example, an LLM summary of "how A differs from B") risks exactly that. Structural diffs with drill-down to passages are safer.

### Gaps
- *Image and Logic* was not opened. The p. 783 quote is via Wikipedia, and the p. 806 quote and the intercalation summary are from secondary sources.
- "Trading zones revisited" is cited as an arXiv preprint; I did not verify a later journal version.

## Q6. Forking as an empirical social process (open source, wikis, platforms)

### Takeaway
Across two decades of open-source studies, forks rarely re-merge. Robles & González-Barahona found merges in 3.2% of 220 notable forks. Zhou, Vasilescu & Kästner found that only 16.18% of GitHub hard forks with an active upstream *ever* synchronize or merge, even though many maintainers *intend* to. Hard forks now emerge gradually and without deliberate planning out of "social forks" (only about 36% even rename). Stigma has largely gone. The binding constraint is tooling: monitoring divergence "is onerous," GitHub's network graph "does not scale," and "current tools work well for short-term social forks but … less well for coordinating changes across repositories that have diverged more significantly." The authors call for tools to "monitor, manage, and integrate changes across a family of hard forks." That is the "comparing is hard" problem, stated independently in software engineering.

### Cited Findings
**Zhou, Vasilescu & Kästner 2020** — Shurui Zhou, Bogdan Vasilescu & Christian Kästner, "How Has Forking Changed in the Last 20 Years? A Study of Hard Forks on GitHub," *ICSE 2020*, Seoul (verified). Read in full at [CMU PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf); [ICSE listing](https://2020.icse-conferences.org/details/icse-2020-papers/33/How-Has-Forking-Changed-in-the-Last-20-Years-A-Study-of-Hard-Forks-on-GitHub).
- Definitions: *hard forks* are "copying a project … to start an independent development line." *Social forks* are public copies on GitHub, often used for contributions or even bookmarking. — [PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)
- Data: a classifier found 15,306 hard forks, and 18 maintainers were interviewed (a 7% response rate). Outcomes: "cases where both the upstream repository and the hard fork remain active for extended periods of time are not common (… 1157 hard forks, 8.8%). Most hard forks actually survive the upstream project, if the upstream project was active when the fork was created (… 7280 hard forks, 47.6%), but many also run out of steam eventually (… 6671 hard forks, 43.6%)." Forks reviving a dead project account for 6.8% (1,052). — [PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)
- **Gradual emergence**: "Once the fork exists (initially created as social fork), there seems to be often a gradual development until developers explicitly consider their fork a separate development line. In fact, many hard forks seem to be triggered by rather small initial changes … only about 36% of the detected hard forks on GitHub have changed the project's name." — [PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)
- **Intent versus action on re-sync**: "even though many interviewees expressed intentions, we see little evidence of actual synchronization or merging across forks … Only 16.18% of all hard forks with active upstream repositories ever synchronize or merge." The explanation: "synchronization and merging becomes difficult once two repositories diverge substantially and … monitoring repositories can becoming overwhelming with current tools." One maintainer syncs only minor improvements "because the fork has diverged to[o] much"; another "only selectively synchronizes features of interest." — [PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)
- **Tooling gap**: "interviewees report that systematically monitoring changes from other repositories is onerous and that current tools like GitHub's network graph are difficult to use and does not scale … Current tools work well for short-term social forks but tend to work less well for coordinating changes across repositories that have diverged more significantly. This provides opportunities for researchers to explore tooling concepts that can monitor, manage, and integrate changes across a family of hard forks." They cite academic prototypes for monitoring (NeedFeed, ASE 2014; Zhou et al., "Identifying Features in Forks," ICSE 2018) and cross-fork patch porting (Ren, FSE 2019), which are "not yet accessible easily to practitioners." — [PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)
- **The specificity–generality dilemma** (why forks are unavoidable): projects "inclusive to all community contributions risk becoming so large and broad that they become expensive to maintain … projects staying close to their original vision … risk alienating users who do not fit that original vision, who then may create hard forks." "[A] family of related projects that serve slightly different needs … but still coordinate may be a way to overcome this … However, current technology does not support coordination across multiple hard forks well." — [PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)
- **Stigma gone**: "With GitHub's rebranding of the word forking, the stigma around hard forking seems to have mostly disappeared … Transparent tooling seems to help with acceptance and with considering multiple hard forks as part of a larger community." But "positive expectations can turn into frustration … if fragmentation leads to competition, confusion, and coordination breakdowns due to insufficient tooling." — [PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)

**Robles & González-Barahona 2012** — Gregorio Robles & Jesús M. González-Barahona, "A Comprehensive Study of Software Forks: Dates, Reasons and Outcomes," OSS 2012 (8th IFIP Int'l Conf. on Open Source Systems, Hammamet), IFIP AICT 378; Best Paper Award (verified). [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-33442-9_1); [replication page](https://gsyc.urjc.es/~grex/oss2012forking/).
- As summarized in Zhou et al. 2020: 220 forked projects referenced from Wikipedia in 2011 ("selection biased toward well-known projects"). Outcomes: "successful branching was most common (43.6%), followed by discontinuation of the fork (29.8%) and discontinuation of the upstream project (13.8%); failure of both and merges were relatively rare (8.7% and 3.2%)." Reasons, in Zhou's rendering: technical (diverging needs, the most common), governance disputes, discontinuation of the original, commercial, legal, personal. — [Zhou et al. PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)
- Abstract summary (via search): "forks occur in every software domain, have become more frequent in recent years, and very few forks merge with the original project." — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-33442-9_1)

**Nyman & Mikkonen 2011** — Linus Nyman & Tommi Mikkonen, "To Fork or Not to Fork: Fork Motivations in SourceForge Projects," OSS 2011 ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-24418-6_18)); also in *International Journal of Open Source Software and Processes* 3(3), 2011 ([IGI](https://www.igi-global.com/article/fork-not-fork/68147)) (both verified as listings). Per Zhou et al., variants "targeting specific needs or user segments that are not accommodated by the upstream project are the most common motivation." The abstract notes a fork "competes for the attention of the same developers." — [Zhou et al. PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf); [Hanken portal](https://harisportal.hanken.fi/en/publications/to-fork-or-not-to-fork-fork-motivations-in-sourceforge-projects/)

**Nyman & Lindman 2013** — Linus Nyman & Juho Lindman, "Code Forking, Governance, and Sustainability in Open Source Software," *Technology Innovation Management Review* 3(1): 7–12, January 2013 (verified). "[C]ode forking represents the single greatest tool available for guaranteeing sustainability in open source software … Forking, and even the mere possibility of forking code, affects the governance and sustainability of open source initiatives on three distinct levels: software, community, and ecosystem." Forking is "the 'invisible hand of sustainability.'" "Commonly, forking occurs due to a community's desire to create different functionality or focus the project in a new direction. Such forks are based on a difference in software requirements or focus, rather than a distrust of the project leaders. When they address disparate community needs, different versions can prosper." — [TIM Review](https://timreview.ca/article/644)

**Gamalielsson & Lundell 2014** — Jonas Gamalielsson & Björn Lundell, "Sustainability of Open Source software communities beyond a fork: How and why has the LibreOffice project evolved?," *Journal of Systems and Software* (2014) (verified via ScienceDirect listing). The study analyses committer retention across the LibreOffice/Apache OpenOffice split from OpenOffice.org. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0164121213002744)

### Inferences
- **Forks diverge, and nobody re-syncs without tooling.** Re-merge rates of 3.2% (notable projects) and 16.18% (any sync on GitHub), set against stated intentions to sync, mean an embedding-fork feature must assume divergence is permanent by default. The value has to come from *comparison*, not from eventual merging. That is the independent confirmation of "forking too easy, comparing too hard": GitHub made forking free and comparison stayed hard.
- **Fork-point + upstream-change feed is the minimum viable comparison.** What maintainers lacked was (a) a scalable way to monitor what changed upstream since the fork, and (b) selective adoption ("only selectively synchronizes features of interest"). For embeddings:
  - store the fork point (the parent embedding's state at fork time);
  - show "since you forked, the parent added or removed these members and relations";
  - allow cherry-picking individual relations or members across the fork.
  Feature (4)'s per-entity revision pins are exactly what makes "changed since fork" computable at passage granularity.
- **Hard forks emerge gradually from social forks, triggered by small changes.** This matches the user's "scattered divergences that later pull together." An embedding UI should make a *lightweight personal fork* (a social fork) nearly free and let it be *promoted* to a named, public school later. That is the analogue of renaming, which only 36% of GitHub hard forks bother to do. Fork identity should be decoupled from fork naming.
- **The specificity–generality dilemma is the embedding version of NPOV versus POV forks.** A single inclusive embedding becomes unmaintainable. A narrow one alienates dissenters, who fork. Zhou et al.'s proposed answer, "a family of related projects … that still coordinate," is the product: embeddings as a *family* with visible lineage, not as isolated copies.
- **Forks based on difference in requirements, not distrust, "can prosper"** (Nyman & Lindman). Embeddings that fork over *framing* (which question they serve), not over authority, should be the expected, healthy case. This recommends asking for a short fork rationale ("what question does this embedding serve differently?"), echoing GEON's "working agreement … relative to the science questions."
- **Invisible supplanting is the social failure mode** (Lee's curator "stunned" to find whole concepts removed). Fork notification to the parent embedding's curators was the missing Federated Wiki feature (prior survey). Lee shows the human cost when it is missing.

### Gaps
- I did not open Robles & González-Barahona's own text (the replication page's PDF link is dead, and IFIP/HAL was bot-blocked). Their outcome percentages come via Zhou et al.
- No CSCW/CHI study of *wiki* content forks as a social process (Fandom → independent wikis, Wikitravel → Wikivoyage) was found in this session beyond Fiesler & Dym's platform-level migration study. A search for Fandom/wiki fork studies returned trade press, not peer-reviewed work.
- I did not verify further empirical work on what *predicts* re-merging (for example, Businge et al. on divergent forks, or Zhou et al. 2019 "What the Fork," FSE) beyond titles surfacing in search.

## Q7. Recent CSCW/CHI (2015–2026) work on pluralistic or multi-perspective knowledge platforms, pluriversal design of knowledge infrastructures, and designing for disagreement

### Takeaway
Nothing close was found. I found no 2015–2026 CSCW/CHI paper that builds or studies a platform in which named, forkable perspective containers hold documents or passages *and* relations, with revision pinning. The recent CSCW work on pluralism is workshop-level (ethical pluralism, CSCW 2023) or about contesting technology futures in design sessions (agonism, 2024). Knowledge-graph studies (Wikidata) examine one shared ontology built by roles, not rival ontologies. The closest *deployed* precedents are older and come from outside the CSCW proceedings: Mukurtu's multiple community records, Srinivasan's fluid ontologies, and GEON's per-subdiscipline ontologies. The prior survey's DiSalvo/Mouffe material is not repeated here.

### Inferences
- **The gap is real, and it sits exactly where the embedding idea lies.** CSCW has decades of theory for plural meaning around shared artifacts (Q1–Q5), and software engineering has hard data on forking (Q6). The field has not produced a knowledge platform that combines them: shared entities, per-perspective relations, lineage between perspectives, and pinned revisions. A MultiBlog embedding feature would be novel in that combination, not just in its interface.
- **The theory gives design criteria that recent HCI work does not:** cooperation without consensus (Star & Griesemer), a comparison substrate via coincident boundaries, structural rather than lexical equivalence (Boland & Tenkasi; Carlile's semantic boundary; Proterozoic/Algonkian), revision-comparability trade-offs (ICD), and fork-family monitoring (Zhou et al.).
- **Evaluation precedent:** the only tool in this lineage evaluated *with users*, Spider, reports its effect as surfacing previously undetected differences. That suggests an evaluation criterion for embedding comparison: does comparing two embeddings reveal a difference the participants did not know they had?
