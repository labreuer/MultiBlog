# Precedents for forkable "embeddings": mapping, versioning, variation and collation mechanisms across KR, the Semantic Web, software-variation research and textual scholarship

Scope note. This file covers only what the prior survey ([`labeled-viewpoints/notes/academic-research.md`](../../labeled-viewpoints/notes/academic-research.md), Q2) did not: it skips Cyc microtheories, `ist`, C-OWL, CKR, the basic named-graphs paper, ViewPoints, Wikidata ranks and van Erp's polyvocal web.

Shorthand used on every item. **(1)** whole entities *or passages* are assigned to one or more containers. **(2)** *relationships between entities* are assigned to a container. **(3)** containers fork from one another. **(4)** a revision is pinned at the moment of membership, and later revisions can be *re-affirmed* as still fitting. **Cmp** says whether the item does anything about "forking is easy, comparing is hard".

Citation status. Unless a line says otherwise, authors, venue and year were checked against Crossref or the publisher/author PDF in this session. Quotations come from the primary text, which was downloaded and searched directly, not from a summariser. Items marked *(unverified)* were not confirmed.

## Q1. Partial, graded, directional, attributed translation between containers: which formalisms let a mapping belong to someone and record loss or non-correspondence?

### Takeaway
The logic-based formalisms make translation **directional and partial**, but none of them makes it **attributed**: DDL bridge rules, E-connection link properties and MCS bridge rules all belong to the target context and carry no author. The mapping standards (Alignment format/EDOAL, SSSOM, Lenticular Lens) do make a mapping a first-class, attributed, graded object. **Only SSSOM combines authorship, confidence, an explicit negative ("Not"), an explicit "no counterpart exists" (`sssom:NoTermFound`) and the *version* of each side's source.** That makes it the closest existing analogue to "this correspondence was asserted by X, against revision R of each side". SKOS, the most widely deployed option, has none of this built in. Halpin et al. showed empirically that even `owl:sameAs` does not mean "same".

### Cited Findings

**Distributed Description Logics (DDL): directional bridge rules and "holes"** *(anticipates coordinate systems between containers; partly (2); Cmp: no)*
- Borgida & Serafini, "Distributed Description Logics: Assimilating Information from Peer Sources," *J. on Data Semantics I*, LNCS 2800, pp. 153–184, 2003. — [Springer/Crossref DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-39733-5_7](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-39733-5_7)
- DDL is "a KR formalism that enables reasoning with multiple ontologies interconnected by directional semantic mapping (bridge rules)". The bridge rules from *i* to *j* come in two forms, **into** (i:A ⊑→ j:G) and **onto** (i:B ⊒→ j:H). Semantically, a domain relation r_ij ⊆ Δ^Ii × Δ^Ij must satisfy r_ij(C^Ii) ⊆ G^Ij for *into* and r_ij(C^Ii) ⊇ G^Ij for *onto*. A **hole** is an interpretation with an empty domain: "Holes are used for fighting propagation of inconsistency." — [Homola, "Distributed Description Logics Revisited," DL 2007, CEUR-WS 250](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-250/paper_51.pdf)
- The motivation is framed as perspective plurality: "The actual situation in the web is characterized by a proliferation of different ontologies. Each ontology describes a specific domain from different perspectives and at different level of granularity." DDL is "a set of stand alone DLs pairwise interrelated with each other via collection of bridge rules." — [Serafini & Tamilin, "Local tableaux for reasoning in distributed description logics," DL 2004, CEUR-WS 104](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-104/12Serafini-final.pdf)
- The directionality is the point: an into-rule gives j only a superset ("what i calls C falls under my G"), never equivalence. The domain relation is many-to-many, so one individual can correspond to several others or to none.

**E-connections: link properties between disjoint domains** *((2) as first-class "link properties"; enforces "names are not synonyms" structurally; Cmp: no)*
- Kutz, Lutz, Wolter & Zakharyaschev, "E-connections of abstract description systems," *Artificial Intelligence* 156(1):1–73, 2004. — [Crossref DOI 10.1016/j.artint.2004.02.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2004.02.002)
- The OWL realisation is Cuenca Grau, Parsia & Sirin, "Combining OWL ontologies using E-Connections," *J. Web Semantics* 4(1):40–59, 2006, which proposes it as "an alternative to the owl:imports construct" and provides "support for E-Connections in both an ontology editor, SWOOP, and an OWL reasoner, Pellet." — [author PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/4568/Econn.pdf)
- The critique of `owl:imports` is that it "results in a completely flat ontology, i.e., none of the imported axioms or facts retain their context ... in OWL, we can let in either all the axioms of a foreign ontology, or none." Link properties "allow to create classes in a certain ontology based on information from a different ontology, **provided that the domains of the ontologies are disjoint, both from a logical and a modeling perspective**." They "are logically interpreted as binary relations, where the first element belongs to its 'source' ontology and the second to its 'target ontology'." — [same PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/4568/Econn.pdf)

**Multi-Context Systems (MCS): nonmonotonic bridge rules, plus diagnosis of which bridges cause disagreement** *(coordinate systems; (2); **Cmp: yes**, through inconsistency explanation)*
- Brewka & Eiter, "Equilibria in Heterogeneous Nonmonotonic Multi-Context Systems," AAAI-07 (pages not verified). "The contexts themselves may be heterogeneous in the sense that they can use different logical languages and different inference systems, and **no notion of global consistency is required**. The information flow is modeled via so-called bridge rules which can refer in their premises to information from other contexts." — [AAAI PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2007/AAAI07-060.pdf)
- Bridge rule form (Def. 2): `s ← (r1:p1), …, (rj:pj), not (rj+1:pj+1), …, not (rm:pm)`. A context is Ci = (Li, kbi, bri), so **each context owns its incoming bridge rules**. The running example is two agents ("Mr.1" and "Mr.2") seeing a box from different angles, each with partial information. — [AAAI PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2007/AAAI07-060.pdf)
- Eiter, Fink, Schüller & Weinzierl define **diagnoses**: "pairs of sets of bridge rules, s.t. if we deactivate the rules in the first set, and add the rules in the second set in unconditional form, the MCS becomes consistent (i.e., admits an equilibrium)". They also define **inconsistency explanations**, which identify "a part of the bridge rules which is required to make the MCS inconsistent." — [KR 2010 PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/1265/1265-7422-1-PB.pdf); journal version *Artificial Intelligence* 216:233–274, 2014 ([Crossref DOI 10.1016/j.artint.2014.07.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2014.07.008))

**SKOS mapping properties: graded but unattributed** *((2) only by convention; no loss recording; Cmp: no)*
- `skos:exactMatch`: "a high degree of confidence that the concepts can be used interchangeably across a wide range of information retrieval applications". `skos:closeMatch`: "sufficiently similar that they can be used interchangeably in some information retrieval applications". closeMatch is deliberately **not transitive**, to avoid "compound errors" when combining mappings across more than two schemes. exactMatch is transitive. "skos:exactMatch is disjoint with each of the properties skos:broadMatch and skos:relatedMatch." Mapping properties are "used to state mapping (alignment) links between SKOS concepts in different concept schemes, where the links are inherent in the meaning of the linked concepts." — [W3C SKOS Reference, 2009](https://www.w3.org/TR/skos-reference/)
- The design history (Baker, Bechhofer, Isaac, Miles, Schreiber & Summers, *J. Web Semantics* 20, 2013) is the most relevant point for "grown-later coordinate systems". The working group made the formal distinction between mapping and intra-scheme semantic relations "very weak". A strong distinction "depended, conceptually, on the ability to 'contain' a concept scheme ... an issue ... the working group considered to be out of scope for SKOS". Also, "the evolution of concept schemes over time could mean that related concepts in two separate schemes could become aggregated into the same scheme, or vice versa". Per the SKOS Primer, mapping relationships are "less inherent to the meaning of the concepts they involve." — [arXiv 1302.1224](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.1224); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1570826813000176)
- SKOS has **no built-in slot for who asserted a mapping, confidence, source version, or "no match"**. Everything of that kind must be layered on (named graphs, SSSOM). This follows from the spec's own property list above.

**Halpin et al., "When owl:sameAs isn't the same" (ISWC 2010)** *(evidence that "names are not synonyms"; Cmp: shows that judging identity is itself contested)*
- Halpin, Hayes, McCusker, McGuinness & Thompson, ISWC 2010, LNCS 6496, pp. 305–320. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-17746-0_20); [open PDF (CiteSeerX)](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=7f5a8c4cebd05eb8b503aef7d9e73e87d37c90c3)
- "owl:sameAs can be viewed as encoding only one point on a scale of similarity, one that is often too strong for many of its current uses." The authors distinguish Identical-but-Referentially-Opaque, Matching, Similar, Related and Unrelated. They propose a **Similarity Ontology** whose properties are "not necessarily transitive and symmetric". — [PDF](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=7f5a8c4cebd05eb8b503aef7d9e73e87d37c90c3)
- Evaluation: 250 retrieved sameAs pairs were rated by three of the authors. "The κ for the six-way forced choice is 0.158, which is non-accidental but considered 'poor' agreement ... one judge preferring sameAs where another judge would be much more strict." — [PDF](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=7f5a8c4cebd05eb8b503aef7d9e73e87d37c90c3)
- A textbook example of "two articles titled X aren't about the same thing": DBpedia's *sodium* has a sameAs link to OpenCyc's *sodium*. But in OpenCyc an element "is the set of all pieces of the pure element", whereas DBpedia's sodium "includes all isotopes". The advice: "one should be wary of accepting conclusions over here that could have been drawn over there." — [PDF](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=7f5a8c4cebd05eb8b503aef7d9e73e87d37c90c3)

**Lenticular Lenses: context-specific identity built from attributed links** *((2) strongly; links carry provenance; **Cmp: yes**, through set algebra over lenses)*
- Idrissou, Hoekstra, van Harmelen, Khalili & van den Besselaar, "Is my:sameAs the same as your:sameAs? Lenticular Lenses for Context-Specific Identity," K-CAP 2017 (venue from the VU portal; DOI not checked). — [VU PDF](https://www.cs.vu.nl/~frankh/postscript/KCAP2017.pdf); [VU portal](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/is-mysameas-the-same-as-yoursameas-lenticular-lenses-for-context-)
- "whether two resources should be considered equal depends not only on their intrinsic properties, but also on the purpose or task". "In a first step, our system generates a set of probable links ... decorated with rich metadata describing **how, why, when and by whom** they were generated. In a second step, a user then selects the links which are suited for the current task and context, constructing a context-specific 'Lenticular Lens'. Such lenses can be combined using operators such as **union, intersection, difference and composition**." The system makes "explicit both the links and the methods for constructing those links as well as the justifications for them." — [VU PDF](https://www.cs.vu.nl/~frankh/postscript/KCAP2017.pdf)

**Alignment format, Alignment API and EDOAL (Euzenat et al.): alignments as first-class objects** *((2); confidence; an "incompatible" relation; Cmp: partial, through evaluation primitives)*
- A correspondence (Cell) has entity1, entity2, a **relation**, and a **measure** ("the confidence that the relation holds", a float in [0,1]). The relations are "=", ">", "<", "**%" (incompatible)**, HasInstance and InstanceOf. The alignment carries onto1/onto2, level, type/arity ("11", "?*", …) and method. "Extensions are additional string-valued qualified attributes" on Alignments and Cells, which is how provenance such as the creator is added. — [Alignment format documentation](https://moex.gitlabpages.inria.fr/alignapi/format.html)
- David, Euzenat, Scharffe & Trojahn dos Santos, "The Alignment API 4.0," *Semantic Web* 2(1):3–10, 2011: "The Alignment API provides abstractions for the notions of **network of ontologies**, alignments and correspondences as well as building blocks for manipulating them such as matchers, evaluators, renderers and parsers." — [SWJ page](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/content/alignment-api-40)
- EDOAL adds algebraic constructors (and/or/not, path composition), restrictions, **value transformations** ("Property values using different encoding or units can be aligned using transformations") and linkkeys. It maps *expressions* rather than names, which is a partial translation beyond 1:1 renaming. — [EDOAL documentation](https://moex.gitlabpages.inria.fr/alignapi/edoal.html)
- Euzenat, "Algebras of ontology alignment relations," ISWC 2008, LNCS 5318, pp. 387–402 (metadata verified). According to secondary descriptions, it provides a relation algebra with a composition table for combining correspondences. The abstract was not retrieved (Springer blocked). — [Crossref DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-88564-1_25](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-88564-1_25)
- Zimmermann & Euzenat, "Three semantics for distributed systems and their relations with alignment composition," ISWC 2006, LNCS 4273, pp. 16–29. Only the metadata was verified; the content was not read. — [Crossref DOI 10.1007/11926078_2](https://doi.org/10.1007/11926078_2)

**SSSOM (Simple Standard for Sharing Ontological Mappings): the attributed, versioned, negatable mapping record** *(**(2) and (4) strongly**; records loss; Cmp: partial)*
- Matentzoglu et al. (a 44-author consortium), "A Simple Standard for Sharing Ontological Mappings (SSSOM)," *Database* 2022: baac035. The aim is metadata "that makes imprecision, inaccuracy and incompleteness in mappings explicit". The paper names the problem "the lack of metadata such as the semantics of the correspondence (is it exact?) or its provenance (was it reviewed by a domain expert?)". — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/database/article/doi/10.1093/database/baac035/6591806)
- **Core model.** A mapping is a triple <subject, predicate, object> plus metadata covering "who authored the statement), the confidence with which the mappings holds, and its justification". The spec says: "the SSSOM standard posits that **there can be no mapping without some form of justification**" (`mapping_justification` is mandatory, drawn from the semapv vocabulary: ManualMappingCuration, LexicalMatching, LogicalReasoning, MappingChaining, MappingReview, …). — [SSSOM spec home](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/); [spec-model](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/spec-model/); [mapping_justification](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/mapping_justification/)
- **Attribution and grading.** The Mapping class has author_id ("persons or groups responsible for asserting the mappings"), creator_id, reviewer_id, `confidence` ("the creator's confidence"), `reviewer_agreement`, `similarity_score`, `curation_rule` ("a (potentially) complex condition executed by an agent that led to the establishment of a mapping"), `mapping_date`, `review_date`, `issue_tracker_item`, `derived_from` and `mapping_cardinality`. — [Mapping class](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/Mapping/)
- **Version pinning (feature 4).** `subject_source_version` / `object_source_version` hold the "Version IRI or version string of the source of the subject term" (example: a dated Mondo release IRI). The mapping set has `mapping_set_version`. "Propagatable" set-level slots, including both source versions, "MUST be interpreted as if all mappings within the set had that same value." The FAQ names the failure this prevents, "non-transparent incompleteness: We don't know when the mapping was created, on the basis of what version of the terminological source ... As time passes, we also lose confidence whether there would now be more suitable mappings". — [subject_source_version](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/subject_source_version/); [spec-model](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/spec-model/); [FAQ](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/faq/)
- **Recording loss and non-correspondence.** `predicate_modifier: Not` means "The meaning of the triple becomes subject_id is not a predicate_id match to object_id". The special value `sssom:NoTermFound` "MAY be used as the object_id of a mapping to explicitly state that the subject of said mapping cannot be mapped to any entity in the domain represented by the object_source slot." The confidence docs show Biomappings publishing a separate *negative* mapping set (e.g., a lexical match at 0.65 later judged wrong). — [PredicateModifierEnum](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/PredicateModifierEnum/); [spec-model](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/spec-model/); [confidence model](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/confidence-model/)
- **Composition ("grown" coordinate systems).** The chaining rules treat exactMatch, broad/narrowMatch and equivalence as chainable. They explicitly do not chain closeMatch, relatedMatch, hasDbXref or seeAlso, and they record `MappingChaining` as the justification. This is "structural, and should not be confused with proper reasoning". — [chaining rules](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/chaining-rules/)
- **Adoption.** The paper lists the Mondo Disease Ontology, EBI's OxO, NMDC, EOSC-Life, Biomappings, OpenTargets, IMPC, MGI and the Center for Cancer Data Harmonization. — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/database/article/doi/10.1093/database/baac035/6591806)

**Symmetric lenses: translation with an explicit "complement" for what does not carry over** *(records loss structurally; composable; Cmp: no)*
- Hofmann, Pierce & Wagner, "Symmetric Lenses," POPL 2011 (*SIGPLAN Notices* 46(1):371–384). In symmetric variants, "each structure contains information not present in the other". The paper presents "a new symmetric formulation, based on **complements** ... [that] admits a good notion of composition." — [author PDF](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~bcpierce/papers/symmetric.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/1925844.1926428](https://doi.org/10.1145/1925844.1926428)

**DOL / Ontohub: typed links in a network of ontologies** *((2); coordinate systems of graded strength; Cmp: no)*
- DOL "adds a meta-level" with heterogeneous imports, conservative extensions and **theory interpretations** (formal, logic-backed links). It also has "non-logical links, called alignments, with, e.g., statistical correspondences between ontology entities." It was standardised through ISO/TC 37 OntoIOp (and later the OMG). — [Mossakowski et al., arXiv 1208.0293](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.0293)
- Ontohub is the repository engine that "supports DOL's interpretations, equivalences and alignments". Its source is on GitHub. — [ResearchGate record](https://researchgate.net/publication/311668591_Ontohub_A_semantic_repository_for_heterogeneous_ontologies); [GitHub](https://github.com/ontohub/ontohub)

### Inferences
- **Answer to "which let a mapping belong to someone and record loss".**
  - SSSOM does both: author, creator and reviewer; `Not`; `NoTermFound`; confidence; and per-side source versions.
  - The Alignment format does both only in part: `%` (incompatible) plus a confidence measure, with authorship only through extension attributes.
  - Lenticular Lens gives authorship and method per link, and makes "which links I accept" a named, composable object.
  - Symmetric lenses record loss formally, as the complement, but have no authorship.
  - DDL, E-connections and MCS are directional and partial, but a bridge rule "belongs" to the target context, not to a person.
  - SKOS offers only graded strength (exact > close > broad/narrow/related), with no attribution or negation.
- **An embedding's translation layer maps closely onto an SSSOM mapping set whose "sources" are embeddings rather than ontologies.**
  - `subject_source` / `object_source` would name embeddings A and B.
  - `subject_source_version` / `object_source_version` would pin the `ydoc_update_id` (or embedding-membership revision) on each side.
  - `predicate_id` would carry a graded, directional relation: exact, close, broad or narrow.
  - `predicate_modifier: Not` would express "these two same-named articles are explicitly *not* the same", which is the user's "names are not synonyms" as a first-class record.
  - `NoTermFound` would express "this concept has no counterpart in B".
  - `reviewer_id` / `review_date` would supply re-affirmation.
  - This is a design inference, not something anyone has built for prose passages.
- The E-connection move of requiring *disjoint domains*, so that nothing is identical across components and only link properties connect them, is the strongest formal version of "synonyms must be structural". Cross-embedding identity would never be assumed from a shared title; it would exist only as an explicit link.
- The MCS diagnosis concept is the only KR mechanism found that *compares*. It localises a disagreement to the minimal set of bridge rules which, once removed or forced, restore agreement. For embeddings, that would mean "which translation assertions are doing the disagreeing". This also suggests that divergence can be *measured through the translation layer* rather than by diffing text.
- Halpin et al.'s κ = 0.158 is a warning for any UI that asks users to judge "same or not". Offering graded options (exact/close/related/unknown) plus attribution is more honest than a binary choice, because judges genuinely disagree.

### Gaps
- Euzenat's 2008 algebra and Zimmermann & Euzenat's 2006 semantics were verified bibliographically only; Springer blocked the abstracts. The claim about "disjunctions of base relations" is not quoted from the source.
- Euzenat & Shvaiko's *Ontology Matching* (Springer 2007; 2nd ed. 2013) was not opened; its definition of an alignment as a first-class object is taken from the API documentation and the SWJ paper instead *(book unverified this session)*.
- No empirical study was found of how often SKOS closeMatch vs exactMatch are used, or misused, across the LOD cloud. The search surfaced only small project-specific counts. Manaf, Bechhofer & Stevens (ESWC 2012) on the state of SKOS vocabularies was not retrieved.
- No adoption numbers were found for SSSOM's `Not` or `NoTermFound` in the wild. That negative mappings are published is shown only by the Biomappings example.
- Kutz et al.'s 2004 AIJ abstract was not read (ScienceDirect blocked). The E-connection description comes from the Cuenca Grau et al. OWL paper.

## Q2. Versioning and forking knowledge containers: pinning assertions to versions and re-affirming them

### Takeaway
The **re-affirmation** idea has a direct lineage. SHOE (2000) let a revision declare `BACKWARD-COMPATIBLE-WITH` and let data sources pin the ontology version they `USE`. It immediately hit the authority problem: anyone could declare a hijacking revision "compatible". OWL 2 inherited the vocabulary (`priorVersion`, `backwardCompatibleWith`, `incompatibleWith`) but gave it **no formal semantics**. Klein et al.'s OntoView made compatibility a *human judgement per concept* ("conceptual change" vs "explication change"), recorded as a relation between concept versions. The git-for-RDF systems (Quit Store, R43ples, TerminusDB) supply branch and merge, but their merges are set-based and blind to meaning. Nanopublications with Trusty URIs supply the immutable, retractable, per-assertion version. PROV and Memento/Robust Links supply the vocabulary for "derived from" and "cite this state".

### Cited Findings

**SHOE: version pinning, backward-compatibility declarations, and "perspectives"** *(**(4)**, (3), and a formal "perspective"; Cmp: no)*
- Heflin & Hendler, "Dynamic Ontologies on the Web," AAAI-2000 (pages not verified). — [AAAI PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2000/AAAI00-068.pdf)
- Syntax: `<ONTOLOGY ID="cs-dept-ontology" VERSION="1.1" BACKWARD-COMPATIBLE-WITH="1.0">`, `<USE-ONTOLOGY ID="univ-ontology" VERSION="1.0" …>`, and `<DEF-RENAME FROM="u.Department" TO="Department">`, so a data source pins the exact ontology version it commits to. "If the revision merely adds ontology elements, then it can be used to form perspectives that semantically subsume the original perspective ... it can specify that it is compatible with previous versions using the optional BACKWARD-COMPATIBLE-WITH field ... Agents and query systems ... can also use it in place of any of the ontologies that it is backward-compatible with." — [AAAI PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2000/AAAI00-068.pdf)
- Formal perspective: "We introduce a perspective P=<S,O> as a data source S=<OS,DS> viewed in the context of an ontology O. If O=OS then we say that P is the intended perspective, otherwise it is an alternate perspective. It is possible that elements of DS will not be well-formed with respect to O, such elements are considered to be **irrelevant to the perspective**." — [AAAI PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2000/AAAI00-068.pdf)
- The authority problem: "an individual ... decides that they want to create a revision to a popular ontology that is owned by somebody else ... The individual then indicates that this ontology is backward-compatible with the original. Unless there is a mechanism to determine if a revision is official, any agents ... will assume that they can use it in place of the old one." There are three remedies: accept only additive revisions; require the same server path; or have "the original ontology ... authorize the revision ... by a `<REVISED-BY>` tag". "Currently, we recommend ... the second approach." — [AAAI PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2000/AAAI00-068.pdf)

**OWL 2 versioning annotations: SHOE's vocabulary without its semantics** *((4) as metadata only)*
- "If an ontology has an ontology IRI, the ontology MAY additionally have a version IRI". The annotation properties are `owl:priorVersion`, `owl:backwardCompatibleWith` ("a prior version ... that is compatible with the current version"), `owl:incompatibleWith` and `owl:deprecated`. However, "Annotations have no effect on the logical aspects of an ontology". — [W3C OWL 2 Structural Specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/owl2-syntax/)

**Klein & Fensel (2001), then OntoView (Klein, Fensel, Kiryakov & Ognyanov 2002): compatibility as an explicit, human-judged relation between concept versions** *(**(4) as re-affirmation**; (3) as a "derivation tree"; **Cmp: yes**, through diff visualisation)*
- Klein & Fensel, "Ontology versioning on the Semantic Web," SWWS 2001. "Ontology versioning just means that there are multiple variants of an ontology around. In practice, those variants often originates from changes to an existing variant of the ontology and thus form a **derivation tree**." The requirements are, "in increasing level of difficulty": identification ("an unambiguous reference to the intended definition"); change specification ("make the relation of one version of a concept or relation to other versions of that construct explicit"); and transparent evolution ("automatically translate and relate the versions and data sources"). — [SWWS PDF](https://files.ifi.uzh.ch/ddis/iswc_archive/iswc/ih/SWWS-2001/program/full/paper56a.pdf)
- Klein et al., "Ontology Versioning and Change Detection on the Web," EKAW 2002, LNCS 2473, pp. 197–212.
  - It distinguishes "conceptual change: a change in the way a domain is interpreted" from "explication change: a change in the way the conceptualization is specified". "It is **not possible to determine automatically** whether a change is a conceptual change or a explication change ... basically a decision of the ontology engineer."
  - Its rule is that a conceptual change gets a new identifier. An explication change keeps the identifier only if it is non-logical.
  - OntoView "allows the users to specify the **conceptual relation between the different versions of concepts**." It was CVS-based, and it separates version relations (A1.0→A2.0) from domain relations (A→B).
  — [VU PDF](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/29675824/EKAW02.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1007/3-540-45810-7_20](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-45810-7_20)

**Git for RDF: Quit Store, R43ples, TerminusDB** *((3) branch and merge; (4) via commits; Cmp: diffs are structural, not semantic)*
- Arndt, Naumann, Radtke, Martin & Marx, "Decentralized Collaborative Knowledge Management using Git," *J. Web Semantics* 54:29–47, 2019. It supports "tracking, reverting, branching, and merging" of RDF datasets with named graphs and blank nodes, and exposes git history as provenance. — [arXiv 1805.03721](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.03721); [Crossref DOI 10.1016/j.websem.2018.08.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.websem.2018.08.002)
- Its merge strategies are Union merge ("does not allow the implementation of conflict detection"), All Ours / All Theirs, Three-Way merge and **Context Merge** ("A Supervised Approach to Identify Conflicts"). The key observation: "Since the Three-Way-Merge does not produce any merge conflicts, it can happen, that during a merge semantic conflicts are introduced. Even though the result is a valid RDF graph two statements could contradict each other". The example is branch A adding "Obama is president of the USA" while branch B adds "Trump is president of the USA". — [arXiv 1805.03721](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.03721)
- R43ples (Graube, Hensel & Urbas, LDQ@SEMANTiCS 2014): "using named graphs to semantically store the differences between revisions ... allows direct access and manipulation of revisions with SPARQL ... enhanced with some additional keywords". The follow-up paper is "Open Semantic Revision Control with R43ples," SEMANTiCS 2016, pp. 49–56. — [CEUR-WS 1215](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1215/paper-03.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/2993318.2993336](https://doi.org/10.1145/2993318.2993336)

A diff "is a structured, semantic description of which facts were added, removed, or changed." — [TerminusDB docs](https://terminusdb.org/docs/knowledge-graph-version-control/)

- Kuhn & Dumontier, "Trusty URIs: Verifiable, Immutable, and Permanent Digital Artifacts for Linked Data," ESWC 2014, LNCS 8465, pp. 395–410. "we propose a technique to include cryptographic hash values in URIs ... to make not only specific resources but their **entire reference trees** verifiable." This is done by publishing a new nanopublication that states that you retract the original". The retraction assertion uses `npx:retracts`, asserted by the researcher's ORCID. — [nanopub library docs](https://nanopub.readthedocs.io/en/latest/publishing/retraction.html)

The article itself could not be opened (PeerJ 403; PMC captcha). — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-387/) *(partly unverified)*
- Kuhn et al., "Decentralized provenance-aware publishing with nanopublications," PeerJ CS 2016: only the metadata was confirmed (the preprint DOI 10.7287/peerj.preprints.1760v1). — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.7287/peerj.preprints.1760v1)

**W3C PROV-O: the derivation vocabulary** *((3) and (4) vocabulary; `alternateOf` is a weak, structural "same thing")*
- "A derivation is a transformation of an entity into another, an update of an entity resulting in a new one, or the construction of a new entity based on a pre-existing entity". `prov:wasRevisionOf` is a subproperty of `prov:wasDerivedFrom`. For `prov:alternateOf`: "Two alternate entities present aspects of the same thing. These aspects may be the same or different, and the alternate entities may or may not overlap in time". `wasQuotedFrom` covers a new entity "created by repeating some or all of the original". **Memento and Robust Links: citing a specific state** *((4) for outbound links)*
- RFC 7089 (Van de Sompel, Nelson & Sanderson, Dec. 2013) defines the Original Resource (URI-R), the Memento (URI-M, "encapsulates a prior state"), the TimeGate ("capable of datetime negotiation") and the TimeMap. The `Accept-Datetime` request header is answered with `Memento-Datetime`. — [RFC 7089](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7089.html)
- A Robust Link decorates an anchor with `data-originalurl`, `data-versiondate` ("the date of the state of the resource that the linker wants the link visitor to be able to experience") and `data-versionurl` (a snapshot). The target problem is link rot *and* content drift. — [Robust Links spec](https://mementoweb.org/robustlinks/spec/); [Code4Lib Journal article](https://journal.code4lib.org/articles/15509)
- Motivating evidence: "Scholarly Context Not Found: One in Five Articles Suffers from Reference Rot" (Klein et al., PLoS ONE 2014; title as retrieved). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277367/)

### Inferences
- The user's feature (4), recording `ydoc_update_id` at membership and later re-affirming, is **SHOE's `USE-ONTOLOGY VERSION` plus `BACKWARD-COMPATIBLE-WITH`, moved from the ontology level down to the entity level**. OntoView adds the refinement that re-affirmation is a *human* judgement ("not possible to determine automatically") that should be recorded *per unit* as a relation between versions, not as a whole-container flag.
- SHOE's hijack scenario predicts a MultiBlog risk: if *anyone* can re-affirm that revision R′ "still fits" embedding E, re-affirmation becomes a vector for quietly reassigning an embedding. The three SHOE remedies translate directly:
  1. Auto-accept only purely additive changes.
  2. Only E's owners may re-affirm, which is the "same server" rule.
  3. E explicitly authorises the revision, which is `REVISED-BY`.
- Quit Store's Obama/Trump example is the RDF version of "forking is easy, comparing is hard". Set-based three-way merges never conflict syntactically, so *all* real conflicts are semantic and invisible to the merge. Its "Context Merge", which flags statements sharing a node that both branches touched, is a cheap heuristic worth copying at the passage level: flag passages that both embeddings edited since their common ancestor.
- Nanopublications show the immutable-assertion discipline at scale: never edit, only supersede or retract, each hash-identified. Combined with the SSSOM-style mapping row from Q1, this gives an append-only log of "X asserted that passage p@r1 in E1 corresponds to passage q@r2 in E2", which is the shape the user's quote-anchor rows already have.
- PROV's `alternateOf` ("aspects of the same thing") is a standards-grade, deliberately weak relation for "same-titled articles in two embeddings that may or may not concern the same thing". It is weaker than SKOS closeMatch and carries no inference.

### Gaps
- No evaluation was found of how often OWL's `backwardCompatibleWith` / `incompatibleWith` are actually used in published ontologies (e.g., BioPortal/OBO statistics).
- No usage or evaluation data was found for Quit Store or R43ples beyond the authors' own performance measurements. The Quit paper claims "overall good performance", but the numbers were not extracted.
- The nanopublication supersede semantics and the network's scale (count of published nanopublications) could not be read from the primary source.
- Heflin & Hendler's 2000 paper does not report whether the REVISED-BY tag was ever implemented.

## Q3. Software variation as the precise analogue of "forking easy, comparing hard": what each mechanism would mean for prose

### Takeaway
Software-engineering research has studied the user's exact failure mode empirically and at scale. Forking is favoured because it is quick and independent. The costs are that features in forks get lost, redundant work is done unknowingly, fixes do not propagate, and "large amounts of information [sit] in an unorganized space". Three kinds of remedy exist:
1. **After-the-fact comparison.** INFOX clusters a fork's scattered changes into labelled features, which is the "pull together" step, automated.
2. **Graduated governance between clone-and-own and an integrated platform.** The virtual platform's L0–L6 levels, and Rubin & Chechik's operators.
3. **Single-artifact representations of variation.** The choice calculus (named dimensions), projectional editing (edit one variant, and the change updates only the visible variants), and delta-oriented programming (named change sets with application conditions).

Research on "variation control systems" now treats revisions (time) and variants (space) as one model, which is what embeddings with pinned revisions need.

### Cited Findings

**Clone-and-own in industry (Dubinsky et al., CSMR 2013)** *(evidence for "forking easy"; Cmp: identifies the problem)*
- Dubinsky, Rubin, Berger, Duszynski, Becker & Czarnecki, CSMR 2013, pp. 25–34. This study of "six industrial software product lines realized via code cloning" found that "cloning, while widely discouraged in literature, is still perceived as a favorable and natural reuse approach by the majority of practitioners ... mainly due to its benefits such as simplicity, availability and independence of developers." — [author PDF](https://gsd.uwaterloo.ca/sites/default/files/2013-csmr-cloning.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1109/CSMR.2013.13](https://doi.org/10.1109/csmr.2013.13)
- Reported costs: "propagating modifications between clones is not a trivial task". On governance, the paper's heading is "(Lack of) Governance", with the finding that "Knowledge about reuse is rarely maintained." — [same PDF](https://gsd.uwaterloo.ca/sites/default/files/2013-csmr-cloning.pdf)

**Forks plus #ifdef in open source (Stănciulescu, Schulze & Wąsowski, ICSME 2015: Marlin 3D-printer firmware)** *(evidence; hybrid of forks and in-artifact variation; Cmp: yes, descriptively)*
- ICSME 2015, pp. 151–160. "The Marlin project has been forked 1588 times in the period of 3 years". "Github forks of Marlin are used more to create disconnected software variants, than to collaborate ... Most forks diverge from the mainline ... more than half of the commits originate in forks. Still, forking ... leads to distribution of large amounts of information in an unorganized space, important bug-fixes are not propagated, and occasionally the same functionality is developed more than once." — [author PDF](https://stefanstanciulescu.com/papers/2015-icsme-stanciulescu.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1109/ICSM.2015.7332461](https://doi.org/10.1109/icsm.2015.7332461)
- 261 forks (37% of active forks) added preprocessor directives, but "Only 83 out of the 261 ... actually created pull requests (32%)". Only 15% of Marlin's forks create pull requests at all. The maintainers require #ifdef guards on new features ("New features that don't do this will be deferred until they do").
- A fork is integrated only when "the quality of the feature is within standards" and "maintainers accept to take over the maintenance". Integration "gave both visibility ... and lower maintenance efforts". Example: Auto Bed Leveling "has been created in a fork, later updated in another fork and finally integrated". — [same PDF](https://stefanstanciulescu.com/papers/2015-icsme-stanciulescu.pdf)

**Rubin & Chechik: operators for managing clones, and the virtual platform (Antkiewicz et al.)** *((3) governance; Cmp: yes, as named operations)*
- Attribution correction: the "virtual platform" is Antkiewicz, Ji, Berger, Czarnecki, Schmorleiz, Lämmel, Stănciulescu, Wąsowski & Schaefer, ICSE 2014 NIER/Companion, pp. 532–535. Rubin and Chechik are **not** among its authors. Their contribution is the operator framework: Rubin & Chechik, "A Framework for Managing Cloned Product Variants," ICSE 2013 NIER, pp. 1233–1236, and Rubin, Czarnecki & Chechik, "Managing cloned variants: a framework and experience," SPLC 2013, pp. 101–110. — [Crossref DOI 10.1109/ICSE.2013.6606686](https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2013.6606686); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/2491627.2491644](https://doi.org/10.1145/2491627.2491644)
- Rubin & Chechik's operators include feature location (returning "the traced set of artifacts that realize the input" feature), `require?` ("whether feature f1 requires feature f2"), `same?` (⟨f1, product1⟩ × ⟨f2, product2⟩ → witnesses), `interact?`, and `compose` (systems × matches × resolution → system). They are demonstrated on "sharing of features between cloned variants and re-engineering the variants into 'single-copy' representations". — [author PDF](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/mjulia/publications/A_Framework_for_Managing_2013.pdf)
- Virtual platform: "an incremental and minimally invasive PLE adoption strategy ... divided into six governance levels". — [Chalmers PDF](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/~bergert/paper/2014-icsenier-virtualplatform.pdf)
  - **L0 Ad-hoc clone-and-own.** Teams copy freely.
  - **L1 Clone-and-own with provenance.** "Teams record provenance information about the original projects and per cloned asset ... for impact analysis and change propagation."
  - **L2 With features.** "Teams declare features and map them to asset fragments that implement them."
  - **L3.** Not extracted.
  - **L4 With a feature model.** New projects start from an existing one and propagate "the needed features from all other projects as allowed by feature model constraints".
  - **L5 Integrated platform plus clone-and-own.** "Only changes worth of propagation and sharing are harvested into the platform."
  - **L6 Fully integrated platform.**
- A tool implementation followed: "Seamless Variability Management With the Virtual Platform" (arXiv 2103.00437; details not read). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.00437)

**INFOX: clustering a fork's scattered changes into labelled features (Zhou et al., ICSE 2018)** *(**Cmp: yes, the closest analogue to "divergences pull together"**)*
- Zhou, Stănciulescu, Leßenich, Xiong, Wąsowski & Kästner, ICSE 2018, pp. 105–116. "When the number of forks becomes large, it is difficult for developers to get or maintain an overview of activities in the forks. Current tools provide little help." — [author PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse18forks.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/3180155.3180205](https://doi.org/10.1145/3180155.3180205)
- Mechanism: take "the diff between the latest commit of the upstream ... and the latest commit of the fork". (1) Build "a dependency graph among all added or changed lines". (2) "Cluster the lines ... using a community-detection technique ... such that lines with many connections in the dependency graph are mapped to the same feature". (3) "Label each cluster by extracting representative keywords with an information-retrieval technique". Commits are not used as the unit, because "commits tangle code of multiple features and even more common[ly] a single feature is scattered across multiple commits." — [same PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse18forks.pdf)
- Evaluation: "effective at identifying features among changes in 10 open-source projects with a median accuracy of 90 percent", and a human-subject study showing "actionable insight". Practitioners' baseline was manual: "I do compare one branch to another". — [same PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse18forks.pdf)

**"What the fork" (Zhou, Vasilescu & Kästner, ESEC/FSE 2019) and the hard-forks study (ICSE 2020)** *(evidence; Cmp: identifies what makes comparison matter)*
- ESEC/FSE 2019, pp. 350–361, studying 1,131 GitHub projects with regression modelling and interviews. It names four inefficiencies: "(1) lost contributions—changes published in forks, but not integrated back ... are hard to find and de-facto lost ... (3) redundant development—developers may independently implement similar functionality in forks without coordination; and (4) fragmented communities". — [author PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/fse19_forks.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/3338906.3338918](https://doi.org/10.1145/3338906.3338918)
- Result: "better modularity of the project structure and more centralized management practices for contributions are strong predictors of more contributions and more merged pull requests ... [but] centralized management also associates with higher risk of community fragmentation through hard forks, as does a low pull request acceptance rate." — [same PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/fse19_forks.pdf)
- Zhou, Vasilescu & Kästner, "How has forking changed in the last 20 years?" ICSE 2020, pp. 445–456, studying "15,306 hard forks on GitHub" with 18 interviews. It found that "hard forks often evolve out of social forks rather than being planned deliberately", and that perceptions shifted toward seeing them "as a positive noncompetitive alternative". — [author PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/3377811.3380412](https://doi.org/10.1145/3377811.3380412)

**The choice calculus (Erwig & Walkingshaw, TOSEM 2011): named dimensions of variation inside one artifact** *(**(1) at passage grain**; Cmp: yes, by construction, since shared parts are stored once)*
- *ACM TOSEM* 21(1), Article 6, 2011. "branches are a highly redundant representation—code and data common to multiple branches are usually duplicated across each branch ... this often forces users to manually copy changes in one branch to other, related branches ... An ideal variation representation should thus seek to minimize redundancy." — [author PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~erwig/papers/ChoiceCalculus_TOSEM11.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/2063239.2063245](https://doi.org/10.1145/2063239.2063245)
- The core construct is the **dimension approach**. "A choice is a set of alternative expressions ... (1) A choice is represented as a mapping from tags to alternative expressions ... direct tagging ... (2) The introduction of tags and their association with expressions are separated. Since the separate introduction of the tags amounts to the definition of a **dimension of variation**, we call this the dimension approach." Choices in the same named dimension are selected in sync. The paper gives "semantics-preserving transformations", normal forms and "quality criteria". "Expression" "includes all kinds of tree-structured documents", which covers prose trees. — [same PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~erwig/papers/ChoiceCalculus_TOSEM11.pdf)

**Projectional editing of variational software (Walkingshaw & Ostermann, GPCE 2014)** *((1) plus editing through a view; Cmp: partial)*
- GPCE 2014, pp. 29–38. "a programmer can partially configure a variational program, edit this simplified view of the code, and then automatically update the original, fully variational source code. The model is based on an **isolation principle where edits affect only the variants that are visible in the view**." It is framed as a lens / view-update problem ("get" and "put"), formalised in the choice calculus. — [author PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~walkiner/papers/gpce14-projectional-editing.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/2658761.2658766](https://doi.org/10.1145/2658761.2658766)
- Follow-up: Stănciulescu, Berger, Walkingshaw & Wąsowski, "Concepts, Operations, and Feasibility of a Projection-Based Variation Control System," ICSME 2016, pp. 323–333 (metadata verified; content not read). — [Crossref DOI 10.1109/ICSME.2016.88](https://doi.org/10.1109/icsme.2016.88)

**Delta-oriented programming (Schaefer, Bettini, Bono, Damiani & Tanzarella, SPLC 2010)** *((3) as deltas over a core; (1); Cmp: partial)*
- SPLC 2010, LNCS 6287, pp. 77–91 (metadata verified). This is a secondary description, since the abstract was not retrieved directly. "a product line is represented by a **core module and a set of delta modules**. The core module provides an implementation of a valid product ... Delta modules specify changes to be applied to the core module ... by adding, modifying and removing code. **Application conditions** attached to delta modules allow handling combinations of features explicitly ... Delta modules generalize feature modules by allowing removal of functionality." — [Semantic Scholar/search summary of the paper](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Delta-Oriented-Programming-of-Software-Product-Schaefer-Bettini/205b2d86bf8d1b35d839beb395dd8c5c2bb5eddc); [Crossref DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-15579-6_6](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-15579-6_6)

**Unifying revisions (time) and variants (space)** *((3) and (4) together; Cmp: conceptual)*
- Linsbauer, Berger & Grünbacher, "A Classification of Variation Control Systems," GPCE 2017, pp. 49–62. Version control handles "revisions (sequential versions) and variants (concurrent versions)", where variants use "branching or forking mechanisms that conceptually clone the whole system". — [Chalmers PDF](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/~bergert/paper/2017-gpce-varcs.pdf); [Crossref DOI 10.1145/3136040.3136054](https://doi.org/10.1145/3136040.3136054)

### Inferences (what each would mean if the artifact were prose)
- **Clone-and-own / hard forks become "fork the article into my embedding".** The measured costs map directly:
  - "Lost contributions" become good edits in one embedding that the parent never sees.
  - "Redundant development" becomes two embeddings independently rewriting the same paragraph.
  - Marlin's "large amounts of information in an unorganized space" is the user's "divergences start scattered".
- **The virtual platform's L1 ("clone-and-own with provenance") is exactly feature (4)** plus the fork edge (3): record, per copied asset, where it came from, so that change propagation is possible. L2 ("declare features and map them to asset fragments") is the step where scattered divergences get *named*, the "school of thought" emerging. L5's "only changes worth propagation and sharing are harvested into the platform" is a policy for when an embedding's divergence gets folded back.
- **INFOX, transposed to prose**, would take all passage-level diffs between embedding E and its parent (possibly across several articles). It would build a graph with edges for co-edited-in-the-same-session, shared quote anchors, shared links, and textual similarity. Community detection would then yield clusters, labelled by keywords. Each cluster is a candidate "divergence theme", which is the automated version of divergences "pulling together". INFOX's evaluation (median 90% on known features) suggests the structural signal is strong in code. Prose dependency edges are weaker, so this would be an open research bet.
- **The choice calculus, transposed**, would make an embedding a *named dimension*. A passage in which embedding E_A and embedding E_B differ becomes a choice `Institution⟨A: "…prestigious organisation…", B: "…interlocking repertoires…"⟩`. Every other choice tagged with the same dimension is selected in sync. This is precisely feature (1) at passage grain, with shared text stored once. That in turn is the calculus's "minimize redundancy" argument against branches.
- **Projectional editing** would let a user edit "the article as embedding E sees it". The isolation principle guarantees that edits touch only E's variant (and variants visible in the view), never E′'s. That is the correct safety property for a multi-embedding editor.
- **Delta-oriented programming** is the representation in which an embedding is "a core document plus named deltas with application conditions". The application condition could be "applies when embedding ∈ {E, descendants of E}". DOP's allowance for *removing* content matters for prose, because a school of thought may delete a claim, not just add one.
- The **space/time unification** research is the precedent for the combination the user wants: embeddings are variability in space, `ydoc_update_id` is variability in time, and "re-affirm revision R′ as still fitting E" is a *feature revision* in Ananieva et al.'s terms.

### Gaps
- The Walkingshaw & Ostermann paper's formal laws (e.g., which lens laws hold) were not extracted beyond the abstract and the isolation principle.
- No study was found that applies INFOX-style clustering or the choice calculus to natural-language documents. The prose interpretations above are inferences.
- Industrial evaluations of the virtual platform's levels beyond the 2014 NIER paper (e.g., the 2021 tool paper's evaluation) were not read.
- The DOP abstract is quoted via a secondary summary (Springer blocked the chapter page).

## Q4. Textual scholarship: the mature comparison layer for variants of one work

### Takeaway
Textual scholarship has long had a working "comparison layer". Witnesses (the analogue of embeddings) are **named containers**. Each reading (a passage-level variant) is **assigned to a set of witnesses** through TEI's `@wit` or the version-set labels of an MVD edge, which is feature (1) at passage grain. A **variant graph** stores shared text once and branches only where witnesses diverge. The Gothenburg model decomposes comparison into tokenise, normalise, align, analyse and visualise. Tools like Juxta and CollateX render heat maps and side-by-side views. Known hard cases are transpositions and within-witness revision layers. Stemmatology groups witnesses by *co-occurring* variants, which is a formal version of divergences "pulling together".

### Cited Findings

**TEI P5 critical apparatus** *((1) readings assigned to witness sets; relationship-grouping via `rdgGrp`; **Cmp: yes**)*
- Elements: `app` ("one entry in a critical apparatus"), `lem` ("the lemma, or base text"), `rdg` ("a single reading"), `rdgGrp` ("groups two or more readings perceived to have a genetic relationship or other affinity"), `@wit` ("a space-delimited list of one or more pointers indicating the witnesses which attest to a given reading"), `listWit`/`witness` and `witDetail` (per-witness details, e.g., which hand). — [TEI Guidelines ch. 13 (P5 v4.12.0, July 2026)](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/TC.html)
- There are three ways to link the apparatus to the text:
  - **Location-referenced**: "indicating explicitly only the block of text on which there is a variant". It "is not possible to find automatically the precise portion of text varied".
  - **Double end-point attachment**: `from`/`to` anchors; "designed to cope with 'overlapping lemmata'".
  - **Parallel segmentation**: "all variants at any point of the text are expressed as variants on one another. In this method, **no two variations can overlap, although they may nest** ... This permits direct comparison of any span of text in any witness with that in any other witness."
  — [TEI TC](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/TC.html)
- **Base text is optional.** "For the use of the app element without a base text, see 13.2.3 The Parallel Segmentation Method", and "Those who prefer to work without the notion of a base text ... may prefer not to use [lem] at all." Parallel segmentation "may be translated into the double end-point attachment method and back without loss of information." (A WebFetch summary had claimed that parallel segmentation privileges a base text. The source text above contradicts it, so that summary was discarded.) — [TEI TC](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/TC.html)
- **Transpositions** "are harder to encode, because they involve variation that occurs in different locations. A single app will therefore not be sufficient, and the variants must be linked". The example is Housman's relocation of Propertius 1.16 lines 25–6, recorded in Heyworth's edition, "which records Housman's conjecture despite disagreeing with it". — [TEI TC](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/TC.html)

**Multi-Version Documents and the variant graph (Schmidt & Colomb 2009)** *(**(1) directly**: each fragment carries its set of versions; Cmp: yes)*
- Schmidt & Colomb, "A data structure for representing multi-version texts online," *Int. J. Human-Computer Studies* 67(6):497–514, 2009. — [Crossref DOI 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2009.02.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2009.02.001)
- "A multi-version document is represented as a list of text fragments ti, each of which is assigned a set of versions vi: {v1, t1}, {v2, t2}, ... {vn, tn}". This is equivalent to a graph whose paths "start at one point, branch, rejoin and split again, until they all join back together at the end". Schmidt contrasts this with markup, "which can only efficiently represent hierarchical structures." — [Schmidt, Multi-Version Documents blog, 2008](http://multiversiondocs.blogspot.com/2008/03/whats-multi-version-document.html)
- The variant-graph idea was adapted from bioinformatics partial-order alignment, and has been adopted by "NMerge, CollateX, and Stemmaweb" (secondary description). — [search summary citing the Helsinki stemmatology wiki](https://wiki.helsinki.fi/xwiki/bin/view/stemmatology/Variant%20graph/)
- MVD handles within-document revision through "levels"/"layers". "All changes to the 'baseline text' of a document (level 1) are grouped as being on level 2. Subsequent changes to level 2 are grouped as level 3." This is used in Ecdosis. — [Bleeker et al., DHQ 16(1), 2022](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000583.pdf)

**The Gothenburg model and CollateX** *(**Cmp: yes, as a pipeline**)*
- The five tasks, per the CollateX documentation:
  - **Tokenization**: texts are "split up into parts – henceforth called tokens".
  - **Normalization/regularization**: equivalence of tokens, e.g., case or punctuation.
  - **Alignment**: finding matches and inserting gaps.
  - **Analysis/feedback**: manual correction, then re-running collation.
  - **Visualization**: deemed out of scope for CollateX itself.
  — [CollateX documentation](https://collatex.net/doc/)
- The variant graph is a DAG in which edges carry only witness sigla. "Common segments of multiple text versions" merge, while "differing segments result in the graph branching". — [CollateX documentation](https://collatex.net/doc/)
- Known limits:
  - Transpositions are held as undirected links that do "not integrate well" with the graph and remain "a problematic case".
  - The progressive-alignment algorithms show "occasional dependence of the result on the order" in which witnesses are merged.
  - Tabular outputs don't show transpositions.
  — [CollateX documentation](https://collatex.net/doc/)
- Haentjens Dekker, van Hulle, Middell, Neyt & van Zundert, "Computer-supported collation of modern manuscripts: CollateX and the Beckett Digital Manuscript Project," *DSH* 30(3):452–470, 2015. — [Crossref DOI 10.1093/llc/fqu007](https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqu007)

**HyperCollate: collating texts that contain their own revisions** *((1) and (4) within one witness; Cmp: yes)*
- Bleeker, Buitendijk, Haentjens Dekker, Neyt & Van Hulle, "Layers of Variation: a Computational Approach to Collating Texts with Revisions," *DHQ* 16(1), 2022. "in-text variation can best be modelled as **nonlinear text**, and ... a collation tool needs to treat in-text variation differently from the way linear text is treated". Its data model is "a hypergraph for textual variation" rather than an XML tree or a plain variant graph. — [DHQ PDF](https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/000583.pdf)

**Juxta** *(Cmp: yes, as UI patterns)*
- Juxta Commons (NINES, University of Virginia) offers three views. A **heat map** overlays a base text with differences highlighted, with shade by amount of variance across witnesses. A **side-by-side** view shows two witnesses in synchronously scrolling columns with lines connecting the sites of difference. A **histogram** gives a global view. These are secondary descriptions. — [Juxta Commons, Journal of Digital Humanities 3(1)](https://journalofdigitalhumanities.org/3-1/juxta-commons/); [Juxta site](https://www.juxtasoftware.org/about/)

**Stemmaweb: from variant graph to genealogy** *(**Cmp: yes, and the "pull together" step**)*
- Andrews & Macé, "Beyond the tree of texts: Building an empirical model of scribal variation through graph analysis of texts and stemmata," *Literary and Linguistic Computing* 28(4):504–521, 2013. — [Crossref DOI 10.1093/llc/fqt032](https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqt032)
- Stemmaweb "extends the CollateX graph to enable user-driven annotation and modification of the graph structure (e.g. the merging and splitting of vertices)". Variant graphs "may be annotated with links between readings that describe their relationship to each other; in order to preserve the directed and acyclic properties of the rest of the graph, these annotation links must be stored and analysed separately." The tool also builds stemmata (genealogical trees of witnesses). This is a secondary description drawn from search results. — [OUP abstract page](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article-abstract/28/4/504/1076624); [Semantic Scholar: "Stemmaweb – a collection of tools for analysis of collated texts"](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Stemmaweb-a-collection-of-tools-for-analysis-of-Andrews/a43c7d30006e77475f5584407b1873d09dcda933)

### Inferences
- **Witness : reading : @wit :: embedding : passage variant : membership.** TEI's `<rdg wit="#A #C">` is already "this passage variant belongs to embeddings A and C", which is feature (1) at passage grain with multi-membership. `rdgGrp` ("genetic relationship or other affinity") is a grouping of *variants* that is independent of witnesses. It is the natural slot for "these divergences belong to one emerging school".
- **An MVD is the prose instance of the choice calculus**: a fragment labelled with a version set is a choice whose alternatives are selected by witness. The two literatures (Erwig & Walkingshaw in SE; Schmidt & Colomb in DH) arrived at the same structure independently. Neither cites the other in the material read here; this is an observation, not a verified claim of non-citation.
- **Parallel segmentation's "no overlap, may nest" rule** is the same constraint that ProseMirror marks and Yjs-anchored ranges impose in practice. **Double end-point attachment** is the anchor-row approach (from/to pointers into a base), and it *does* tolerate overlap. The TEI choice between them is a documented trade-off that MultiBlog already faces between marks and anchor rows (docs/COLLAB.md).
- The **Gothenburg separation of normalisation from alignment** matters for "names are not synonyms". *What counts as the same token* is an explicit, editable stage, not buried in the diff. For embeddings, "which passages correspond" (alignment) should be kept separate from "which differences are trivial" (normalisation).
- **Stemmatology is the formal precedent for "divergences pull together"**. Witness families are inferred from variants that *co-occur* across many passages, and scattered individual differences become evidence for groupings only in aggregate. Stemmaweb's separate store of reading-relationship annotations, kept outside the DAG, mirrors the Q1 recommendation: keep correspondences, graded and attributed, in a separate layer from the texts.
- Two things the scholarly tools do *not* do are relevant here. They assume one work with many witnesses, where the alignment coordinate system is the text itself. They do not align *different articles* across containers. That is exactly the gap the user's ontology and translation idea must fill.

### Gaps
- The Schmidt & Colomb IJHCS article itself was not opened (paywalled). The MVD description comes from Schmidt's own 2008 blog post and the DHQ 2022 article.
- No usage or evaluation data (users, editions produced) was found for Juxta Commons or Stemmaweb. The Juxta description is secondary.
- The original Gothenburg model publication (the 2009 Interedition/COST workshop) was not located. The model's steps are quoted from the CollateX documentation.

## Q5. Systems that combine several of these (containers + versioned membership + attributed mappings + comparison view)

### Takeaway
No system found combines all four features with a comparison view. Several combine two or three:
- **SSSOM + Mapping Commons**: attributed, versioned, negatable mappings between versioned sources. There is no comparison UI in the standard.
- **OntoView**: versions, human-judged relations between concept versions, and a diff view.
- **Quit Store and TerminusDB**: branch/merge containers with provenance and structural diffs, but no mappings.
- **Lenticular Lens**: attributed links, context-specific selections, and set algebra to compare lenses.
- **Ontohub/DOL**: networks of ontologies with typed links, in a git-backed repository.
- **ORKG**: versioned, DOI-published comparison tables.
- **Stemmaweb/CollateX**: containers (witnesses), passage-level membership, a comparison view and grouping, but for one work only.

### Cited Findings
- **SSSOM / Mapping Commons.** Mapping sets are versioned (`mapping_set_version`, mapped to `owl:versionInfo`), and each mapping pins both sources' versions and carries author, reviewer, justification, confidence, `Not` and `NoTermFound`. The FAQ's stated ideal is that mappings should be "reviewable and editable in much the same way as open ontologies, offering issue trackers and an active community", maintained "as part of ontology release pipelines or by primary mapping creators". — [SSSOM FAQ](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/faq/); [mapping_set_version](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/mapping_set_version/)
- **OntoView** "maintain[s] not only the transformations between ontologies, but also the conceptual relation between concepts in different versions. The system allows ontology engineers to compare versions of ontology and to specify these conceptual relations. For the visualization of differences, it uses an adaptable rule-based mechanism that finds and classifies changes in RDF-based ontologies." — [Klein et al., EKAW 2002 (VU PDF)](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/29675824/EKAW02.pdf)
- **Quit Store**: git branches as containers, merge strategies, and provenance exposed from commits. — [arXiv 1805.03721](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.03721). **TerminusDB**: branch, merge, blame and time-travel over triples, with semantic (triple-level) diffs. — [TerminusDB docs](https://terminusdb.org/docs/knowledge-graph-version-control/)
- **Lenticular Lens**: attributed linksets, a user-constructed context-specific lens, and union/intersection/difference/composition over lenses. — [Idrissou et al. 2017](https://www.cs.vu.nl/~frankh/postscript/KCAP2017.pdf); [lenticularlens.org](https://lenticularlens.org/)
- **Ontohub/DOL**: heterogeneous ontologies with interpretations, equivalences and alignments. — [arXiv 1208.0293](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.0293); [GitHub](https://github.com/ontohub/ontohub)
- **ORKG (Open Research Knowledge Graph)**: "Comparison tables can be published with DOI, exported in various formats ... Contributions and smart reviews are versioned so that all changes can be discussed ... updated and new versions published." This is a secondary summary of Stocker et al., "FAIR scientific information with the Open Research Knowledge Graph," *FAIR Connect* 2023. — [SAGE DOI 10.3233/FC-221513](https://dx.doi.org/10.3233/FC-221513)
- **Variation control systems.** Ananieva et al.'s unified model is validated against existing tools from both product-line engineering and configuration management, and Linsbauer et al. classify systems that manage revisions and variants together. These are the SE literature's closest "combined" systems. — [Ananieva et al. 2022](https://pure.tue.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/218415271/s10664_021_10097_z.pdf); [Linsbauer et al. 2017](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/~bergert/paper/2017-gpce-varcs.pdf)

### Inferences
- A MultiBlog "embedding" design could be assembled almost entirely from proven parts:
  - containers with fork edges and per-member pinned revisions (virtual platform L1; SHOE `USE-ONTOLOGY VERSION`; PROV `wasDerivedFrom`);
  - passage-level membership with multi-assignment (TEI `@wit`; MVD version sets; choice-calculus dimensions);
  - an append-only, attributed, versioned, negatable correspondence table between embeddings (SSSOM row shape, with immutability à la nanopublications);
  - context-specific selections of correspondences that can be combined and differenced (Lenticular Lens);
  - a comparison layer (Gothenburg pipeline, heat map and side-by-side views, and INFOX-style clustering of scattered diffs into themes).
  
  The novel part is applying the correspondence layer *between different articles in different containers*, not between witnesses of one work or between ontology terms.
- The strongest single warning across all four literatures is that **structural merge and identity are cheap, and semantic judgement is expensive and contested**:
  - Quit's conflict-free three-way merge still lets contradictions through.
  - Halpin's judges agreed at κ = 0.158.
  - OntoView says conceptual change cannot be detected automatically.
  - TEI and CollateX call transpositions "a problematic case".
  
  So the system should *record* judgements (who, against which revisions, how strongly, including "not the same" and "no counterpart") rather than try to compute them.

### Gaps
- No system was found that pins *passage-level* membership to a CRDT or update-log revision and supports re-affirmation. This combination appears to be unbuilt.
- BioPortal's mapping store (attributed mappings between versioned ontology submissions) was not researched in this session and may be a further combined precedent.
- ORKG's versioning is described from a secondary summary; its data model for comparison versions was not read.
