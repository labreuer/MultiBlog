# Digital-humanities and heritage precedents for "embeddings": rival tellings, attributed assertions, community-scoped knowledge structures

Scope note: This file does not repeat what the prior MultiWiki survey already covers: Cyc microtheories, named graphs, Wikidata ranks, and van Erp & de Boer's polyvocal Semantic Web (all in [`labeled-viewpoints/notes/academic-research.md`](../../labeled-viewpoints/notes/academic-research.md) Q2), plus BibleHub, Sefaria, Perseus, the Talmud page, the Hexapla, the Griesbach synopsis, Diderot and comparison charts. Feature numbers refer to the brief:
(1) whole entities or parts of entities are assigned to one or more embeddings;
(2) relationships between entities are also assigned to an embedding;
(3) embeddings fork from each other;
(4) the revision is recorded at the time of assignment, and later revisions are re-affirmed.
Where only an abstract, a preprint or a secondary source was read, the citation says so.

## Q1. PeriodO: period definitions scoped to the authority that asserted them, versioning, and rival definitions of "the same" period

### Takeaway
PeriodO is the closest built precedent for features (1) and (4). A "period" is never a free-standing fact. It is a *definition quoted from a citable source*, grouped with the other definitions from that source. Rival definitions of "Early Bronze Age" sit side by side unreconciled, and the project explicitly refuses to decide which one is correct. Every change is a reviewed patch with PROV-O links that separate a stable identifier from its versions.

### Cited Findings
- **Citations verified.**
  - Golden, P. & Shaw, R. (2016), "Nanopublication beyond the sciences: the PeriodO period gazetteer," *PeerJ Computer Science* 2:e44, doi:10.7717/peerj-cs.44. Published 2016-02-03, CC-BY. — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.7717/peerj-cs.44). The full text was read from an [archived copy of the PeerJ page](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/), because peerj.com returned HTTP 403.
  - Rabinowitz, A., Shaw, R., Buchanan, S., Golden, P. & Kansa, E. (2016), "Making Sense of the Ways We Make Sense of the Past: The PeriodO Project," *Bulletin of the Institute of Classical Studies* 59(2):42–55. The abstract describes "a Linked Data gazetteer of period definitions that transparently record the spatial and temporal boundaries assigned to a given period by an authoritative source". — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/bics/article-abstract/59/2/42/5588190). I read only the abstract.
- **What a period is.** "PeriodO defines a 'period definition' as a scholarly assertion about the name and spatiotemporal extent of a period. The core of a period definition consists of text quoted from the original source… Multiple period definitions from the same source are grouped into a period collection." The worked example is Belarte (2008), whose single sentence yields two definitions: "Classical Iberian Period (400–200 BC)" and "Early Iberian Period (525–400 BC)". — [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/)
- **Grouping by source does not assert coherence.** "Belonging to the same period collection does not imply that period definitions compose a periodization. A periodization is a single coherent, continuous division of historical time… A period collection, on the other hand, is simply a set of period definitions that share the same source." When the definitions do form a periodization, "this can be indicated through the addition of statements relating the period definitions to one another." — [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/). The current technical overview repeats the rule: "An authority is simply a set of periods that share a source… Belonging to the same authority does not imply any semantic relationship between periods, other than sharing a source." — [PeriodO technical overview](https://perio.do/technical-overview/)
- **No canonical period, by design.** Golden & Shaw: "we are not concerned with making an exhaustive taxonomy of 'correct' periods or facilitating the 'discovery' of new periods (a non sequitur—there are no periods that exist in the world that are awaiting discovery…). Instead we are interested in enabling the study and citation of how and by whom time has been segmented into different periods. It is not necessary that these segmentations be falsifiable…; they only need to be comparable." — [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/)
- **Periods are discursive, and rivals are settled only by argument.** "The 'Late Bronze Age' is a purely discursive construct… one cannot disprove the idea that there was a time period called the 'Late Bronze Age' from around 1100 B.C. to 500 B.C.; one can only argue that another definition has more credence based on non-experimental, discursive arguments." The authors reject the proposal that nanopublications be falsifiable. — [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/)
- **How rivals are compared.** In the 2016 paper's Fig. 3, "Searching for 'early bronze' results in sixty period definitions with matching labels, from a variety of sources. The time range facet updates to show the distribution of temporal extents defined by these various sources." — [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/). The project describes its purpose as helping "scholars and students see where period definitions overlap or diverge." — [perio.do](https://perio.do/en/)
- **The source's words are authoritative; the curators' normalization is secondary.** `skos:prefLabel` is "the name of the period exactly as given in the original source". Curators add `skos:altLabel`, OWL-Time year ranges and gazetteer links. "The skos:prefLabel of an interval should be considered the authoritative description." `skos:note` holds text from the source, while `skos:editorialNote` holds curator notes that "do not appear in the original source." — [PeriodO technical overview](https://perio.do/technical-overview/)
- **Nanopublication structure.** A nanopublication has three parts: "An assertion…, The provenance of that assertion (who made that assertion, where, when, etc.), The provenance of the nanopublication itself (who formed or extracted the assertion, when, and by what method)." — [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/)
- **Change workflow and versioning.**
  - "contributors can submit proposed changes in the form of patches, and the PeriodO editors can accept or reject them. All proposed and accepted changes are stored, and each period definition has a history of changes in the form of patch submissions and approvals."
  - Each patch is a `prov:Activity` over a versioned dataset (`…/p0d?version=1`). It points to a JSON-Patch file and generates entities, each of which has "a prov:specializationOf statement that refers to the permanent identifier… (with no particular version specified)". Revisions carry "prov:wasRevisionOf statements that refer to the version that they were descended from."
  - A public changelog lists the activities in order, so "one can reconstruct the origin of each change to the dataset as a whole, or to individual period assertions."

  — [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/)
- **Uncertainty notation was abandoned in practice.** The project tried encoding uncertainty as Julian-day scientific notation, but "in practice, we found this scheme to be overly complex… In almost every single case that we observed, authors did not explicitly state a precise level of uncertainty." It fell back to start / latest-start / earliest-end / end intervals. — [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/)

### Inferences
- **Feature (1), whole entities.** A PeriodO authority is an embedding whose members are quoted definitions, not shared entities. The "same" period under two authorities is two records. They are related only because they are findable together by label, time and place facets. There is no sameAs link.
- **Feature (1), parts.** Membership is anchored to a passage: the quoted sentence and the `bibo:locator` page. That is a quote anchor into the source.
- **Feature (4), strongly.** Every definition has a stable identifier (`prov:specializationOf`) and versioned states (`prov:wasRevisionOf`), and changes are reviewed patches. That is exactly the split between "entity" and "entity at revision N" that feature (4) needs.
- **Feature (4), "re-affirm" is missing.** PeriodO's versions are of the *curated record*, not of the source. Its source is immutable, a published book, so nothing ever needs re-affirming. MultiBlog's documents are live, so re-affirmation is new work that PeriodO does not model.
- **Feature (2), weakly.** Relations between definitions, such as "these form a periodization, in this order", are added *within* an authority and not across authorities.
- **Feature (3): no forking.** One authority cannot derive from another. When a later scholar reuses Renfrew's periods, the result is a new authority citing the new source.
- **Design lesson.** "Grouping ≠ coherence" is worth copying. Being assigned to an embedding should not by default claim that the members form a consistent system. A separate, explicit assertion should say that they do: PeriodO's "periodization".
- **MacIntyre angle.** PeriodO's refusal to rank ("they only need to be comparable") is the museum-of-texts move in miniature. It is safe for periods because the tellings are scholarly definitions, and it keeps each definition's own words as the authoritative form.

### Gaps
- I could not open the BICS paper beyond its abstract, or Shaw et al. 2015 on the patch system. The current number of definitions and authorities was not found on the pages I fetched; the 2016 "sixty definitions for 'early bronze'" is the only figure.
- I found no PeriodO statement about how often patches are rejected, or about disputes among editors.

## Q2. Factoid prosopography: every assertion tied to a source, and how contradictory tellings are held and compared

### Takeaway
The factoid model at King's College London (KCL) stores *what a source says*, not what is true. Two sources that contradict each other produce two factoids, and the prosopographer's identity judgments are kept as a separate layer. Pasin & Bradley reformulate each factoid as a "Document-Interpretation-Act", which they map to CIDOC CRM's E13 Attribute Assignment: a reified "A says that X is Y".

### Cited Findings
- **Citations verified.**
  - Pasin, M. & Bradley, J. (2015), "Factoid-based prosopography and computer ontologies: towards an integrated approach," *Digital Scholarship in the Humanities* 30(1):86–97. — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article-abstract/30/1/86/352888). Quotations below come from the authors' preprint ("Draft 10/10/12"): [Pasin preprint PDF](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf).
  - Bradley, J. & Short, H. (2005), "Texts into databases: the evolving field of new-style prosopography," *Literary and Linguistic Computing* 20(Suppl. 1):3–24, doi:10.1093/llc/fqi022. The Pasin preprint cites an earlier 2003 ACH/ALLC version. — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Texts%20into%20databases%20new-style%20prosopography); the full citation also appears in the reference list of [Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/).
- **Projects using the model.** The Prosopography of the Byzantine Empire and Prosopography of the Byzantine World (PBE/PBW), the Prosopography of Anglo-Saxon England (PASE), the People of Medieval Scotland (PoMS) "and now more generally" at KCL's Department of Digital Humanities. Each factoid is "the spot in a primary source where something is said about… persons". — [Pasin & Bradley preprint](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf)
- **Contradictions coexist by construction.** "By creating factoids which assert things about what the source says about people, the factoid approach prioritizes the sources, rather than our historians' reading of them. Our data about a person is not, then, so much a narrative that presents a summary written by the prosopographer as a collection of information about what the sources say about him/her, and can represent the multiple, perhaps contradictory, voices of the different sources simultaneously: one saying she is a Saxon, but another saying that she was from Northumbria… If two sources contradict each other, two contradictory factoids will be recorded. For this reason, we call the factoid model source driven." — [Pasin & Bradley preprint](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf)
- **Three parts and an act.** Every factoid implies "an agent who creates the factoid…; a document that inspires it; finally, a state of affairs". The act that binds them is the "Document-Interpretation-Act", which has "an author (who performs the interpretation), a source (what is being interpreted) and an interpretation". — [Pasin & Bradley preprint](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf)
- **CIDOC CRM's E13 Attribute Assignment as the join point.** Quoting the CRM (Crofts et al. 2011), E13 allows "documentation of how the respective assignment came about, and whose opinion it was", so that assigned properties "can also be seen as directly attached to the respective item… possibly as a collection of contradictory values." Pasin & Bradley call E13 "a simple reification mechanism… statements of the kind 'A says that X is Y'" and make their Document-Interpretation-Act a subclass of it. — [Pasin & Bradley preprint](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf)
- **Identity is the historians' assertion, not an inference.** "The identity assertion is the work of our historian partners, and the point of the ontology is not to derive the identity of persons… but merely to express what the historians assert about their materials (including assertion of identity of individuals)". Against Veltman's claim that ontologies "assume a single world-view", they argue that "formal structuring if designed correctly need not impose… a single perspective… By introducing the source as an intermediary, the model can also accommodate contradictory statements from different sources." — [Pasin & Bradley preprint](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf)
- **Self-reported weaknesses.** Historians were asked to be "as literal as possible", "as if they were just 'replicating' the information", although "even the most literal reading cannot escape a fundamental cultural hermeneutical horizon". The link between source and state of affairs "does not express very well its intended meaning", and they want "N possible evidence relations reflecting the degree of certainty". The Clergy of the Church of England Database (CCEd) added certainty codes, which were shown to users but "not used to enhance the search algorithms". — [Pasin & Bradley preprint](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Feature (2), at the finest grain.** Each factoid is an attributed relationship between person, event and place, scoped to a source. The *source* is the telling. An embedding would be a coarser scope, a school, whose members could include factoids.
- **Feature (1) is implicit.** The factoid is a pointer to a passage ("the spot in a primary source"), so assigning *parts* of documents is the model's core. It has no named container above the source level.
- **Features (3) and (4) are absent.** Sources are fixed documents, so there is no revision to pin and nothing to fork.
- **The key lesson for MultiBlog.** The model keeps three layers apart: what a source says (factoid), who read it that way (the interpretation act's author), and which records denote the same person (a separate identity assertion). The third layer is the one that holds rival *tellings* together. An embedding that rejects another embedding's identification ("these two figures are one person") is recording a rival identity assertion, not a rival fact.
- **Stated limits of literalism.** The model's founders concede that it cannot avoid interpretation, which is the reason for attributing the interpretation act.

### Gaps
- I did not reach Bradley & Short's full text. I found no study of *how users actually compared* contradictory factoids, whether in a UI or in reported use. Critiques of the factoid model from outside KCL were not searched.

## Q3. CIDOC CRM's argumentation extension, CRMinf: beliefs, proposition sets, who holds what when

### Takeaway
CRMinf is the most explicit data model found for features (3) and (4). A **Belief** (I2) is a *temporal entity*: one actor holding one **Proposition Set** (I4, which "could be implemented as a named graph") at one truth value over a time-span. Adopting another actor's belief is its own activity, **Belief Adoption** (I7), justified by trust rather than inference. A belief "goes out of existence" when the holder's value changes, and a new belief is created with the old one as its premise.

### Cited Findings
- **Citations verified.**
  - Doerr, M., Kritsotaki, A. & Boutsika, K. (2011), "Factual argumentation—a core model for assertions making," *Journal on Computing and Cultural Heritage* 3(3), article 8 (pp. 1–34), doi:10.1145/1921614.1921615. The abstract proposes "an innovative integrated model of factual argumentation that includes evolution, composition, and revision of arguments. It makes explicit both the processes of argument-making and the states of belief at a particular point in time". — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Factual%20argumentation%20a%20core%20model%20for%20assertions%20making). I read only the abstract.
  - CRMinf v1.0 (October 2023), editors Doerr, Fafalios and Stead, CC BY 4.0. It "is reducing the IAM model in Doerr, Kritsotaki and Boutsika (2011)"; IAM is the Integrated Argumentation Model. It "has so far been validated in the British Museum Discovering Sloan[e] project." — [CRMinf v1.0 PDF](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf); [version page](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/ModelVersion/version-1.0)
- **I2 Belief.** "the notion that the associated I4 Proposition Set is held to have a particular I6 Belief Value by a particular E39 Actor. This can be understood as the period of time that an individual group holds a particular set of propositions to be true, false, or somewhere in between." Its example is "Ian Hodder's belief from 1996 on, that Floor B was earlier than wall C…". — [CRMinf v1.0](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf)
- **I4 Proposition Set.** "the sets of formal, binary propositions that an I2 Belief is held about. It could be implemented as a named graph, a spreadsheet, or any other structured dataset." — [CRMinf v1.0](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf)
- **Rival tellings as parallel belief adoptions.** The worked example contrasts Francesca Bologna (2021) adopting *Tacitus's* account ("Nero… was at Antium… did not return to Rome until the fire approached his house") with her reading of *Suetonius's* account, in which Nero was "singing in Rome while it was burning". Each is a separate proposition set of CRM statements about the same E5 event, the Great Fire of Rome. — [CRMinf v1.0](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf)
- **I7 Belief Adoption.** "the action of an E39 Actor adopting propositions taken from an interpretation of the intended meaning of an instance of E73 Information Object as being true… The basis of I7 Belief Adoption is the justification of trust in the source of the adopted propositions, rather than the application of rules for inferring the respective propositions… Typical examples are the citation of academic papers or the reuse of datasets." — [CRMinf v1.0](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf)
- **Belief lifecycle.**
  - "Only one E39 Actor may hold a particular instance of I2 Belief, though the E38 Actor may, of course, be an instance of E74 Group." A group may gain or lose members "without affecting the belief the group representatively maintains. The members supporting the common belief may not necessarily be individually convinced of it."
  - "If any other instances of E39 Actor wish to adopt the I6 Belief Value about part or all of the I4 Proposition Set…, then a new instance of I7 Belief Adoption must be used to create a new instance of I2 Belief… [which] must share at least some of the propositions in the original I4 Proposition Set."
  - "An instance of I2 Belief goes out of existence when the instance of E39 Actor changes its I6 Belief Value about one or more of the propositions… Should the… Actor continue to hold the same opinion about other propositions…, then a new instance of I5 Inference Making would create a new instance of I2 Belief. The new instance of I5 Inference Making would use the original instance of I2 Belief as a premise."

  — [CRMinf v1.0](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf)
- **Other classes.** I6 Belief Value is "discrete logic, modal logic, probability, fuzziness…", with a minimum of "True; False; Unknown". I3 Inference Logic may be "a rule such as 'later layers are on top of earlier layers', or even a term like 'social intuition', if this is scholarly acceptable". — [CRMinf v1.0](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Feature (3), directly.** Belief Adoption is a fork: another actor takes "part or all" of a proposition set and gets a *new* belief sharing "at least some" propositions. A forked embedding is an I7 adoption from its parent, and the fork records why it trusts the parent.
- **Feature (4), directly.** A belief is time-bounded and dies when any proposition's value changes. A re-affirmation is a new I5 whose premise is the old belief. So "revision N still fits" can be modelled as a *new belief with the prior belief as premise* rather than as a mutation. That keeps the history of commitments separate from the history of the documents.
- **Feature (2).** The propositions in a set are CRM statements, that is relationships between entities. So CRMinf treats relationships as belonging to a belief. It names the named graph as the natural implementation, which the prior survey already covered as a mechanism.
- **Group beliefs.** CRMinf allows a group's belief that no individual member holds. That matches "schools of thought", where an embedding's commitments need not be each contributor's.
- **Heaviness is a risk.** Even the 2023 spec says "the subclasses of I5 Inference Making are not fully developed" and validates the model on one project. Expect that users will not fill in inference logic.

### Gaps
- I did not read the 2011 JOCCH full text. I found no report of CRMinf adoption beyond the stated British Museum validation, and no UI in which rival proposition sets are compared.

## Q4. Drucker & Nowviskie's Temporal Modelling, Drucker 2011, and other multi-perspective timeline tools (Topotime, Heterochronologies)

### Takeaway
Drucker's line of work treats a timeline as an *interpretation from a standpoint*: "time as a factor of X", with a now-slider for the observer's position. The later Heterochronologies project is the sharpest precedent for comparing tellings without a master frame. It compares chronologies "without subsuming them into a hegemonic data model" through "floating correlation" around the events that they share. Topotime adds per-period source attribution and rival part–whole structures.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation verified.** Drucker, J. (2011), "Humanities Approaches to Graphical Display," *DHQ* 5(1). — [DHQ](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/5/1/000091/000091.html)
- **Capta.** "Capta is 'taken' actively while data is assumed to be a 'given'… Humanistic inquiry acknowledges the situated, partial, and constitutive character of knowledge production". Visualization tools "carry with them assumptions of knowledge as observer-independent and certain, rather than observer co-dependent and interpretative." — [Drucker 2011](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/5/1/000091/000091.html)
- **Every metric has a standpoint.** "we need to conceive of every metric 'as a factor of X', where X is a point of view, agenda, assumption, presumption, or simply a convention." On time: "Temporality = time as a factor of X where X is any variable (fear, speed, anxiety, foreshadowing, regret, reconsideration, narration, etc.)". — [Drucker 2011](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/5/1/000091/000091.html)
- **Temporal Modelling.** "The temporal modeling project Bethany Nowviskie and I designed almost ten years ago" aimed at "a set of conceptual primitives for the modeling of temporal relations. These included graphical expressions meant to meet the needs of multiple points of view, reworking events according to a changed position within a temporal sequence, and a set of what we called inflections". Inflections were divided into "semantic" types (qualities of an entity) and "syntactic" types (relational, "marking the effect of one event… or another"). The goal was that "a graphical model could be created intuitively as an interpretation and then used to generate structured data as a result". Even their own community "were unsettled by elastic or stretchy timelines, multiple points of view from within the system". — [Drucker 2011](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/5/1/000091/000091.html). Nowviskie's CV and other secondary pages date the ACH/ALLC presentation to 2003 and describe a "Now-slider Demo" — [search result summary citing nowviskie.org and SpecLab](https://nowviskie.org/cv/). This date was not checked against the ACH/ALLC 2003 programme.
- **Parallax.** "A parallax view, in which prospective anticipation is gradually replaced with retrospective reassessment, can be generated with a slider… The slider indicates a point of view". The stated design principles are "non-continuous, non-homogenous, and multi-directional temporality, as well as the point of view parallax", replacing "uni-directional, continuous, homogenous time". — [Drucker 2011](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/5/1/000091/000091.html)
- **Heterochronologies.** Drucker, J., Polack, P. & Santachiara, P. (2022), "Heterochronologies: a platform for correlation and research in temporal graphics," *DHQ* 16(3). — [DHQ](https://digitalhumanities.org/dhq/vol/16/3/000624/000624.html)
  - Linked Data "enable[s] interoperability at the expense of specificity… it is not focused on… enabling the correlation and comparison of diverse ontologies, or preserving and exploring their epistemic and cultural specificity".
  - The project "regards temporality as a concept expressed epistemically through various culturally-specific, authoritative ontologies… so that the underlying ontologies may be compared without subsuming them into a hegemonic data model… an exercise in comparative ontology."
  - Its method: "align these representations around chronological events that they have in common… floating correlation across points of reference in distinct contexts… rather than within a single unifying framework… Egyptian dynastic periodization can be matched with geological events and Olympiads without all of these needing to be expressed in the Gregorian calendar, and the Winter Counts do not have to take the birth of Christ as the start point".
  - "Chrono-References" (Gregorian, Julian, Islamic, Jewish, Egyptian dynasties, Winter counts, Mayan, Chinese) serve as "substitutable schemes of reference".

  — [DHQ](https://digitalhumanities.org/dhq/vol/16/3/000624/000624.html)
- **Topotime.** Grossner, K. & Meeks, E. (2014), "Topotime: Representing historical temporality," DH2014 (ADHO). Quoted from the abstract:
  - "Periods must have a unique id, a source attribution, and a label for graphical display."
  - "Another scholar's chronology for the same composite event might include an entirely different set of sub-events."
  - Timespans have "fixed or 'fuzzy' bounds."

  — [DH abstracts index](https://dh-abstracts.library.virginia.edu/works/2120). I read the abstract as summarized by the fetch tool, not the full paper.

### Inferences
- **Drucker gives the rationale, not a data model.** An embedding is Drucker's "X". A timeline under embedding E is "time as a factor of E". Temporal Modelling anticipates the *output* side: an interpretation authored graphically and exported as structured data. It has no container, fork or versioning model (features 3 and 4 are absent).
- **Heterochronologies anticipates the comparison view.** Rival tellings are aligned on shared events ("floating correlation") rather than all projected onto one axis. This is the same move as the prior survey's "manufacture the missing coordinate system with a quote anchor into a shared factual spine". It adds a caution: the spine must be *events the tellings share*, not a neutral calendar that one party supplies. That is the MacIntyrean point stated in a data-modelling key.
- **Topotime shows rival part–whole structure is normal.** Two scholars' "same composite event" can have different sub-events. So feature (2), relationships such as part-of scoped to an embedding, is needed even for chronology.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found that Temporal Modelling was used beyond a demo, or of user studies of rival-chronology comparison in Heterochronologies. I found no project named "rival chronologies" as such.

## Q5. Narrative ontologies and other models for several narratives over shared events

### Takeaway
Two models make narrative-scoped or authority-scoped assertions first-class. The Narrative Ontology (NOnt) treats each narrative as its own knowledge base over possibly shared events, and ties text fragments to events. The Simple Event Model (SEM) scopes any statement "according to" an Authority through a `sem:View`. Neither implements comparison or forking of narratives: NOnt explicitly defers multi-narrative operation.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation verified.** Meghini, C., Bartalesi, V. & Metilli, D. (2021), "Representing narratives in digital libraries: The narrative ontology," *Semantic Web* 12(2):241–264, doi:10.3233/SW-200421. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.3233/SW-200421). I read the journal's submission version: [SWJ PDF](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/system/files/swj2539.pdf)
- **Motivation.** Digital libraries cannot "discover a narrative… create one, or… compare two narratives." — [Meghini et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.3233/SW-200421)
- **Narrator.** "We view a narrative as a story told by a narrator, which may be an individual person or a group… The narrative reflects the point of view of its narrator." — [SWJ PDF](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/system/files/swj2539.pdf)
- **Fabula and narration.** The fabula is events plus mereological, causal and temporal relations. A narration is linked to the fabula by a *reference* relation: "a relation that connects regions of narrations, which we call narrative fragment…, to events of the fabula." Two narrations of the same fabula may differ in "the set of fabula's events narrated…; the order…; the expressions used". Narrations "that are incompatible… are not narrations of the same fabula. This fact does not prevent to compare the narrations, for instance to appreciate the differences." — [SWJ PDF](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/system/files/swj2539.pdf)
- **Contradiction handled by partition.** "a narrative about the life of Dante Alighieri may include a travel to France as an event, while another narrative may deny the occurrence of that event… the presence of different versions of the same story is not to be seen as accidental or undesirable in a DL. To the contrary, it manifests different point of views that is important, in some cases vital, to document. On the other hand, the arising of logical contradictions in a KB is highly undesirable… In order to enable a DL to hold incompatible narratives while at the same time avoiding the rise of inconsistencies, we view each narrative as a separate KB, and a DL as a set of narratives, possibly sharing a common set of factual components that occur in the fabulae of these narratives. In the present study, we focus on the structure and the operation of single narratives". — [SWJ PDF](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/system/files/swj2539.pdf)
- **The authoring tool.** The narrative-building tool records "the primary sources of each event", the textual fragment, and "relating the textual fragment with its narrator". It meets the need to let users "keep track of their decisions and compare them with the decisions of other users". It imports default events from Wikidata. — [SWJ PDF](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/system/files/swj2539.pdf)

(2011), "Design and use of the Simple Event Model (SEM)," *Journal of Web Semantics* 9(2):128–136, doi:10.1016/j.websem.2011.03.003. — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Design%20and%20use%20of%20the%20Simple%20Event%20Model)
- **SEM's viewpoint support.**
  - The specification's lead example: "The Dutch launched the first police action in the Dutch East Indies in 1947; the Dutch presented themselves as liberators but were seen as occupiers by the Indonesian people." It "contains conflicting views on the role of the actor… it makes explicit according to which authority the roles hold". The place's type is "an independent Republic according to the Indonesians, but according to the Dutch… a 'controlled region'."
  - SEM "is capable of handling different viewpoints. In particular three aspects…: (1) Event bounded roles, (2) time bounded validity of facts…, and (3) attribution of the authoritative source of a statement."
  - `sem:View`: "Views are properties that only hold according to a certain Authority." `sem:accordingTo`: "used to state which Authority says that a property constrained by the View Constraint is true." `sem:Authority`: "entities that state SEM properties… people, organizations, sources of information, etc."

  — [SEM specification](https://semanticweb.cs.vu.nl/2009/11/sem/). The site's TLS certificate had expired when fetched.
- **Related but read only in abstract.** van den Akker, C. et al. (2011), "Digital hermeneutics: Agora and the online understanding of cultural heritage," WebSci '11, doi:10.1145/2527031.2527039. It models "object-event and event-event relationships that support the interpretation of objects" for Rijksmuseum paintings. — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Digital%20hermeneutics%20Agora)

### Inferences
- **NOnt maps most closely onto the user's design.**
  - A narrative is a container (a separate KB).
  - Its events and their part-of, causal and temporal relations belong to that narrative: feature (2).
  - Fragments of text are anchored to events: feature (1) for parts.
  - Events can be shared across narratives, which is the shared spine.

  Its authors' own gap is the user's opportunity: "we focus on… single narratives". NOnt defines what comparison would operate on but does not implement comparison, forks or versioning (features 3 and 4 are absent).
- **SEM scopes per statement.** A View is scoped per *property instance*, not per container: many small "according to" tags. It shows that role and type disputes ("liberator" or "occupier", "republic" or "controlled region") are the typical content of rival tellings. They are rival *typings* of shared entities, not rival events. That supports the overview's worry that "institution" in two embeddings may not name the same kind of thing.
- **The consistency trade-off is explicit in NOnt.** Contradictions must be partitioned so that each KB stays queryable. An embedding therefore needs to be internally consistent even though the embeddings disagree with one another. This echoes the Cyc microtheory lesson in the prior survey.

### Gaps
- I found no deployed system that shows two NOnt narratives side by side. NOnt's successor work, such as the Mingei craft heritage use or Wikidata-based narrative tooling, was not examined.

## Q6. Community-authored ontologies over museum collections: fluid ontologies, Return to Babel, the Zuni/Cambridge work, the Reciprocal Research Network, Mukurtu and Local Contexts. What did communities' descriptions reveal, and what knowledge should not be translated or made public?

### Takeaway
Practice repeatedly found two things. First, the museum's description often got *what kind of thing* an object was wrong. Enote's delegation put misinformation at about 80% of Zuni records, and a "Hopi blanket" was in fact a Zuni ceremonial shawl. Communities describe objects as things-in-use embedded in narratives and relationships, not as specimens. Second, some knowledge must not circulate at all, or only to some people. The durable systems (Mukurtu, the Plateau Peoples' Web Portal, the RRN, TK Labels) answered both by keeping **parallel, attributed records that share one media object**, each carrying its **own access protocol**. They did not merge into one catalogue.

### Inferences
- **Feature (1): a shared object with independent records.** Mukurtu's community record is the most exact built analogue of assigning an entity to several embeddings. One media asset carries N records, each with independent metadata, its own steward and its own protocol, displayed together. The Plateau "tribal path" is the same idea per tribe. The RRN's normalized and raw tabs are a weak version: the institution's own record kept in two forms.
- **Feature (2) is mostly absent in these systems.** They attach *descriptions* per community, not per-community *relations* between objects. Village Voice's and Eventspace's community-drawn concept maps (metaviews) are the exception: relations authored per community or participant over shared nodes.
- **Feature (3).** Eventspace's shareable metaviews ("everyone can create, edit, and add metaviews") come closest. I found no system that forks one community's structure from another's.
- **Feature (4).** None records the revision of the object record at the time a community record was written.
- **"What kind of thing" answer.** Communities corrected *category* ("Hopi blanket" to Zuni ceremonial shawl), *provenance* and *use*. They said that objects are things-in-use tied to people, places and practice. Warumungu elders said ritual objects are "disconnected from the practices, people, and places that they need to be efficacious". This is MacIntyre's complaint made by the tradition's own members about an archive, and it is what feature (1) cannot fix alone. An object placed in an embedding still needs its relations, which is why feature (2) matters.
- **Knowledge that should not be translated or made public is a first-class requirement, and it constrains comparison.**
  - Zuni participants said some objects "should not be displayed" at all.
  - Warumungu protocols restrict by gender, family, country and death.
  - The TK Labels' answer, redacting the item while keeping the label and a description, is a pattern for an embedding that includes a passage but does not show it to everyone.
  - A comparison page that puts every embedding side by side is itself a "neutral context" in MacIntyre's sense. Some embeddings may need to be *uncomparable to outsiders*.
- **Boast's asymmetry critique applies to the platform.** The host's catalogue ("the language of museums") remains the default record, and community voices are added to it. A multi-author blog that offers "embeddings" is also the host. Whose record is shown first, and who stewards the shared object, are political choices, not neutral defaults.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full text of the 2007, 2009 or 2010 Srinivasan papers, or of Boast & Enote (2013). Detailed Zuni findings, such as specific categories disputed or objects Zuni reviewers declined to describe, come from the abstract and one secondary source.
- I found no published measurement of how often Mukurtu sites use community records, or of whether multiple community records on one item ever *conflict*.
- I did not verify the Library of Congress's adoption of TK Labels on the Passamaquoddy wax-cylinder recordings, which I believe happened. It is omitted as a finding.

## Q7. Controversy mapping as a method (Venturini 2010, 2012; Venturini & Munk 2021; MACOSPOL and EMAPS)

### Takeaway
Controversy mapping supplies the method, not the data model. Objectivity comes from *multiplying partial viewpoints* ("second-degree objectivity"). Actors in a controversy "cannot even agree on questions". The analyst must "roam from cosmos to cosmos", each cosmos being an actor's orderly vision of how the world should be. This bears on embeddings as structures: rival embeddings may carve the question differently, not just answer it differently.

### Cited Findings
- **Citations verified.**
  - Venturini, T. (2010), "Diving in magma: how to explore controversies with actor-network theory," *Public Understanding of Science* 19(3):258–273, doi:10.1177/0963662509102694; published online 2009. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963662509102694). Quotations are from the médialab draft: [PDF](https://medialab.sciencespo.fr/publications/Venturini-Diving_in_Magma.pdf)
  - Venturini, T. (2012), "Building on faults: How to represent controversies with digital methods," *PUS* 21(7):796–812, doi:10.1177/0963662510387558; published online 2010. It proposes "the controversy-website… a multilayered toolkit to trace and aggregate information on public debates." — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Building%20on%20faults%20controversies%20digital%20methods)
  - Venturini, T. & Munk, A. K. (2021), *Controversy Mapping: A Field Guide*, Polity, ISBN 9781509544516. — [Wiley/Polity](https://www.wiley.com/en-cn/Controversy+Mapping:+A+Field+Guide-p-9781509544516); [CIS-CNRS](https://cis.cnrs.fr/en/controversy-mapping-pub/). The book itself was not read.

- **Objectivity through multiplicity.** "Objectivity can be pursued only by multiplying the points of observation. The more numerous and partial are the perspectives from which a phenomenon is considered, the more objective and impartial will be its observation". Footnote 10: "Latour calls 'second-degree objectivity' this effort to consider as much subjectivity as possible. Unlike first-degree objectivity, which defines a situation of collective agreement, second-degree objectivity is attained by revealing the full extent of actors' disagreement". — [Venturini draft](https://medialab.sciencespo.fr/publications/Venturini-Diving_in_Magma.pdf)
- **Actors disagree on the questions.** "The difficulty of controversy is not that actors disagree on answers, but that they cannot even agree on questions." - **Cosmos and cosmopolitics.** "ideologies are orderly and harmonious: they are not universes, but cosmos… Only roaming from cosmos to cosmos, social cartographers can perceive the full extent of their controversies." The mapper "does not boast impartiality − it just requires its practitioners to present other partialities besides their own. Social cartography is not meant to close controversies, but to show that they may be closed in many different ways." — [Venturini draft](https://medialab.sciencespo.fr/publications/Venturini-Diving_in_Magma.pdf)
- **Power and definition.** MACOSPOL defines controversy as "every bit of science and technology which is not yet stabilized, closed or 'black boxed'… shared uncertainty". Venturini adds: "Controversies are struggles to conserve or reverse social inequalities." — [Venturini draft](https://medialab.sciencespo.fr/publications/Venturini-Diving_in_Magma.pdf)
- **The projects.** MACOSPOL (FP7 project 217701) was coordinated by Bruno Latour and built the platform mappingcontroversies.net. EMAPS was a three-year FP7 follow-on ("drawing directly on the results of the project MACOSPOL"), with climate maps at climaps.eu. — [CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/217701); [UiO TIK](https://www.sv.uio.no/tik/english/research/projects/macospol/); [médialab EMAPS](https://medialab.sciencespo.fr/en/activities/emaps/)

### Inferences
- **None of the four features as a data model.** Controversy mapping is about *how an observer composes* many partial views. Its lesson for embeddings is epistemic: a comparison view should show the full extent of disagreement ("second-degree objectivity") rather than a neutral summary.
- **"Cannot agree on questions" supports the overview's instinct.** Rival embeddings may differ in *which entities and relations exist* (feature 2), not only in claims about shared entities. SEM's "liberator or occupier" and NOnt's "incompatible narrations are not of the same fabula" say the same thing in data-model terms.
- **Cosmos and embedding.** A "cosmos" is an orderly vision that an actor holds. It is close to the user's "school of thought" and, like MacIntyre's traditions, it is defined by coherence rather than by topic.

### Gaps
- I did not read the 2021 field guide. I found no evaluation of how users actually read MACOSPOL or EMAPS controversy websites.

## Q8. Textual editions that present rival readings or rival editorial theories side by side (beyond collation tools)

### Takeaway
I verified only a little here. Shillingsburg's "orientations" (documentary, sociological, authorial, bibliographic, aesthetic) are the textual-scholarship analogue of schools of thought: each orientation yields a *different edited text* from the same documents. His later "knowledge site" idea is reported to host several at once.

### Cited Findings
- Shillingsburg, P. L., *Scholarly Editing in the Computer Age: Theory and Practice*, 3rd ed. ### Inferences
- An editorial orientation is an embedding over a shared document set. It selects *parts*, readings from particular witnesses (feature 1), and asserts *relations*, such as "this reading is the author's final intention" (feature 2). If editions were modelled this way, each edition would be a fork of an earlier edition with some variants re-decided (feature 3).

## Q9. Synthesis: which features (1)–(4) each precedent anticipates, and what practice showed about comparing rival tellings

### Takeaway
Features (1) and (2) are well precedented. Parts are covered by PeriodO's quoted definitions, factoids and NOnt fragments. Whole entities are covered by Mukurtu's community records. Relations are covered by NOnt fabulae, SEM Views and CRMinf proposition sets. Feature (4) has one strong precedent (PeriodO's PROV versioning) and one conceptual one (CRMinf's belief lifecycle). Feature (3), forking, appears only as CRMinf's Belief Adoption and Eventspace's shareable metaviews. **No surveyed system implements forkable, versioned, named containers of entities *and* relations.**

### Inferences
- **The novel part of the user's design is (3) plus (4) over (1) plus (2) together.** The model would be a named, forkable container of passage anchors *and* relations, pinned to document revisions and re-affirmed over time. CRMinf gives it a vocabulary:
  - embedding membership = a Belief held by a group;
  - a fork = a Belief Adoption of part or all of a parent;
  - a re-affirmation = a new Inference Making with the old belief as premise.

  PeriodO gives the engineering pattern: a stable ID, `wasRevisionOf` chains, reviewed patches and a public changelog.
- **Keep "grouped" separate from "coherent".** PeriodO's authority/periodization split and NOnt's "each narrative is a separate KB" both point to an explicit, separate claim that an embedding's members form a consistent system. Membership alone should not make that claim.
- **Comparison needs a shared spine, and the spine is the danger.** Heterochronologies' floating correlation on shared events and NOnt's "common set of factual components" are the positive pattern. Boast's asymmetry critique and Christen's Curtis example show the failure: a host frame presented as neutral.
  - Taking MacIntyre seriously means embeddings must be able to (a) retype shared entities, as in SEM, and (b) decline to be shown in comparison, or show a labeled redaction as TK Labels do.
  - They must also (c) carry their own relations, because the object without its practices is the decontextualized museum piece.
- **Communities' own descriptions changed the category, not just the facts.** Examples are the ceremonial shawl and the ritual objects that need practice to be efficacious. Some knowledge was not for outsiders at all. A tag cannot express either point; both need the composite, relation-bearing structure the user already wants.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study comparing how readers understand side-by-side rival *structures*, as opposed to side-by-side texts. No precedent found re-affirms a membership against a *changed* source, because every surveyed source is immutable apart from PeriodO's curated records.
