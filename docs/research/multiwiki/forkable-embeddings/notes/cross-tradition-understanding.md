## 1. MacIntyre's own positive account: how a tradition defeats or learns from a rival, what it asks of the learner, and whether it can be institutionalized

### Takeaway
MacIntyre's answer is a *first-person, asymmetric narrative* test, not a comparison run from a neutral place. A tradition vindicates itself when it can tell, in its own terms, why its rival failed *by the rival's own standards*. Only someone who has learned the rival as a "second first language" can judge that, and doing so takes a "rare gift of empathy". His earlier essay "Relativism, Power and Philosophy" (1985) spells out the neutral "third language" that the design brief is tempted to build, and argues that it fails in a specific way. The piece that most directly anticipates a multi-embedding wiki is his 1991 claim that comparative work is really "comparing Confucian comparisons … with Aristotelian comparisons". His one institutional proposal is the university of "constrained disagreement". I found no secondary literature treating crisis-resolution as a procedure a *system* could run.

**WJWR (1988), chs. 18–20.** The full text is only a lending-library scan, so what follows comes through secondary sources.
- The three requirements for resolving a crisis (WJWR ch. 18, "360ff"), paraphrased by John Flett. First, the new framework "must systematically solve the previously intractable problems". Second, "it must articulate the causes of the tradition's sterility". Third, "it must be in continuity with the defining beliefs of the preceding tradition." — [Flett, "Alasdair MacIntyre's Tradition-Constituted Enquiry in Polanyian Perspective," *Tradition & Discovery* 26:2](https://www.polanyisociety.org/TAD%20WEB%20ARCHIVE/TAD26-2/TAD26-2-fnl-pg6-20-pdf.pdf)
- What is demanded of the learner, as Flett quotes WJWR p. 167: a "rare gift of empathy". Protagonists must "understand the theses, arguments, and concepts of their rival in such a way that they are able to view themselves from such an alien standpoint and to re-characterize their own beliefs in an appropriate manner from the alien perspective". Flett adds: "MacIntyre dubs this learning an alien tradition, learning a 'second first language' (WJ, 394ff)." — [Flett](https://www.polanyisociety.org/TAD%20WEB%20ARCHIVE/TAD26-2/TAD26-2-fnl-pg6-20-pdf.pdf)
- Defeat is not the same as crisis-resolution, per Flett: when a tradition adopts a rival's answer, "juxtaposition rather than continuity has given coherence to their problematic. Flett quotes Three Rival Versions: "rationality… requires this acknowledgement of defeat in respect of truth…" — [Flett](https://www.polanyisociety.org/TAD%20WEB%20ARCHIVE/TAD26-2/TAD26-2-fnl-pg6-20-pdf.pdf)
- The TRV claim that truth outruns warrant (TRV 121–22, quoted in Flett's n. 13): "in judging of truth and falsity there is always some ineliminable reference beyond the scheme within which those judgements are made … Truth cannot be identified with, or collapsed into warranted assertibility." This is the direct counter to the "thin" languages' "truth assimilated … to warranted assertibility". — [Flett, n. 13](https://www.polanyisociety.org/TAD%20WEB%20ARCHIVE/TAD26-2/TAD26-2-fnl-pg6-20-pdf.pdf)
- WJWR p. 350: "There is no standing ground, no place for enquiry … apart from that which is provided by some particular tradition or other." — [IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/mac-over/)
- WJWR p. 355, as the IEP paraphrases it: whether a dispute is settled "depends on whether the adherents of either tradition, on seeing a failure in their own tradition are willing to consider an answer offered by their rival". — [IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/mac-over/)

**"Relativism, Power and Philosophy" (APA Presidential Address, delivered 29 Dec. 1984; *Proceedings and Addresses of the APA* 59(1), Sept. 1985, 5–22).** This is the direct precursor of WJWR ch. 19. Quotations below were read from a JSTOR-derived text; I restored the spacing, and pages follow the scan's running heads.
- On proper names (p. 7): "If, for example, I speak in Irish … of Doire Colmcille … the presuppositions and implications of my utterance are quite other than if I speak in English of Londonderry. But, it may be asked, are these not simply two names of one and the same place? The answer is first that no proper name of place or person names any place or person as such; it names in the first instance only for those who are members of some particular linguistic and cultural community, by identifying places and persons in terms of the scheme of identification shared by, and perhaps partially constitutive of, that community." — [text via pdfcoffee (JSTOR scan)](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html); citation per [Chopra](https://samirchopra.com/2016/05/05/alasdair-macintyre-on-relativism-and-the-immigrant/) and [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/MACRPA-2)
- Also p. 7: "'Doire Colmcille' is as a name untranslatable; you can translate the Gaelic expression 'doire Colmcille' by the English expression 'St. Columba's oak grove'; but that cannot be the translation of a place name, for it is not itself the name of any place." — [same](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)
- Against the reply that a stranger can use "Londonderry" neutrally (p. 8): "that stranger's secondary use of the name is therefore parasitic upon its uses by the primary community. They are very specifically names-as-used-by-strangers-or-tourists." — [same](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)
- The competence test for bilinguals (p. 10): "One of the marks of a genuinely adequate knowledge of two quite different languages by one and the same person is that person's ability to discriminate between those parts of each language which are translatable into the other and those which are not. Some degree of partial untranslatability marks the relationship of every language to every other." Also: "that we can understand completely what is being said in some language other than our own never entails that we can translate what we understand." — [same](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)
- On power (p. 11): "one instrument of such force is the imposition of one's own language at the expense of the other's." — [same](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)
- Specification of the neutral "third language" (p. 13). "A necessary first step out of the relativistic predicament would be the learning of some third language". It must have two features. First, its use "does not presuppose allegiance to either of the two rival sets of beliefs". Second, it "must be able to provide the resources for an accurate representation of the two competing schemes of belief", including each community's traditional background. In it, "Names in consequence will have to have become detached from those descriptions which, within some given and presupposed context defined by the beliefs of some particular community, uniquely identify person or place … Names of places will have become equally available for any user to employ whatever his or her beliefs. Names having been Fregean will have become by a process of social change Kripkean." — [same](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)
- More features of that language (p. 14): "A second feature of this type of language will be the absence of texts which are canonical for its common use. Allusion and quotation will have become specialised devices and the literate will have been divorced from the literary." "Where the text is in a foreign language, translation will be supplemented both by paraphrase and by scholarly gloss." "Words as common as 'polis' and 'dikaiosune' in fifth century Attic Greek cannot be translated in any strict sense into twentieth century English … but their use can be quite adequately elucidated." — [same](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)
- Asymmetry of representation (p. 14): modern English, following Charles Kahn, can accurately represent the Homeric uses of *eimi*, but "it would not have been possible within the Homeric linguistic community to represent accurately the modern English uses of 'exist'." — [same](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)
- Why the third language fails (p. 15): it has no canonical texts, so it accumulates all of them, "the whole finally to be assembled in that modern liberal arts college museum of academic culture, whose introductory tour is provided by those Great Books courses which run from Gilgamesh to Saul Bellow via Confucius, Dante, Newton, Tristram Shandy and Margaret Mead". Inside such cultures "Rational justification … becomes an essentially contested concept". This is the "museum" charge, three years before WJWR. — [same](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)

### Inferences
- MacIntyre's success criterion is a **narrative written from inside one tradition**, in two parts: an account of why the rival failed on its own terms, plus, in a real crisis, a re-telling of one's own history. A system cannot compute that verdict. It can host it, keep its revisions and let it be contested. The embedding design fits the 1991 "comparison of comparisons" almost exactly, provided each embedding is treated as one tradition's comparison and never as the system's. A top-level "neutral comparison" view would be the 1985 "third language".
- The 1985 specification of the third language reads like a database schema. Names are "detached" from community descriptions and "equally available for any user"; nothing is canonical; foreign texts are "supplemented both by paraphrase and by scholarly gloss". A wiki's global IDs, revision stamps and relation types are Kripkean names in exactly this sense. **This is the sharpest form of the "new museum" objection**, and it comes from MacIntyre himself. The design has to decide whether its anchors claim only to be *secondary, tourist-grade* identifiers (which MacIntyre concedes exist, as "names-as-used-by-strangers-or-tourists") or whether they serve as the terms of comparison.
- MacIntyre's own mark of bilingual competence, the ability to tell which parts of each language are translatable and which are not, implies a concrete feature. A mapping layer between embeddings or ontologies should make **"untranslatable here, and why" a first-class, authored relation**, not merely an absence of equivalence. The *eimi*/'exist' asymmetry implies that **mappings should be directional**: a rendering of A-in-B's-terms does not license B-in-A's-terms.
- WJWR asks the learner to "re-characterize their own beliefs … from the alien perspective". That suggests a second artifact type beside "how we see them": "how we look from their side", authored by one's own tradition but meant for the rival to check (see §4).

## 2. Thick and thin: Walzer's reiterated minimum, and whether thick concepts can be mapped across traditions by extension alone

### Takeaway
Walzer's thin morality is *not* a common coordinate system. It is "some reiterated features of particular thick or maximal moralities", it shows up only in crises and encounters, and any statement of it is "necessarily expressive of our own thick morality". The McDowell–Williams disentangling argument says an outsider cannot master a thick term's extension without grasping its evaluative point. The "shapelessness" thesis says no independent non-evaluative similarity holds that extension together. Together they imply that **mapping one tradition's term onto another's by extension alone presupposes the insider competence the mapping was supposed to replace.** The debate is live: Blackburn, Elstein & Hurka and Väyrynen resist; Roberts and Kirchin defend.

### Cited Findings
**Walzer, *Thick and Thin: Moral Argument at Home and Abroad* (Notre Dame, 1994), ch. 1 "Moral Minimalism".** Page numbers from the chapter PDF.
- The Prague picture (pp. 1–2): marchers carry signs saying "Truth" and "Justice", and "I knew immediately what the signs meant … I could carry the same signs." The agreement works partly because of "what the marchers did not mean": "No particular account of truth was at issue here." — [Walzer ch. 1 PDF](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)
- Embedding (p. 3): "minimalist meanings are embedded in the maximal morality, expressed in the same idiom, sharing the same (historical/cultural/religious/political) orientation. Minimalism is liberated from its embeddedness and appears independently, in varying degrees of thinness, only in the course of a personal or social crisis or a political confrontation." Also: "Were there no common understanding of tyranny, access would fail. At the same time, the same words have further meanings for the marchers … which we, looking on from far away, may well miss. They resonate differently in Prague than their translations resonate in, say, Paris or New York." — [same](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)
- Against a neutral code (p. 7): the standard view hopes to "construct a complete objective and unexpressive code -- a kind of moral Esperanto. But this hope is misbegotten, for minimalism is neither objective nor unexpressive. It is reiteratively particularist and locally significant". — [same](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)
- p. 9: "A moral equivalent of Esperanto is probably impossible -- or, rather, just as Esperanto is much closer to European languages than to any others, so minimalism when it is expressed as Minimal Morality will be forced into the idiom and orientation of one of the maximal moralities. There is no neutral (unexpressive) moral language." — [same](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)
- p. 10: "the moral minimum is not a freestanding morality. It simply designates some reiterated features of particular thick or maximal moralities." — [same](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)
- p. 18: "It is the product of a historical conjuncture, not of a philosophical 'in-the-beginning.' Minimalism is not foundational: it is not the case that different groups of people discover that they are all committed to the same set of ultimate values." Also: "the minimum is not the foundation of the maximum, only a piece of it. The value of minimalism lies in the encounters it facilitates, of which it is also the product." — [same](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)
- pp. 18–19: "Minimalism leaves room for thickness elsewhere; indeed, it presupposes thickness elsewhere. If we did not have our own parade, we could not march vicariously in Prague." — [same](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)

**Williams (1985) and McDowell (1981); the disentangling debate.**
- Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985): thick concepts are "action-guiding" and "guided by the world" (pp. 128, 140–41). Their world-guidedness "is enough to refute the simplest oppositions of fact and value" (p. 150). Thin terms "do not display world-guidedness" (p. 152). — [IEP, "Thick Concepts"](https://iep.utm.edu/thick-co/)
- Williams concedes that an anthropologist can anticipate insider usage but must at least "grasp imaginatively" the evaluative point of a term like "chaste" (1985: 142). — [IEP, "Thick Concepts"](https://iep.utm.edu/thick-co/)
- The SEP entry reconstructs McDowell 1981 [1998: 202]. (D1) If E could be disentangled into a co-extensive non-evaluative description D plus an evaluation, one could master E's extension without understanding its evaluative orientation. (D2) "It isn't possible to anticipate the usage of E in a way required for mastering its extension without understanding the evaluative orientation of E." (C) So E cannot be disentangled. McDowell's scenario starts from "a specific conception of some moral virtue … current in a reasonably cohesive moral community" and an "outsider" who cannot share its evaluative perspective. — [SEP, "Thick Ethical Concepts" (first pub. 2016, rev. 21 Jan 2025)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thick-ethical-concepts/)
- SEP's "Shapelessness" thesis: "The extensions of evaluative terms and concepts aren't unified under independently intelligible non-evaluative relations of real similarity, not even as a matter of synthetic a posteriori truth." — [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thick-ethical-concepts/)
- Responses, as SEP summarizes them. Blackburn: "evaluation drives extension", yet the two remain separable. Elstein & Hurka: two reductive analysis patterns. Väyrynen: the evaluation is a pragmatically generated "not-at-issue" implication, not semantic content. Roberts and Kirchin: defend Shapelessness and inseparability. Eklund: objectionable thick terms such as "chaste". — [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thick-ethical-concepts/)
- SEP does not treat cross-cultural translation systematically. It calls thick terms "parochial" and discusses a "hypertraditional" society whose vocabulary is all thick. — [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thick-ethical-concepts/)

### Inferences
- Walzer maps closely onto the brief's hope for "coordinate systems grown into existence later". His thin morality *is* grown later, out of encounters, as "a piece" of each thick morality. But he adds two constraints the design should respect. First, the thin layer is **owned**: every articulation is in some tradition's idiom ("no neutral (unexpressive) moral language"). Second, it is **episodic**: it appears in "crisis or … confrontation". So an ontology bridging embeddings should itself be an attributed, forkable embedding, and never a system-level layer.
- Walzer also offers something checkable: the minimum is *reiterated*. An embedding-based system could record **reiteration** directly. Several embeddings would each independently point, in their own words, at the same passage or event and say "this, too, we condemn". That is different from asserting a shared concept. Recognition of this kind comes *from* each thick side, which is what Walzer means.
- The disentangling argument hits hardest at ontology alignment by extension (owl:sameAs, or matching instance sets). If Shapelessness holds, an outsider cannot construct the extension of "chaste" (or *li*, *ḥalāl*, "grace"). **Extensional mappings are only as good as the insider competence of whoever authored them.** The design should record who mapped, from which embedding, and whether insiders accepted it. Väyrynen's pragmatic view is the best resource for the opposite argument: if evaluation is not truth-conditional, extensional mapping becomes more respectable.

### Gaps
- I read McDowell's own wording only as SEP reconstructs it. The original is "Non-Cognitivism and Rule-Following" in Holtzman & Leich (eds.), *Wittgenstein: To Follow a Rule* (1981), reprinted in McDowell 1998, p. 202 per SEP.
- I did not fetch Väyrynen (*The Lewd, the Rude and the Nasty*, 2013), Roberts, Kirchin (*Thick Evaluation*, 2017) or Elstein & Hurka (2009) directly. Their positions are as SEP characterizes them.
- I found no literature applying the disentangling argument to ontology alignment or knowledge-graph mapping specifically.

## 3. Kuhn's late view (bilingualism, interpretation vs. translation, ostension) and whether shared *reference* can be neutral where shared *sense* is not

### Takeaway
Kuhn (1982) separates **interpretation** (learning a new language, which is always possible) from **translation**, which may fail. He explicitly grants that historians "can and must use modern language to identify referents of out-of-date terms", and that such reference-determinations work "like the native's pointing to gavagais" as the examples from which meaning is learned. He denies that they amount to translation, because "reference-preserving translations may be incoherent". On shared reference the verdict is split. **Scheffler and Kitcher** say it secures comparability. **Kuhn** says it is necessary scaffolding but not meaning, and for *kind* terms reference is itself taxonomy-relative. **MacIntyre (1985)** goes furthest: even *proper names* are community-bound, and neutral "Kripkean" naming is a product of the modern third language.

### Cited Findings
**Kuhn, "Commensurability, Comparability, Communicability," *PSA 1982*, vol. 2, pp. 669–688 (pub. 1983).** Pages checked in the JSTOR scan.
- p. 670: "The claim that two theories are incommensurable is then the claim that there is no language, neutral or otherwise, into which both theories, conceived as sets of sentences, can be translated without residue or loss. No more in its metaphorical than its literal form does incommensurability imply incomparability". — [Kuhn 1982 PDF](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)
- pp. 672–673: interpretation "is an enterprise practiced by historians and anthropologists … If the interpreter succeeds, what he or she has in the first instance done is learn a new language … Acquiring a new language is not the same as translating from it into one's own. Success with the first does not imply success with the second." — [same](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)
- p. 673: "Rather than translate, the interpreter can simply learn the animal and use the natives' term for it." But "There need be no English description coreferential with the native term 'gavagai'". The interpreter "may have learned to recognize distinguishing features unknown to English speakers and for which English supplies no descriptive terminology." — [same](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)
- p. 674, on Kitcher's phlogiston critique: "I have no doubt that historians dealing with old scientific texts can and must use modern language to identify referents of out-of-date terms. Like the native's pointing to gavagais, these reference-determinations often provide the concrete examples from which historians may hope to learn what the problematical expressions in their texts mean." But Kitcher "describes this process of reference-determination as translation, and he suggests that its availability should bring talk of incommensurability to a close. In both these respects he seems to me mistaken." — [same](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)
- pp. 674–675: leaving non-referring terms blank "is, however, to fail as a translator … old scientific texts must be treated with at least the courtesy normally extended to works of fiction. They report what scientists of the past believed, independent of its truth-value". — [same](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)
- p. 681: "What is it that translation must preserve? Not merely reference, I have argued, for reference-preserving translations may be incoherent, impossible to understand while the terms they employ are taken in their usual sense." — [same](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)
- p. 683: "Translation is, of course, only the first resort of those who seek comprehension. Communication can be established in its absence. But where translation is not feasible, the very different processes of interpretation and language acquisition are required." — [same](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)

**The late Kuhn (*The Road Since Structure*) and bilingualism.**
- SEP: "Where Kuhn had earlier likened the process by which historians come to understand antiquated science as a special type of translation, he retracted these claims, insisting that the process is one of language learning, not translation" (Kuhn 2000 [1993], 238, 244). In the SEP authors' own words: "one must become *bilingual*, learning to use (and keep separate) the incongruently structured lexical taxonomies". SEP also gives the "no-overlap principle", under which kind terms may not overlap "unless they are related as species to genus", and Kuhn's stress on *local* incommensurability. — [SEP, "The Incommensurability of Scientific Theories" (Oberheim & Hoyningen-Huene, rev. 7 May 2024)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incommensurability/)
- 3 Quarks Daily attributes to Kuhn's 1987 lecture "Regaining the Past" (in *The Presence of Past Science*) the line that the historian "must become bilingual, speaking with both Aristotle and Newton on their own language". It also attributes: "There is no truth-preserving way which the Greek statements can be rendered in our later lexicon." The wording is not verified against a primary text. — [3 Quarks Daily, "The Incommensurable Legacy of Thomas Kuhn" (2023)](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2023/01/the-incommensurable-legacy-of-thomas-kuhn.html)

**Ostension, names vs. kinds.**
- Pirozelli: for Kuhn, "learning begins with the ostension of similar objects" (Kuhn 1974: 309; 1989: 66). Kuhn distinguishes two kinds of ostension: "while for proper names a single act of ostension may be enough to establish the reference, kind terms demand a number of acts of ostension" (Kuhn 1979: 200; 1989: 67; 1993a: 230). One flaw Kuhn finds in the causal theory of reference is that it cannot tell these two classes apart (Kuhn 1989). — [Pirozelli, "Thomas Kuhn's Philosophy of Language," *Trans/Form/Ação* 43 (2020): 345–372](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3842/384272794025.pdf)
- Pirozelli: for Kuhn "the reference of a taxonomic term is not independent of human classification". A kind "will populate the world as well as divide up a preexisting population" (1993a: 229). — [Pirozelli](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3842/384272794025.pdf)
- Mary Hesse replied in the same PSA volume ("Comment on Kuhn's 'Commensurability, Comparability, Communicability'", *PSA 1982* vol. 2, 704–711). The handoff summarizes it as pushing Kuhn toward a theory of ostensive and analogical language learning. — [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/HESCOK)

**Reference as the neutral ground: the realist reply.**
- Scheffler, *Science and Subjectivity* (1967), used Frege's sense/reference distinction: "even if the sense of a scientific term varies with theory, it does not follow that the reference of the term also varies with theory. Terms may co-refer but differ in sense." Kitcher's context-sensitive "reference of tokens" extends that line (1978). — [search summaries of Sankey's overviews](https://philarchive.org/archive/SANIAO-3); [PhilPapers, Scheffler](https://philpapers.org/rec/SCHSAS-4)
- MacIntyre's counter on names (1985): see §1 (Doire Colmcille; "names-as-used-by-strangers-or-tourists"; "Names having been Fregean will have become by a process of social change Kripkean"). — [MacIntyre 1985 text](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)

### Inferences
- For the design, the **passage anchor** (a quote-anchored span, a revision stamp) is the analogue of pointing at gavagais. Kuhn says pointing of this kind is legitimate and *indispensable* for language learning. It is what lets a learner in one embedding see which passages the other tradition calls by a term. Kuhn denies only that it counts as translation. So the right claim for anchors is "a shared place to point at while learning", not "a shared meaning". That is defensible even on Kuhn's terms.
- The anchor is closer to Kuhn's easy case, proper names or demonstratives needing one ostension, than to kind terms. MacIntyre contests even that case. Two readings follow. On a Kuhnian reading, pointing at "the same passage in revision r" is about as neutral as anything gets. On MacIntyre's reading it is neutral only as a tourist's name, parasitic on some community's primary use. **What makes a passage "the same passage", and which revision and span count, is itself tradition-laden** where the text is canonical for one side. An example is which recension of a scripture, or which edition of a disputed history. Anchors into a doc that one embedding treats as canonical and another as one source among many are not symmetric.
- Scheffler/Kitcher-style comparison does not need shared meaning, only shared reference. On that basis a system *could* hold claims from different embeddings "about the same event" side by side without translating them. Kuhn's reply sets the limit: side-by-side display gives comparability only for referring expressions. It says nothing about non-referring or evaluative terms (phlogiston, the thick concepts of §2), which is where traditions most differ.

## 4. Interactional expertise, the Imitation Game and the ideological Turing test as operational tests of whether a rendering "would be recognized" by insiders

### Takeaway
Collins and Evans offer the closest thing to an *empirical, repeatable* version of MacIntyre's insider-recognition criterion. In the Imitation Game, insider judges try to tell a genuine member from an outsider pretending to be one, and the method has been run on active Christians vs. secular participants across European countries. Their claim that fluency in a form of life can be gained "from spoken discourse alone" is an optimistic counterpart to the "second first language". Caplan's ideological Turing test (2011) is a lighter, informal version that is widely cited but, as far as I found, not formally validated.

### Cited Findings
- Collins, Evans, Ribeiro & Hall, "Experiments with interactional expertise," *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science A* 37(4), 2006, 656–674, doi:10.1016/j.shpsa.2006.09.005. As PhilPapers summarizes the abstract: interactional expertise "is developed through linguistic interaction without full scale practical immersion in a culture". In the experiments, "the linguistic performance of those well socialized in the language of a specialist group is indistinguishable from those with full blown practical socialization but distinguishable from those who are not well socialized". — [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/COLEWI); [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsa.2006.09.005)
- Collins 2004, as quoted in Collins & Evans 2015: "it is possible to learn to say everything that can be said about bicycle-riding, car-driving or the use of a stick by a blind man, without ever having ridden a bike, driven a car, or been blind and used a stick. One could learn to pass the corresponding Turing Tests purely [from] spending enough time talking with the practitioners" (Collins 2004b: 127, i.e. "Interactional expertise as a third kind of knowledge," *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 3(2): 125–143). — [Collins & Evans, "Expertise revisited, Part I—Interactional expertise," *SHPS A* 54 (2015) 113–123, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.04423)
- Collins & Evans 2015 on the underlying thesis: "a conceptual grasp of socially embedded practices can be acquired from spoken discourse alone". They also stress that passing the game is not *required* for interactional expertise. In Collins's gravitational-wave game, "mathematical and algebraic questions were banned, personal questions were not asked … the dialogues had to be edited to remove obvious indicators of style". They introduce "ubiquitous interactional expertise" and note that where it ends "is not clear (how should we describe the blind or the religious?)". — [arXiv 1611.04423](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.04423)
- Collins & Evans, "Quantifying the Tacit: The Imitation Game and Social Fluency," *Sociology* 48(1), 2014, 3–19. The abstract reports trials with the colour blind, the blind and those with perfect pitch, and "on more conventional sociological topics such as the social relationships between men and women, homosexuals and heterosexuals, and active Christians and secular students". The method can "compare social relationships across social and temporal distances in both a qualitative and a quantitative way". — [Crossref/SAGE DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0038038512455735)
- Collins, Evans, Weinel, Lyttleton-Smith, Bartlett & Hall, "The Imitation Game and the Nature of Mixed Methods," *Journal of Mixed Methods Research* 11(4), 2017, 510–527. Per the abstract, "deep cultural input is initially supplied by the players of the Game, who act as 'proxy researchers'". — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1558689815619824)
- Evans, Collins, Weinel, Lyttleton-Smith, O'Mahoney & Wehrens, "Evaluating the imitation game as a method for comparative research: a replication study using imitation games about religion," *International Journal of Social Research Methodology* 26(3), 249–262 (online Nov. 2021). The design ran religion Imitation Games in several European countries and repeated them about 12 months later. The hypothesis was that "non-Christian members of strongly Christian countries would find it easy to pass as members of the practicing majority". — [T&F record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13645579.2021.1986316); findings summary via search
- IMGAME was an ERC Advanced Grant (269463), 2011–2016, €2,260,083, "A new method for cross-cultural and cross-temporal comparison of societies". Partners were Palermo, Granada, Wroclaw, Budapest, Rotterdam, Cardiff, Uppsala and Aarhus. — [Harry Collins, "The Imitation Game"](https://sites.cardiff.ac.uk/harrycollins/the-imitation-game/)
- A book-length treatment, Collins, Hall, Evans & Weinel, *Imitation Games and Turing Tests* (MIT Press), describes the game as "the human equivalent of the Turing Test", targeting "tacit knowledge and linguistic fluency associated with membership of a particular group". The publisher page lists a publication date of **15 June 2027**, which looks forthcoming or erroneous; treat as unverified. — [PRH/MIT Press listing](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/856657/imitation-games-and-turing-tests-by-harry-collins-martin-hall-robert-evans-and-martin-weinel/)
- Caplan, "The Ideological Turing Test," EconLog, 20 June 2011, replying to Krugman: "Put me and five random liberal social science Ph.D.s in a chat room. Let liberal readers ask questions for an hour, then vote on who isn't really a liberal." Also: "If someone can correctly explain a position but continue to disagree with it, that position is less likely to be correct." — [EconLog](https://www.econlib.org/archives/2011/06/the_ideological.html)
- A known critique exists: Noah Smith, "Against the Ideological Turing Test" (2014). I did not read it. — [Noahpinion](http://noahpinionblog.blogspot.com/2014/01/against-ideological-turing-test.html)

### Inferences
- The Imitation Game turns MacIntyre's "would not be recognized or accepted by speakers … for whom the original language-in-use was their first first language" into a procedure. **Insiders judge; the renderer is anonymized among real insiders; the pass rate is the measure.** An embedding system could attach such a test to any cross-embedding rendering, for example "Tradition A as summarized in embedding B", with judges drawn from A's own embedding and the verdict stored against the revision tested. That would make recognizability by insiders an attribute that is recorded, forkable and version-stamped, rather than the platform's claim.
- There is a real gap between Collins and MacIntyre. Collins's interactional expertise is *linguistic fluency without practice or allegiance*. MacIntyre's second first language is tied to a "language-in-use" embedded in a community's practices, and it ends in a *judgment of truth*, which may be the admission of defeat. Passing an Imitation Game shows one can render a tradition recognizably. It does not show one has seen from inside why it succeeds or fails. The game is necessary for MacIntyre's test but not sufficient.
- Collins's caveats matter for any automated version: banned question types, edited style, the judges' reliance on substance. His "ubiquitous interactional expertise" also suggests that the religious majority–minority case is the *easy* one. Its difficulty depends on how culturally ambient the tradition is, which is the point of the replication study's hypothesis.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the country-by-country results (pass or identification rates) of the religion Imitation Games, or the replication study's conclusions. Only design and hypothesis are confirmed.
- I found no formal validation study of Caplan's ITT, nor any published use of Imitation Games to evaluate *texts* (summaries or encyclopedia entries) rather than live respondents.

## 5. Trading zones, interlanguages and pragmatic boundaries: local coordination without global translation

### Takeaway
Galison's trading zone is the best-developed model of groups coordinating *without* shared meaning: "local coordination, despite vast global differences". It works through contact languages that run from jargon to pidgin to creole. Collins, Evans & Gorman show that interlanguage is only one of four outcomes. The "fractionated" zone keeps the forms of life distinct while sharing a boundary object or a practice language. Carlile adds that where *interests* conflict (a "pragmatic" boundary) knowledge must be *transformed*, not translated, and warns that the layers do not stack like a communication protocol. Zeitlyn (2026) recasts trading zones as "mosaics of thick descriptions, held within thinly described structures".

### Cited Findings
- Galison, *Image and Logic* (1997), as Wikipedia quotes it (p. 783): "Two groups can agree on rules of exchange even if they ascribe utterly different significance to the objects being exchanged; they may even disagree on the meaning of the exchange process itself. Nonetheless, the trading partners can hammer out a local coordination, despite vast global differences." Also: collaborating groups "frequently establish contact languages, systems of discourse that can vary from the most function-specific jargons, through semispecific pidgins, to full-fledged creoles" (p. 783). — [Wikipedia, "Trading zones"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_zones)
- Collins, Evans & Gorman, "Trading zones and interactional expertise," *SHPS A* 38(4), 2007, 657–666, doi:10.1016/j.shpsa.2007.09.003. They classify trading zones on two axes: coercion (voluntary vs. enforced) and outcome (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous culture). That gives four cells: "inter-language" (e.g. biochemistry), "fractionated", "subversive" and "enforced". — [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsa.2007.09.003)
- Collins, Evans & Gorman, "Trading zones revisited" (arXiv preprint, 2017). The term trading zone "was introduced by Peter Galison as a supposed resolution of the problem caused by Kuhn's notion of 'paradigm incommensurability'". They apply it "far more generally to 'forms of life' which vary hugely in scale and are embedded within one another". In the fractionated cell "the parties … rather than attempting a merger, continue to maintain the difference between their forms-of-life even while they cooperate … by … sharing only a fraction of their respective forms-of-life". Methods are "sharing a 'boundary object'" or interactional expertise. They add an "ambassadorial model" and warn that "'boundary object' is often used rather loosely". — [arXiv 1712.06327](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.06327)
- Carlile, "Transferring, Translating, and Transforming: An Integrative Framework for Managing Knowledge Across Boundaries," *Organization Science* 15(5), 2004, 555–568, doi:10.1287/orsc.1040.0094. The abstract names "three progressively complex boundaries—syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic—and three progressively complex processes—transfer, translation, and transformation". — [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.1040.0094)
- Carlile, pp. 559–560. "A focus on pragmatic boundaries recognizes that knowledge is invested in practice and so is 'at stake'." "When different interests arise, developing an adequate common knowledge is a political process of negotiating and defining common interests." "At a pragmatic boundary actors must be able to represent current and more novel forms of knowledge, learn about their consequences, and transform their domain-specific knowledge accordingly." — [Carlile 2004 PDF](https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BA504_Fall%202008/Uploaded%20in%20Nov%202007/Carlile%20(2004).pdf)
- Carlile, p. 560, on layering: the levels "should not be seen like 'layers in a communication protocol' as they are in network computing (i.e., the International Standards Organization (ISO) model). In such a mathematically based view of communication the interfaces between increasingly complex layers can be managed through common standards and algorithmic processing. However, this is what breaks down as we move up to semantic and pragmatic levels". — [same](https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BA504_Fall%202008/Uploaded%20in%20Nov%202007/Carlile%20(2004).pdf)
- Zeitlyn, "Trading Zones Between Thick and Thin: Anthropological Description as Scaffold or Mosaic," *American Anthropologist* 128(1), 2026, 159–170, doi:10.1111/aman.70056. Abstract: "anthropology requires thin description as an essential counterpart for thick description. Thin accounts provide the scaffolding within which thick descriptions sit." Trading zones connect communities "despite their differences (possibly including different ontologies)". The example is "silent trade, in which exchange occurs without direct communication so that there is literally no sharing of meanings!" "Anthropology consists of mosaics of thick descriptions, held within thinly described structures." Galison's "laminated descriptions" are invoked. — [Crossref/Wiley](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aman.70056)

### Inferences
- The fractionated trading zone is the model that fits embeddings best. Forms of life stay separate, and cooperation runs through a shared fraction: anchored passages, revision stamps, perhaps a small relation vocabulary. It does not require a creole. Galison's inter-language outcome, a new common idiom, is what the brief's "ontologies & translations between ontologies" could drift toward. On MacIntyre's terms that drift is exactly where the risk lies: a creole *is* a new third language.
- Zeitlyn states the defensible version of the design directly: **thin scaffolding holding thick mosaics**. The thin part (anchors, stamps, provenance) makes no claim about meaning. The thick parts (each embedding's rendering) never have to agree. The museum objection bites only if the scaffold starts ranking, categorizing or explaining the tiles, i.e. if it brings them "under one and the same aesthetic rubric", in the words of the WJWR passage.
- Carlile's warning bears directly on a stack-shaped design: anchors, then relations, then ontologies, then ontology-to-ontology mappings. At the semantic and pragmatic levels "common standards and algorithmic processing" break down, so mappings between ontologies are **negotiations with stakes**, not interface specifications. Where rival tellings of history carry interests (legitimacy, land, doctrine), the boundary is pragmatic by definition.

### Gaps
- *Image and Logic* page numbers come from Wikipedia (p. 783). Some secondary uses give p. 803 for the same sentence. Not verified against the book.
- I did not read Star & Griesemer 1989 (boundary objects) directly, although it is the canonical source Collins et al. invoke. It is worth adding if boundary objects become a design term.

## 6. Hermeneutics: Gadamer's fusion of horizons, Taylor's "language of perspicuous contrast", MacIntyre's relation to both, and bridge concepts in comparative ethics

### Takeaway
Taylor's "language of perspicuous contrast" is the most explicit *method* for comparing without translating into either side's terms. The adequate language "is not our language of understanding, or theirs". It formulates both ways of life "as alternative possibilities in relation to some human constants at work in both", and it can show *either* side's self-understanding to be distorted. It differs from MacIntyre by allowing a third language that is revisable and produced by the encounter, anchored in "human constants", where MacIntyre's comparisons are always owned by a standpoint. Stalnaker's "bridge concepts" operationalize Taylor-like comparison. Kline's review shows how such bridges quietly carry one tradition's framework.

### Cited Findings
- Taylor, "Understanding and Ethnocentricity," in *Philosophy and the Human Sciences: Philosophical Papers 2* (CUP, 1985), ch. 4. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/philosophical-papers/understanding-and-ethnocentricity/1E4C57A48ADAA1E7C233641369470F98)
- Taylor 1985: 123–125, as block-quoted in Webster's thesis: "The interpretive view, I want to argue, avoids the two equal and opposite mistakes: on one hand, of ignoring self-descriptions altogether, and … on the other hand, of taking these descriptions with ultimate seriousness, so that they become incorrigible. […] But if not in their terms, how else can we understand them but in our own? Aren't we unavoidably committed to ethnocentricity? No, I want to argue, we are not. The error in this view is to hold that the language of cross-cultural theory has to be either theirs or ours. […] while challenging their language of self-understanding, we may also be challenging ours. […] In fact, it will almost always be the case that the adequate language in which we can understand another society is not our language of understanding, or theirs, but rather what one could call a language of perspicuous contrast. This would be a language in which we could formulate both their way of life and ours as alternative possibilities in relation to some human constants at work in both. It would be a language in which the possible human variations would be so formulated that both our form of life and theirs could be perspicuously described as alternative such variations." — [Joseph Webster, *Protestants and Prawns* (PhD, Edinburgh, 2011), quoting Taylor 1985: 123–125](https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/a5995656-9ac9-4a0a-a6d0-480b7188ede9/content)
- Webster applies this in fieldwork, positioning the method against "cultural translation" (Geertz): "rather than viewing anthropology as an act of 'cultural translation' (Geertz 1973), it may be of more help to see it as an effort towards forming … 'a language of perspicuous contrast' (1985:125)". — [Webster 2011](https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/a5995656-9ac9-4a0a-a6d0-480b7188ede9/content)
- Taylor had used the phrase earlier, in "Understanding and Explanation in the Geisteswissenschaften," in Holtzman & Leich (eds.), *To Follow a Rule* (1981), the same volume as McDowell's disentangling paper. Costa cites the page inconsistently ("pp. 128-148, here p. 205"). — [Costa, *Philosophy and Social Criticism* 41(4–5), 2015, 413–422](https://cris.fbk.eu/retrieve/91bb28b5-d5f8-46ae-91e9-c905a6cab24f/Costa-1.pdf)
- Tchir summarizes Taylor: the "incorrigibility thesis" takes the other's self-understanding "with 'ultimate seriousness'" and so rules out any account of a culture as "wrong, confused, or deluded". A language of perspicuous contrast lets us see "the co-existence of contradicting, or 'nonjointly realizable' goods". — [Tchir, "Reconciliation and the Fusion of Horizons" (CPSA 2016)](https://cpsa-acsp.ca/documents/conference/2016/Tchir.pdf)
- Taylor, "Understanding the Other: A Gadamerian View on Conceptual Schemes," in Malpas, Arnswald & Kertscher (eds.), *Gadamer's Century* (MIT Press, 2002), pp. 279–297. Quoted by secondary sources: "The crucial moment … where we allow ourselves to be interpellated by the other; where the difference escapes from its categorization as an error, a fault, or a lesser, undeveloped version of what we are, and challenges us to see it as a viable human alternative." Page not verified. — [NDPR review of *Gadamer's Century*](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/gadamer-s-century-essays-in-honor-of-hans-georg-gadamer/) (pages); quote per search results incl. [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Understanding-the-other-:-A-Gadamerian-view-on-1-Taylor/ed38848c99c6e8ee83eb8d890cd299798cd2f1fd)
- MacIntyre, "On Not Having the Last Word: Thoughts on Our Debts to Gadamer," same volume, pp. 157–172. He asks "What have I learned and perhaps only could have learned from Gadamer?" He regrets that "Gadamer has never entered into dialogue with a distinctively Thomistic Aristotelianism" (157). He argues that Gadamer's better reading of Plato than Natorp's shows progress in philosophy, against a claim of Gadamer's. He calls Gadamer "the exemplary practitioner of the hermeneutic virtues, both intellectual and moral" (171). The same volume holds McDowell, "Gadamer and Davidson on Understanding and Relativism" (173–193). — [NDPR review](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/gadamer-s-century-essays-in-honor-of-hans-georg-gadamer/)
- Stalnaker, *Overcoming Our Evil: Human Nature and Spiritual Exercises in Xunzi and Augustine* (Georgetown UP, 2006). Comparison proceeds through "bridge concepts … [that] provide loose focal points around which comparative engagement can take place". The four are "human nature, spiritual exercises, person, and the will". A search summary gives Stalnaker's definition as "general ideas, such as 'virtue' and 'human nature,' which can be given enough content to be meaningful and guide comparative inquiry yet are open to still greater specification in particular cases". Not verified verbatim. — [T. C. Kline III, NDPR review 2007.03.14](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/overcoming-our-evil-human-nature-and-spiritual-exercises-in-xunzi-and-augustine/)
- Kline's critique: Stalnaker "could have more fully developed the explanation of Xunzi's spiritual exercises … if he had chosen bridge concepts and a structure of analysis that better fit Xunzi's own understanding". He "continues an unfortunate tradition of examining Xunzi in terms of a conceptual framework rooted in the Augustinian tradition, a framework that distorts his analysis". — [Kline, NDPR](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/overcoming-our-evil-human-nature-and-spiritual-exercises-in-xunzi-and-augustine/)

### Inferences
- Taylor's and MacIntyre's positions are the two poles the design must choose between, or support both of.
  - **Taylor**: a third, contrastive language is possible, but it is *made in the encounter*, it is revisable, and it can indict either party. That licenses a shared cross-embedding vocabulary as long as it is (a) authored jointly or contestably, (b) allowed to show *either* side distorted, and (c) anchored in named "human constants" that are themselves open to challenge.
  - **MacIntyre**: every comparison belongs to someone ("Confucian comparisons … with Aristotelian comparisons"). That licenses only embeddings that each contain their own comparative account.
  - A system that makes the contrast-language itself **just another forkable embedding**, which parties can adopt, fork or reject, holds both poles without deciding between them.
- Stalnaker's bridge concepts are a working prototype of "coordinate systems grown into existence later". They are also a warning. Kline shows that the choice of coordinates ("will", "person") carried one side's framework. **Coordinate systems should be attributed to their author's embedding, and a rival embedding should be able to propose alternative coordinates over the same anchors.**
- Taylor's "interpellated" moment, where difference escapes being categorized "as an error, a fault, or a lesser, undeveloped version of what we are", describes something a system can at least avoid preventing. A comparison view whose default categories are one side's ("heresy", "myth", "pre-scientific") makes that moment structurally harder.

## 7. Critiques that any comparative representation is a "view from nowhere" or a museum, and responses (only what adds to the handoff)

### Takeaway
Nagel defines objectivity as a matter of degree: a view is more objective the less it depends on the knower's make-up and position. Haraway's reply is the one most apt against a side-by-side comparison platform. Both totalizing *and* relativist representations are "god tricks", and "relativism is a way of being nowhere while claiming to be everywhere equally". Strathern's *Partial Connections* aims to "get rid of the problem of 'comparison'", replacing it with analogy among parts that "cannot be compared insofar as they are not isomorphic". Together with Viveiros de Castro (in the handoff), these let one argue that a *neutral* comparison layer is itself a position, and that the honest alternative is located, partial, attributed connection.

### Cited Findings
- Nagel, *The View From Nowhere* (1986): "A view or form of thought is more objective than another if it relies less on the specifics of the individual's makeup and position in the world, or on the character of the particular type of creature he is." Also: "The wider the range of subjective types to which a form of understanding is accessible—the less it depends on specific subjective capacities—the more objective it is." Page not verified. — [search result drawing on Wikipedia, "The View from Nowhere"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_View_from_Nowhere)
- Haraway, "Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective," *Feminist Studies* 14(3), 1988, 575–599, doi:10.2307/3178066. The "god trick" of "seeing everything from nowhere" is a "conquering gaze from nowhere" (p. 581, per secondary citation). — [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/3178066); page per [ONCURATING](https://www.on-curating.org/issue-53-reader/Situated-Knowledges-and-Interdependence.html) and related search results
- Haraway: "relativism is a way of being nowhere while claiming to be everywhere equally". The alternative to both relativism and totalization is "partial, locatable, critical knowledges sustaining the possibility of webs of connections called solidarity in politics and shared conversations in epistemology". Page not verified (commonly p. 584). — [search summary; notes on Haraway](http://www.ageofsignificance.org/documents/Haraway.pdf)
- Strathern, *Partial Connections* ([1991] 2004), as Lebner summarizes it. The book focuses on "g[etting] rid of the problem of 'comparison'" (PC xxviii). The cyborg image: it "does not observe any scale, it is neither singular nor plural, neither one nor many, a circuit of connections that joins parts that cannot be compared insofar as they are not isomorphic with one another" (PC 54). Strathern "maintains that translation is a 'fancy' (GOG, 29); one can never render others fully legible, as they 'are', in anthropological language", and deploys "analogy rather than (conventional) comparison". — [Lebner (ed.), *Redescribing Relations* (Berghahn, 2017), Introduction](https://www.berghahnbooks.com/downloads/intros/LebnerRedescribing_intro.pdf)
- MacIntyre's own museum charge, in its earliest form (1985, p. 15), is quoted in §1. It is a "museum of academic culture" whose tour is the Great Books course.

### Inferences
- Haraway sharpens the risk to a precise failure mode. A platform that shows every embedding *side by side, equally, from above* commits the relativist god trick even if it never ranks anything. The mitigation her account suggests is **locatedness in the interface**. Every comparison view is opened *from* some embedding, labeled as such, with that embedding's framings foregrounded. There is no default "view from all embeddings".
- Strathern's parts that "are not isomorphic" argue against requiring that every cross-embedding link be an equivalence or mapping. Links might instead be **partial connections**: "this passage in A bears on that passage in B", with the bearing described in each side's own terms and no claim of a shared category. That is weaker than an ontology mapping and more honest.
- The museum and the trading zone look alike (both hold things from many traditions). They differ in **who acts**. In the museum, curators recontextualize objects for a public that has no framework. In the trading zone, parties who keep their frameworks coordinate for their own purposes. A wiki is a museum if its main use is readers browsing embeddings they belong to none of. It is closer to a trading zone, or MacIntyre's university of constrained disagreement, if the main activity is embeddings writing *about and at each other* with adherents present.

## 8. Indigenous data sovereignty and cultural protocols as institutional refusals of total translatability (brief)

### Takeaway
The CARE Principles and Mukurtu CMS are working institutions built on the premise that not everything should be accessible, or legible, to everyone on the same terms. Christen frames this as "alternative access regimes … based on divergent social and ethical systems", against an openness ideology with a "cultural blindness". They show that a software system *can* encode a tradition's own protocols for who may see and handle what, as governance rather than as translation.

### Cited Findings
- Carroll et al., "The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance," *Data Science Journal* 19: 43 (published 4 Nov. 2020), doi:10.5334/dsj-2020-043. Developed by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group within the Research Data Alliance. The four principles are Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility and Ethics. They "are people– and purpose-oriented" and "complement the existing data-centric approach" of FAIR. The goal: "Be FAIR and CARE." They build on OCAP® and Te Mana Raraunga, among others. — [Data Science Journal](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043)
- CARE aims at "value-based relationships that position data approaches within Indigenous cultures and knowledge systems", and at creating value "in ways that are grounded in Indigenous worldviews". — [Data Science Journal](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043)
- Christen, "Does Information Really Want to be Free? Indigenous Knowledge Systems and the Question of Openness," *International Journal of Communication* 6 (2012): 2870–2893.
  - p. 2878: open-access advocates' "faith in openness as an end in and of itself has distracted them from seeing the possibilities of alternative access regimes that are neither oppressive nor controlling, but based on divergent social and ethical systems and ways of imagining information and its movement between various groups of people".
  - p. 2878: knowledge seen as "assemblages of dynamic modes of making sense of the world that are embedded in cultural, social, and political systems".
  - p. 2885: Mukurtu CMS "recognizes the granular level and socially malleable protocols that drive both access to and the circulation of cultural materials". The Warumungu managers set protocols "based on family and place-based relations".
  - Mukurtu is described as "a free and open source digital archive and content management tool … that can be adapted to the local cultural protocols and dynamic intellectual property needs of any indigenous community".
  - [IJoC PDF](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/viewFile/1618/828)

### Inferences
- The inference that matters for the design: MacIntyre's objection concerns what happens to a tradition's texts when they are *rendered for* outsiders. CARE and Mukurtu show a practical counter-move. The community keeps **authority over how its materials circulate and in what context they are seen**. In an embedding system that could mean per-embedding protocols: which renderings of us by others may be shown inside our embedding, which of our texts may be anchored into by outsiders, and with what framing attached. These protocols are distinct from ordinary visibility permissions.
- Christen's Slashdot episode, where commenters called the protocols "DRM/Censorship", previews the objection any such feature will meet from openness norms. It is worth anticipating.

### Gaps
- I did not examine Local Contexts' Traditional Knowledge (TK) Labels (Anderson & Christen) directly. It is the closest existing *metadata* standard for attaching a community's protocol to an item, and probably the most transferable precedent for anchored passages.
