# Discussion

Follow-ups to the report from the same session, 2026-09-22: Claude's first-person answer to the
MacIntyre question, as revised after reading chapters 17–20 of *Whose Justice? Which
Rationality?* (WJWR), and a discussion of what to call the feature. Page numbers are to the
Notre Dame 1988 edition. The first-cut plan for the feature is design, not research, and is not
recorded here.

## Is an embedding a route out of MacIntyre's predicament?

It can be a route out of the *belief* that everything translates, as far as the system's own
commitments go. It is a route out of the *predicament* only if the people who render one
tradition in another's terms are the bilinguals MacIntyre describes, and the system holds them
answerable to the traditions they render. WJWR names what the feature is reaching for: "some
institutional forum or arena within which the terms of the debate have not already predetermined
its outcome" (pp. 392–393). Its chapters also say what would turn such a forum back into a
museum:

1. **No shared spine.** A layer that admits a sentence only when every perspective approves it
   is the internationalized language, and a gate built from cross-perspective ratings is the
   second level of chapter 17's liberal order: "counting votes, responding to consumer choice,
   surveying public opinion" (p. 343). Power there lies with "those who are able to determine
   what the alternatives are to be" (p. 345), which on a platform means whoever designs the
   schema. Every comparison should open from some embedding, as in MacIntyre's 1991 phrase
   "comparing Confucian comparisons … with Aristotelian comparisons" (quoted secondhand).
2. **Ids and anchors are the stranger's labels.** Pure reference is "the artefact of a
   particular type of social and cultural order" (p. 379), and naming is "naming as" and "naming
   for" (p. 378): of Doire Columcille and Londonderry, "to use either name is to deny the
   legitimacy of the other." Anchors earn their place differently. Traditions fix meaning by
   quotation ("What is x?" is answered by quoting "a line or two from a poem," p. 382), so an
   anchor is how an embedding marks the passages it holds canonical. A shared doc's title is a
   name, so an embedding may need its own name for a shared thing.
3. **Mappings are phrase books** (pp. 381–382). They can record "translation by same-saying";
   "translation by linguistic innovation" (p. 372) happens inside the receiving embedding, as new
   vocabulary in its own texts. And "no counterpart" is dated: what "cannot as yet be said ... at
   that particular time" (p. 372).
4. **Two kinds of rendering.** Describing a rival in one's own terms, with the grounds for
   rejecting it, is the legitimate first stage of controversy (p. 166), and it isn't submitted
   to its subjects: Horace in Hebrew "could only have emerged ... as at once false and
   blasphemous" (p. 380). Insider recognition applies to renderings that claim to speak for a
   tradition, judged by bilinguals (p. 385), and to recharacterizing oneself "from the alien
   perspective of the rival tradition" (p. 167). MacIntyre's own test is one of production: "How
   far can he or she pass as a native?" (p. 375).
5. **Assertion, not report.** Inside an embedding, claims are asserted and a rival may be called
   false. "E held X as of revision N" is truth "assimilated ... to warranted assertibility"
   (p. 384), whereas a truth claim has content "other than even idealized versions of assertions
   of warranted assertibility" (p. 388). Attribution is the outsider's register, and should never
   be the only one a reader is shown.
6. **Not perspectives.** Traditions are "a multiplicity of antagonistic commitments" (p. 368),
   not costumes to move between. Cheap forking without canons of accuracy is the condition in
   which "mistranslation becomes ... increasingly difficult to achieve" (pp. 386–387). What turns
   a perspective into a tradition is continuity (p. 369): re-affirming its members and answering
   its critics over time.
7. **Rival histories decide.** "The rival claims to truth of contending traditions of enquiry
   depend for their vindication upon the adequacy and the explanatory power of the histories
   which the resources of each of those traditions in conflict enable their adherents to write"
   (p. 403). On this account the request's four purposes (schools, tellings of a history,
   framings, a discipline's trajectory) are one activity. The book itself ends at a fork, with
   "at least four alternative ways of continuing" that "no one author could write" (p. 401).
8. **On the handoff's doxa hypothesis.** With no root embedding, "modern cosmopolitan" becomes
   one embedding among others, and its presuppositions become things its rivals can point at.
   That is Bourdieu's move from doxa to orthodoxy, induced on purpose. The cost is on record too:
   "momentary agreements can become programmed commitments" (Ribes & Bowker 2009).

The first answer, given before the book was read, got five things wrong. It said pointing was
neutral (it is the stranger's use, pp. 377–379); it offered attributed mappings as translation
(they are phrase books); it proposed insider recognition for every rendering (it applies only to
some); it recommended attaching provenance to everything (that is the modern register); and it
called the second museum "sealed rooms" where MacIntyre's word is perspectivism.

## What to call it

- **Words to drop:** perspective, viewpoint, lens, frame and stance, which are perspectivist
  (pp. 367–368); account, which collides with user accounts; and scheme, which collides with
  schema.
- **"Commitment"** names what a person has. MacIntyre's usual word for it is "allegiance",
  about 85 uses against 13, as in "constitutive of allegiance to the tradition" (p. 356).
- **"Tradition"** is a status earned through continuity (p. 369). **"Tendency"** is his word
  for a faction within one (p. 348), and fits a fork that has not yet pulled together.
- **"Embedding"** connects directly. Homeric *dikē* "is embedded in and draws part of its
  distinctive character from the larger conceptual scheme in which it is at home" (p. 23).
  Giddens's *disembedding*, "the 'lifting out' of social relations from local contexts of
  interaction," is the museum move, and *re-embedding*, which pins relations "(however partially
  or transitorily) to local conditions of time and place," is what a version-pinned member does
  (*The Consequences of Modernity*, 1990; the wording was checked only through secondary
  sources). The costs: readers think of ML vectors; in mathematics an embedding is a map, not a
  container; and structuralist philosophy of science already uses it as a technical term.
- **Suggested split:** *embedding* for the act and the row (a passage embedded at update N,
  with re-affirming as re-embedding); *school* for the container, which is Kuhn's word for the
  rivals before a paradigm settles (*Structure*, p. 4); *tendency* and *tradition* as statuses;
  and *allegiance* for a person's relation to any of them. Not decided: the first-cut plan kept
  "embedding" for the container.
