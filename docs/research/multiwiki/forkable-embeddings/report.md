# Precedents Cover Each Feature, Never All Four

**Every one of the four features in the proposed "embedding" has a working precedent, most of them built before 2000, and no system in any of the seven literatures surveyed combines them.** Hypertext research built named, swappable link sets laid over documents they did not own (Intermedia's webs and Microcosm's linkbases, 1985–92), contexts that forked, diffed and merged with a recorded basis (Xerox's PIE, 1980; Tektronix's Neptune, 1986), and links pinned either to "a particular version of a node" or to "the 'current' version"; digital humanities built attributed rival definitions (PeriodO), beliefs adopted in part that expire when revised (CIDOC CRMinf) and several community records on one object (Mukurtu); the Semantic Web built attributed, version-pinned mappings that can say "not the same" and "no counterpart" (SSSOM); and textual scholarship built passage-level membership in named witnesses with mature comparison views. Practice showed the same things across fields: forks rarely re-merge (**3.2%** of 220 notable open-source forks; **16.18%** of 15,306 GitHub hard forks ever synchronize), users will not declare structure early and leave typed relations unused, judgments of sameness are contested (three judges rating owl:sameAs links agreed at **κ = 0.158**), and the one tool evaluated for exchanging perspective structures surfaced differences "undetected, sometimes for many years." The unbuilt piece is precise: nothing aligns rival treatments that live in *different articles inside different containers*, because collation tools assume one work with many witnesses and ontology matchers align terms rather than prose. On MacIntyre, the literature cuts both ways, and his own writings supply both edges. His 1985 specification of a neutral "third language" (names detached from any community's descriptions, no canonical texts, foreign texts "supplemented both by paraphrase and by scholarly gloss") reads like a database schema and is the sharpest statement of the museum objection. Chapters 17–20 of *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* carry it into three things the design leans on: pure reference, which he calls "the artefact of a particular type of social and cultural order"; tables of correspondences, which do the work of a phrase book; and uniform attribution, which reports a tradition's claims "in detachment from all substantive criteria and standards of truth and rationality." The same chapters name what the proposal is reaching for, "some institutional forum or arena within which the terms of the debate have not already predetermined its outcome," and say what it would have to hold: each tradition's problematic, its canonical texts, and the rival histories whose "explanatory power" decides between traditions. His 1991 claim that comparative study is really "comparing Confucian comparisons … with Aristotelian comparisons" describes an architecture in which every comparison belongs to a tradition. Walzer's reiterated thin morality, Galison's trading zones and Indigenous data-governance systems add working models of understanding without a neutral idiom, and with MacIntyre's own tests they converge on the conditions that decide which one a system becomes: no comparison from nowhere; embeddings that assert, and may call a rival false, rather than only being reported on; mappings treated as aids rather than as translation; insider recognition applied only to renderings that claim to speak for the insiders; community control over circulation; and adherents writing their rivals' histories at each other rather than outsiders touring.

## Hypertext and heritage systems built each feature separately, mostly before 2000

The separable link set was the mainstream hypertext architecture from about 1985 to 1995, and it covers features (1) and (2) almost exactly. In Brown's Intermedia, "block and link information is not stored within individual documents but is superimposed on them," so that opening a **web** "imposes a particular set of blocks and links on a set of documents while that web is open"; the blocks were selections within a document owned by the web, not by the document ([Yankelovich et al. 1988](http://csis.pace.edu/~marchese/CS835/Lec4/r1081.pdf)). Southampton's Microcosm stored "all information concerning links" in **linkbases**, ran several at once (the documented "common configuration" was the author's linkbase plus one per user), shipped a utility for "merging links from other linkbases," and grouped files and linkbases into **applications** that "may intersect with others or include others" ([Davis et al., CSTR 92-15](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/250713/3/index.html)). Southampton's later FOHM and Auld Linky work generalised the container into context objects that attach to any element of a structure, down to a single binding of a link, and defined a "concept" association whose membership depends on context: "thus the same concept can be seen differently according to the context of the viewer" ([Griffiths et al. 2002](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/256829/1/mis02.pdf)). That is a formal answer, twenty years early, to two people naming an article "institution". None of these sources shows the architecture used for rival interpretations, though. Webs served courses, such as George Landow's "Context 32: A Web of English Literature"; linkbases served author and reader layers; and Intermedia allowed only one web open at a time, so two could never be compared.

The same community built the deepest precedents for features (3) and (4). Xerox PARC's PIE (Goldstein and Bobrow, 1980) structured each **context** as a stack of layers, let users create alternative contexts and "compare them without leaving the design environment," highlighted differences because "there is an explicit model of the differences between contexts," and recorded every merge as a new layer carrying "descriptions … specifying the contexts participating in the merger and the basis for the merger" ([PIE, CSL-81-3](http://worrydream.com/refs/Goldstein%20-%20PIE%20four%20reports.pdf)). It also gave each agent's perspective its own name space, "since one agent cannot know the names chosen by another" (same). Frank Halasz called this the versioning model hypertext should "adopt and improve on," and in 1991 summed up hypertext versioning work since Delisle and Schwartz's Neptune contexts as "Basically, none" ([Halasz 1991](https://www.eastgate.com/hypertext/Halasz/Halasz7Rev.pdf)). Neptune supplied both halves of feature (4): "the link attachment may refer to a particular version of a node or it may always refer to the 'current' version of the node," the second option keeping "a history of link attachment offsets" ([Delisle & Schwartz 1986](https://papers.cumincad.org/data/works/att/31d5.content.pdf)). Whitehead's analysis of the whole line names the design space (pin to a specific revision, float to the latest, or select by label) and notes that "label-based revision selection has the additional benefit of creating internally consistent hypertext structures, assuming the hypertext was consistent when the labels were applied" ([Whitehead 2000](https://ics.uci.edu/~ejw/papers/whitehead_diss.pdf)). In those terms, re-affirming that a newer revision still fits means moving a label forward after a human judgment. The costs were structural: Neptune duplicated every object on each fork, and HyperPro's duplicated links lost their shared identity, which made "do both schools draw this link?" unanswerable (same). All of this work aimed at design alternatives and co-authoring, never at rival schools.

Digital humanities and heritage work built the attributed, plural side. PeriodO treats a period as "a scholarly assertion about the name and spatiotemporal extent of a period," quoted from a source, and groups definitions from one source into an **authority** whose members share nothing "other than sharing a source"; it declines to decide which is right, since period divisions "only need to be comparable," and a search for "early bronze" returns **sixty** rival definitions faceted by time range ([Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/); [PeriodO technical overview](https://perio.do/technical-overview/)). Factoid prosopography at King's College London stores what each source says, so "if two sources contradict each other, two contradictory factoids will be recorded," and keeps the historians' judgment that two records denote one person as a separate assertion ([Pasin & Bradley](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf)). CIDOC CRMinf is the closest data model to forking and re-affirmation. A **Belief** is one actor, possibly a group whose members "may not necessarily be individually convinced of it," holding a proposition set that "could be implemented as a named graph"; another actor adopts "part or all" of it through **Belief Adoption**, justified by "trust in the source … rather than the application of rules"; and a belief "goes out of existence" when any value changes, succeeded by a new belief that cites the old one as premise ([CRMinf v1.0](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf)). The Narrative Ontology holds each narrative as "a separate KB" over "a common set of factual components," anchoring text fragments to events, but its authors "focus on the structure and the operation of single narratives" ([Meghini et al.](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/system/files/swj2539.pdf)); the Simple Event Model scopes individual statements to an authority, as when the Dutch in the East Indies are liberators by one account and occupiers by another ([SEM](https://semanticweb.cs.vu.nl/2009/11/sem/)). For the timelines the proposal anticipates, Topotime already observes that "another scholar's chronology for the same composite event might include an entirely different set of sub-events" ([Grossner & Meeks 2014](https://dh-abstracts.library.virginia.edu/works/2120)). Eventspace let each participant "dynamically construct a custom view and arrangement of the existing nodes" and share it, so that "multiple evolving ontologies are available at any given time for accessing the same set of knowledge" ([Srinivasan & Huang 2005](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/33j8k61m)). Mukurtu gives one media asset several community records whose "metadata is completely independent," each under its own cultural protocol and "displayed together," against what Kimberly Christen calls other systems' reliance on "the one authority record" ([Mukurtu](https://mukurtu.org/support/how-to-create-community-records/); [NEH](https://www.neh.gov/article/mukurtu-digital-platform-does-more-manage-content)).

Textual scholarship and software-variation research supply passage-level membership in several containers at once. TEI's critical apparatus assigns each reading to a set of witnesses (`<rdg wit="#A #C">`), and its parallel-segmentation method lets any span in one witness be compared with any other, on the condition that "no two variations can overlap, although they may nest" ([TEI P5, ch. 13](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/TC.html)); Schmidt's multi-version documents store fragments "each of which is assigned a set of versions" ([Schmidt 2008](http://multiversiondocs.blogspot.com/2008/03/whats-multi-version-document.html)). Erwig and Walkingshaw's choice calculus reached the same structure independently for code, calling branches "a highly redundant representation" and selecting every choice tagged with a named **dimension** together ([TOSEM 2011](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~erwig/papers/ChoiceCalculus_TOSEM11.pdf)), and projectional editing adds the safety rule that "edits affect only the variants that are visible in the view" ([Walkingshaw & Ostermann 2014](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~walkiner/papers/gpce14-projectional-editing.pdf)). Every survey returned the same negative result, with the caveat that each rests on web searches rather than a systematic database review. No hypertext paper describes rival scholarly schools represented as separate link sets over one corpus and then compared. No DH or heritage system found implements forkable, versioned, named containers holding both entities and relations, and none re-affirms membership against a changed source, because every source found was immutable apart from PeriodO's curated records. No knowledge-representation system pins passage-level membership to the revision log of a collaboratively edited document, no CSCW or CHI paper from 2015–2026 builds such a platform, and ClaiMaker's designers listed "investigating the modelling of complex constructs such as 'school of thought'" as future work that never shipped ([Mancini & Buckingham Shum 2006](https://kmi.open.ac.uk/publications/pdf/kmi-06-14.pdf)). The gap is sharpest in comparison. Collation tools align witnesses of one work, where the text itself is the coordinate system, and ontology matchers align terms, so nothing aligns rival treatments that sit in different articles inside different containers ([CollateX documentation](https://collatex.net/doc/)).

The difference between an embedding and a tag also has explicit precedent. Halasz complained in 1991 that the hypermedia model lacked "a way of representing and dealing with groups of nodes and links as unique entities separate from their individual components," and wanted part-of relations "distinct from the standard kind of reference links" ([Halasz 1991](https://www.eastgate.com/hypertext/Halasz/Halasz7Rev.pdf)). Parunak's HyperSet sits at the tag end of the spectrum, where an analyst thinks of "including it in or excluding it from a set of related nodes," and even he argued for a hybrid supporting "both set and graph manipulations" ([Parunak 1991](https://doi.org/10.1145/122974.122998)). PeriodO separates a collection that merely shares a source from a periodization, "a single coherent, continuous division of historical time," which needs added statements relating the definitions ([Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/)). Heritage practice shows why membership alone fails: Zuni reviewers corrected what *kind* of thing objects were (a catalogued "Hopi blanket" was a Zuni ceremonial shawl) ([AMNH 2011](https://web.archive.org/web/2023id_/https://www.amnh.org/explore/news-blogs/news-posts/zuni-museum-enlists-amnh)), and Warumungu protocols exist because archived ritual objects had been "disconnected from the practices, people, and places that they need to be efficacious" ([Christen 2012](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/1618/828)). A tag can record that an object belongs; only relations can record what it belongs *as*.

## Forks almost never re-merge, so the value has to come from comparison

Software engineering has measured "forking too easy, comparing too hard" at scale, and the numbers say divergence is permanent by default. Robles and González-Barahona found that **3.2%** of 220 notable forks merged back, and Zhou, Vasilescu and Kästner classified 15,306 hard forks on GitHub and found that "only **16.18%** of all hard forks with active upstream repositories ever synchronize or merge," even though "many interviewees expressed intentions" to ([Zhou et al., ICSE 2020](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)). The interviewees blamed the tools: monitoring other repositories "is onerous," GitHub's network graph is "difficult to use and does not scale," and "current tools work well for short-term social forks but tend to work less well for coordinating changes across repositories that have diverged more significantly" (same). The Marlin firmware project was forked 1,588 times in three years, more than half its commits originated in forks, and the result was "large amounts of information in an unorganized space, important bug-fixes are not propagated, and occasionally the same functionality is developed more than once" ([Stănciulescu et al. 2015](https://stefanstanciulescu.com/papers/2015-icsme-stanciulescu.pdf)). Industry keeps cloning anyway, "due to its benefits such as simplicity, availability and independence of developers" ([Dubinsky et al. 2013](https://gsd.uwaterloo.ca/sites/default/files/2013-csmr-cloning.pdf)), and the costs listed in "What the Fork" ("lost contributions," "redundant development," "fragmented communities") are the prose problem stated for code ([Zhou et al. 2019](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/fse19_forks.pdf)). Forking itself is not the disease. Nyman and Lindman call the possibility of forking "the 'invisible hand of sustainability'" and find that forks driven by different requirements rather than distrust "can prosper" ([Nyman & Lindman 2013](https://timreview.ca/article/644)). The missing piece is a view of the whole family, which is why Zhou et al. end by calling for tools that can "monitor, manage, and integrate changes across a family of hard forks."

How forks arise bears directly on the proposal's "peel away" observation. Hard forks on GitHub "seem to be triggered by rather small initial changes," grow gradually out of casual forks, and only about **36%** ever rename themselves ([Zhou et al. 2020](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse20-forks.pdf)), so a fork's identity forms long before anyone names it. Ethnography adds what goes wrong socially. In Charlotte Lee's year with a museum exhibition team, rival "structuring artifacts … often competed with each other for primacy"; the educators' narrative "was derived from the curator's narrative" and "quickly supplanted" it, and one curator "was stunned to discover that the museum staff seemed to be removing and changing whole concepts" ([Lee 2007](https://depts.washington.edu/csclab/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/Lee-2007.pdf)). A fork notification is the feature that would have prevented that invisible replacement. The International Classification of Diseases shows a governance answer: each revision sets off "an immediate efflorescence of modifications," so the committee issued rules for modifying the list, with "an algorithm for working back from the modified list to the ICD itself. Recoverability, while expensive, is theoretically possible and the open history is maintained in part" ([Bowker & Star 1999](https://hcommons.org/app/uploads/sites/1001532/2020/03/Bowker-1999-Sorting-Things-Out-Classification-and-Its-Consequences.pdf)). Software research formalized the same ladder as the "virtual platform," which runs from ad hoc copying (level 0) through "clone-and-own with provenance," in which "teams record provenance information about the original projects and per cloned asset" (level 1), to a fully integrated platform (level 6) ([Antkiewicz et al. 2014](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/~bergert/paper/2014-icsenier-virtualplatform.pdf)). Level 1 is feature (4) plus a fork edge.

Several fields built comparison machinery, and each version shows where the hard part lies. PIE highlighted differences between contexts, and CoVer shipped three merge tools plus a "task view" that Whitehead calls "a significant innovation, since it provides a visualization of just composite evolution" ([Whitehead 2000](https://ics.uci.edu/~ejw/papers/whitehead_diss.pdf)). Version control for RDF knowledge graphs branches and merges them, but its three-way merge never produces a syntactic conflict, so "even though the result is a valid RDF graph two statements could contradict each other," as when one branch asserts Obama as president and the other Trump ([Arndt et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.03721)). Merging by set arithmetic is free, and a conflict of meaning is invisible to it. INFOX attacks scattering directly. It does not use commits, which "tangle code of multiple features" while "a single feature is scattered across multiple commits," but builds a dependency graph over every changed line between a fork and its upstream, clusters the lines by community detection and labels each cluster with keywords, identifying features "with a median accuracy of 90 percent" across ten projects ([Zhou et al., ICSE 2018](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/pdf/icse18forks.pdf)). Applied to prose, it would cluster the passage changes an embedding made across several articles into candidate themes; the dependency signal in prose is weaker than in code, so that is an untested bet. Textual scholarship has the most mature pipeline, the Gothenburg model's tokenise, normalise, align, analyse and visualise, with variant graphs that store shared text once and branch where witnesses diverge ([CollateX](https://collatex.net/doc/)) and views such as Juxta's heat map, synchronized side-by-side and histogram ([Juxta Commons](https://journalofdigitalhumanities.org/3-1/juxta-commons/)). Its admitted weak points are transpositions, still "a problematic case," and results that depend on the order in which witnesses are merged. Its separation of normalisation (which differences are trivial) from alignment (which passages correspond) is a structural form of "names are not synonyms." Delta-oriented programming, which models a variant as a core plus named deltas that can add *and remove* content ([Schaefer et al. 2010](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Delta-Oriented-Programming-of-Software-Product-Schaefer-Bettini/205b2d86bf8d1b35d839beb395dd8c5c2bb5eddc)), matters because a school of thought may delete a claim, not only add one.

Research in CSCW and digital humanities says what shape a comparison should take. Star and Griesemer's "coincident boundaries" are "common objects which have the same boundaries but different internal contents," letting work "be conducted autonomously while cooperating parties share a common referent"; their example is California, whose outline both amateurs' and biologists' maps shared ([Star & Griesemer 1989](https://lchc.ucsd.edu/mca/Mail/xmcamail.2012_09.dir/pdfuaCxVBhVe5.pdf)). The comparison substrate is a common outline, not a common interpretation. Heterochronologies does the same for time, aligning rival chronologies "around chronological events that they have in common" by "floating correlation," so that "the Winter Counts do not have to take the birth of Christ as the start point" ([Drucker et al. 2022](https://digitalhumanities.org/dhq/vol/16/3/000624/000624.html)). Boland and Tenkasi, quoting Dougherty, warn against assuming the pieces add up, since rival accounts are not blind men feeling an elephant but "eye witnesses at an accident … each tells us a 'complete' story, but tells a different one" ([Boland & Tenkasi 1996](https://ceo.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/1996/11/1996-14-g96_14-Distributed_Cognition.pdf)); the whole embedding, not the single claim, is the unit of comparison. And field studies close off the shortcut of merging. Large efforts to integrate shared information spaces "inherently tend to reproduce fragmentation as an unintended consequence" ([Rolland et al. 2006](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1180875.1180951)), and coordination practices across boundaries produced "suppression of difference, loss of comprehension" ([Kellogg et al. 2006](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v17y2006i1p22-44.html)).

Carlile's escalating boundaries tell a comparison view which kind of difference it faces. At a *syntactic* boundary a shared vocabulary lets knowledge be transferred; at a *semantic* boundary, where the same words carry different meanings, it must be translated; at a *pragmatic* boundary, where "knowledge is invested in practice and so is 'at stake,'" building common knowledge "is a political process of negotiating and defining common interests," and knowledge must be transformed ([Carlile 2004](https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BA504_Fall%202008/Uploaded%20in%20Nov%202007/Carlile%20(2004).pdf)). He warns that these levels should not be seen like "layers in a communication protocol," because "common standards and algorithmic processing" are exactly what "breaks down as we move up to semantic and pragmatic levels" (same). A stack of anchors, then relations, then ontologies, then mappings between ontologies is the protocol picture he rejects for the upper two levels.

## Structure arrives late, and users refuse to declare it early

The proposal expects differences to "peel away" in a disorganized way and "pull together" later, and hypertext research gives the strongest evidence that people build structure this way whether or not a tool asks. Aquanet, built at Xerox PARC for Toulmin-style analysis, was assessed over two years that produced "almost 2000 nodes and more than 20 representational types," yet its "relations [were] less commonly used than the tool's design anticipated, and the structures users built [were] more volatile than expected." Users expressed relationships through spatial layout instead, and regions that "emerged" were later reused as the basis for another collaboration ([Marshall & Rogers 1992](http://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://www.csdl.tamu.edu/~shipman/viki/papers/echt92/echt92.html)). The team abandoned a "Concepts" type created early and declined to restructure another because "we had already integrated a significant amount of source material … and did not relish the thought of moving it" (same). Shipman and Marshall's synthesis, "Formality Considered Harmful," names four reasons users reject formal structure ("cognitive overhead, tacit knowledge, premature structure, and situational structure") and records NoteCards analysts for whom "typed links -- the strongest formalization mechanism provided -- were rarely used, and when they were, they were seldom used consistently." Their users' own questions state the proposal's problem from the other side: "What if I commit to this formalization only to later find out it is wrong?" and "Why should I formalize this when I cannot agree with anyone else on what the formalization should be?" ([Shipman & Marshall 1999, preprint](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/shipman/viki/papers/tochi/tochi.html)). Trigg's rich taxonomy of link types (Refutation, AlternateView, isSupersededBy) met the same fate, and "many hypertext system designers abandoned typed links completely" ([Bernstein on Trigg](https://www.eastgate.com/HypertextNow/archives/Trigg.html)).

The same thing happened wherever a system offered explicit fields for uncertainty or disagreement. In the GEON earth-science project, representing uncertainty was "a promised capability of ontologies; however, … we never saw it employed in practice" ([Ribes & Bowker 2009](https://ics.uci.edu/~gbowker/publications/Ribes%20Bowker%20-%20Between%20Meaning%20and%20Machine.pdf)). PeriodO built an uncertainty notation and dropped it as "overly complex," because "in almost every single case that we observed, authors did not explicitly state a precise level of uncertainty" ([Golden & Shaw 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/2017id_/https://peerj.com/articles/cs-44/)). The Clergy of the Church of England Database added certainty codes that users saw but that were "not used to enhance the search algorithms" ([Pasin & Bradley](https://lambdamusic.github.io/support.michelepasin.org/public_articles/2014-Pasin-Bradley.pdf)). The discourse-graph systems that modelled scholarly claims with typed relations (ScholOnto, SWAN at Alzforum, micropublications in Domeo) "are no longer active, and the impact of these deployments has not been empirically evaluated," with "some consensus that these problems center on the 'human' side of the infrastructure such as tools and incentives for authors"; their successor deliberately formalizes incrementally, after the fact ([Chan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20666)). The largest survivor, the Open Research Knowledge Graph, held **4,241 comparisons against 68,469 papers** on 22 September 2026 ([ORKG statistics](https://orkg.org/api/statistics/content-types/comparison-count)), and its first quality assessment examined six comparisons ([D'Souza et al. 2024](https://www.jlis.it/index.php/jlis/article/view/547)). Curation at this grain is expensive even with institutional backing.

Making structure explicit is nonetheless where the discoveries happen. When GEON's geoscientists tried to encode their field, "what had appeared to be a shared epistemic umbrella – 'the geosciences' – began to break down into a finely grained mapping of disciplinary differences," and beliefs "held unproblematically" before turned into "endless nuanced disagreements" once written down, at the cost that "momentary agreements can become programmed commitments" ([Ribes & Bowker 2009](https://ics.uci.edu/~gbowker/publications/Ribes%20Bowker%20-%20Between%20Meaning%20and%20Machine.pdf)). Boland and Tenkasi's Spider, the only tool in this line evaluated with users (qualitatively), had managers draw their perspective as a directed graph of factors and causal relations and then exchange maps: "discussing one's map with others almost always raises awareness of differences in perspectives that had gone undetected, sometimes for many years." The maps were built "by telling a story about a dramatic incident," and the authors conclude that "communication is in these dialogues, not in the map as a transmitted message" ([Boland & Tenkasi 1996](https://ceo.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/1996/11/1996-14-g96_14-Distributed_Cognition.pdf)). For authoring, the lesson is that structure should be cheap to start, narrative first and allowed to firm up later; for comparison, the payoff is surfacing differences nobody knew they had.

Several theories describe the peel-away-then-pull-together path. Lee's boundary negotiating artifacts move from private "self-explanation" notes, to "inclusion" proposals put to a group, to competing "structuring" artifacts ([Lee 2007](https://depts.washington.edu/csclab/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/Lee-2007.pdf)). Chen and Barker argue that "when concepts are represented by frames, the transformation from one taxonomy to another can be achieved in a piecemeal fashion not preconditioned by a crisis stage" ([Chen & Barker 2000](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/abs/continuity-through-revolutions-a-framebased-account-of-conceptual-change-during-scientific-revolutions/1262C51817AC751853A4028DB3FCFEF7)). Thagard's nine degrees of conceptual change run from cumulative additions (instances, rules, part and kind relations, new concepts) to reorganizations by "branch jumping" and "tree switching" ([Thagard 2006](http://cogsci.uwaterloo.ca/Articles/conc.change.life.pdf)). Fleck saw scattered, tentative "journal science" consolidate into handbook ("vademecum") science, and treated a change of thought style as collective and unintended ([SEP: Fleck](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fleck/)). Kuhn insisted incommensurability had always been "local," restricting change "to a few, typically interconnected concepts" ([SEP: Incommensurability](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incommensurability/)), which suggests that the scattered divergences likely to pull together are the ones already linked to each other. MacIntyre adds that a pull-together is legible only afterwards. Physics was in crisis between Boltzmann and Bohr although few physicists thought so, because "an epistemological crisis may only be recognized for what it was in retrospect," and passing through one "enables the adherents of a tradition of enquiry to rewrite its history in a more insightful way" (MacIntyre, *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?*, University of Notre Dame Press, 1988, p. 363; hereafter WJWR, cited from the book itself). The scattered differences become a crisis only in the history written after it is resolved.

Tools for recognizing a pull-together exist, and every one treats its output as a suggestion. Marshall and Shipman built heuristic recognizers for implicit structure in spatial hypertext and concluded that recognition "is feasible" but that "interaction will be important in guiding such recognition" ([Marshall & Shipman 1993](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/shipman/viki/papers/ht93/ht93.html)); Shipman and McCall's Hyper-Object Substrate let users enter information informally and accept system-suggested structure later ([Shipman & McCall 1994](https://doi.org/10.1145/191666.191768)). Benn and colleagues reduced a debate's supports, disputes and undercuts to a single agreement relation, clustered the result, stored each cluster as a `ViewpointCluster`, and counted two clusters as opposing "if at least half of the viewpoints in one cluster have a 'disputes' relation with the viewpoints in the other"; on abortion the algorithm settled on five clusters, with the stated aim "not to find the definitive clustering arrangement, but rather to detect interesting phenomena" ([Benn et al., KMI-08-02](https://kmi.open.ac.uk/publications/pdf/kmi-08-02.pdf)). Stemmatology groups manuscripts by variants that co-occur across many passages, so scattered differences count as evidence of a family only in aggregate ([Andrews & Macé 2013](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article-abstract/28/4/504/1076624)). Citation and link networks find paradigm-like modules and turning points ([Ju et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08381)), but co-citation links "have no semantics" ([Mancini & Buckingham Shum 2006](https://kmi.open.ac.uk/publications/pdf/kmi-06-14.pdf)), and a cluster is not a school until someone names what it holds in common. No precedent joins machine-suggested clusters to schools whose identity users author and which persist across versions.

Only one experiment has tested whether reading one corpus through several named frames changes what readers can do, and it comes from Cognitive Flexibility Theory. Jacobson and Spiro had 34 students study cases cut into "minicases," each "criss-crossed" under several thematic perspectives with a commentary per case; the control group did better on "memory" for facts, the criss-crossing group on knowledge "transfer," and the criss-crossing group also studied longer, 125 against 83 minutes on average ([Jacobson & Spiro 1993](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED355508.pdf)). The study is small, old and framed by its designers, but it is the nearest evaluated analogue to one passage assigned to several embeddings, each with its own commentary.

## Sameness is a recorded judgment, never a matching name

"Names are not synonyms" is documented in every field surveyed, usually with a worked example. MacIntyre opened his 1985 APA presidential address with it: to speak of Doire Colmcille in Irish and of Londonderry in English is not to use two names for one place, because "no proper name of place or person names any place or person as such; it names in the first instance only for those who are members of some particular linguistic and cultural community" ([MacIntyre 1985, p. 7](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)). Bowker and Star make the point for counting: "If 'injection' means giving medication by needle in one country and by suppositories in another, for example, there is no use trying to count the number of injections given worldwide until some equivalence is reached by negotiation" ([Bowker & Star 1999](https://hcommons.org/app/uploads/sites/1001532/2020/03/Bowker-1999-Sorting-Things-Out-Classification-and-Its-Consequences.pdf)). Millerand and Bowker found communities for whom "casualty" means "someone injured or killed" and others for whom it is "simply a euphemism for 'fatality'" ([Millerand & Bowker 2009](https://ics.uci.edu/~gbowker/metadataeng.pdf)). GEON split over Proterozoic versus Algonkian, terms that "refer approximately to the same period" but carry "differing importance within geoscientific institutions and subdisciplines," and a hydrology workshop over such questions "broke down altogether" ([Ribes & Bowker 2009](https://ics.uci.edu/~gbowker/publications/Ribes%20Bowker%20-%20Between%20Meaning%20and%20Machine.pdf)). DBpedia's sodium is linked by owl:sameAs to OpenCyc's sodium, although one includes all isotopes and the other is "the set of all pieces of the pure element"; three of the paper's authors rated 250 sameAs pairs and agreed at **κ = 0.158**, "considered 'poor' agreement" ([Halpin et al. 2010](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=7f5a8c4cebd05eb8b503aef7d9e73e87d37c90c3)). ClaiMaker's designers observed that "ontology" means one thing to a philosopher and "something rather different" to a Semantic Web researcher ([Mancini & Buckingham Shum 2006](https://kmi.open.ac.uk/publications/pdf/kmi-06-14.pdf)), and SEM's lead example types the same territory as "an independent Republic according to the Indonesians" and a "controlled region" according to the Dutch ([SEM](https://semanticweb.cs.vu.nl/2009/11/sem/)). Rival tellings mostly differ in how they classify shared entities, not in which events they include.

WJWR adds two refinements. Sameness of subject matter is itself judged within each tradition, since "each of course may have its own peculiar standards by which to judge what is to be accounted one and the same in the relevant respect," yet genuine disagreement requires that "at some level of characterization" both sides "can recognize that it is one and the same subject matter about which they are making claims" (WJWR, p. 351). A sameness claim asserted from one side records one tradition's reading; the same claim endorsed from both sides is what turns talking past each other into a disagreement. And in other areas what one tradition asserts "may have no counterpart whatsoever" in another (p. 350). Sameness across traditions is also historical, not only structural: chains such as "velle …, voluntas, voluntary; auctoritas …, authority, author; … dikaiosunē, ius, iustitia, justice" are "strings which can be read as aphoristic versions of such histories" (p. 383), each link forged by a translator at a particular time.

Every formal model of conceptual change in philosophy of science makes identity structural. Thagard locates a concept by its kind and part relations ([Thagard 2006](http://cogsci.uwaterloo.ca/Articles/conc.change.life.pdf)); Andersen, Barker and Chen compare concepts as frames, attribute by attribute ([Botterill, NDPR](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/the-cognitive-structure-of-scientific-revolutions/)); Gärdenfors and Zenker locate them in conceptual spaces of dimensions with metrics and weights ([Gärdenfors & Zenker 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-011-0060-0)); and Votsis and Schurz used frames to test how much of the caloric theory of heat survived into the kinetic theory ([Votsis & Schurz 2012](https://votsis.org/PDF/Votsis_Schurz_A_Frame-Theoretic_Analysis_of_Two_Rival_Conceptions_of_Heat.pdf)). Kuhn's late no-overlap principle adds a hard constraint: kind terms may not overlap in their referents "unless they are related as species to genus," so a revision that re-divides kinds produces a taxonomy that cannot be merged with its predecessor, and in Hacking's gloss "distinct lexicons covering overlapping subject matters cannot be merged" ([SEP: Kuhn](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thomas-kuhn/)). That is an argument, not just a preference, for keeping rival schools in separate containers joined by partial translation rather than folding them into one taxonomy of tags. The Indiana Philosophy Ontology shows the alternative: it reconciles expert feedback into one taxonomy by automated reasoning weighted by expertise ([Niepert et al. 2009](https://bulletin.jcdl.org/Bulletin/v5n1/Niepert/niepert.html)), so wherever experts' distinctions conflict, one side's must be dropped.

Systems that treat synonymy as structural already exist on a small scale. Micropublications group claims from separate citation lineages into "Similarity groups" around a curator-chosen "holotype," normalizing wording "without dropping necessary qualifiers and hedging" ([Clark et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.3506)). ClaiMaker separated shared base relation classes from each field's "dialect" labels, so vocabulary could vary without breaking comparison ([Mancini & Buckingham Shum 2006](https://kmi.open.ac.uk/publications/pdf/kmi-06-14.pdf)), and PIE, as noted, kept each perspective's attribute names independent. E-connections go furthest formally: ontologies may be combined only "provided that the domains of the ontologies are disjoint," so no individual is ever identical across components and only explicit link properties connect them ([Cuenca Grau et al. 2006](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/4568/Econn.pdf)).

The mapping formalisms differ mainly in what they let a correspondence record, and the proposal's "translations between ontologies" needs the fullest version.

SSSOM is the nearest existing analogue to a translation layer between embeddings. A mapping carries its author, reviewer and confidence plus a mandatory justification ("there can be no mapping without some form of justification"); `predicate_modifier: Not` states that two terms are explicitly *not* a match; `sssom:NoTermFound` states that a term "cannot be mapped to any entity" in the other source; and `subject_source_version` and `object_source_version` pin each side's version, preventing what the FAQ calls "non-transparent incompleteness," not knowing "on the basis of what version of the terminological source" a mapping was made ([SSSOM spec](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/spec-model/); [FAQ](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/faq/)). Its chaining rules refuse to compose closeMatch or relatedMatch, and label any chained mapping "structural," something that "should not be confused with proper reasoning" ([chaining rules](https://mapping-commons.github.io/sssom/dev/chaining-rules/)). Lenticular Lenses add the missing element of context: links are generated with metadata on "how, why, when and by whom," a user selects those suited to a task into a named lens, and lenses combine by "union, intersection, difference and composition" ([Idrissou et al. 2017](https://www.cs.vu.nl/~frankh/postscript/KCAP2017.pdf)). Multi-Context Systems contribute the only knowledge-representation mechanism that *compares*: a diagnosis finds the minimal set of bridge rules that, switched off or forced, restore consistency ([Eiter et al. 2010](https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/1265/1265-7422-1-PB.pdf)), which in effect locates the translation assertions doing the disagreeing. Nobody has applied any of these to passages of prose. Treating an embedding's translation layer as an SSSOM set whose "sources" are embeddings pinned to revisions is a design inference, not a precedent.

WJWR marks the limits of any such layer. A table of correspondences is what MacIntyre calls a phrase book, matching sentence with sentence and context, for which "the measure of success in the matching is therefore pragmatic"; the "badge of elementary linguistic competence" is instead "knowing how to go on and go further," which "you cannot learn how to say from phrase books or indeed from any recursively enumerable set of individual English sentences" (WJWR, pp. 381–382). He distinguishes "translation by same-saying" from "translation by linguistic innovation," as when the Septuagint's translators transformed Greek and Cicero neologized Latin (p. 372), and observes that a tradition "may have had to enrich itself significantly in order to be able to provide a representation of some of the characteristic positions of the other" (p. 370). A mapping can record same-saying; innovation happens inside the receiving tradition, as new vocabulary in its own texts, and would show up as edits within an embedding rather than as rows between two. Untranslatability, finally, is dated: a tradition entering a new language must identify "what it is which cannot as yet be said in the new language, which is so far and at that particular time untranslatable" (p. 372), so a "no counterpart" record is a claim about a revision, not a permanent fact. Where rival traditions share "texts, modes of evaluation, whole practices," translation "will generally be able to proceed almost entirely by same-saying" (p. 387), which is where a mapping layer earns its keep.

Coordinate systems grown after the fact have a practice record, and it favours agreements that are scoped and provisional. GEON's participants found four ways to "go on": drop to a coarser level where consensus is stronger (Precambrian instead of Proterozoic or Algonkian); a "working agreement" relative to "the 'science questions' geologists would like to address, rather than to a more abstract goal of serving the entire community"; a "rain-check"; and representing the uncertainty, which was never used. Where GEON did encode plurality, it did so structurally, since "two representational schemas for color legends were included in the ontology, allowing users to choose their convention of preference" ([Ribes & Bowker 2009](https://ics.uci.edu/~gbowker/publications/Ribes%20Bowker%20-%20Between%20Meaning%20and%20Machine.pdf)). That is two embeddings, not an uncertainty field. Galison's trading zones grow contact languages "from the most function-specific jargons, through semispecific pidgins, to full-fledged creoles" ([Wikipedia: Trading zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_zones)), and Collins, Evans and Gorman distinguish zones that merge into an "inter-language," with biochemistry the paradigm case, from "fractionated" zones whose parties "continue to maintain the difference between their forms-of-life even while they cooperate … sharing only a fraction of their respective forms-of-life" ([Collins, Evans & Gorman 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.06327)). The SKOS working group drew only a weak line between mappings and relations inside one scheme, because a strong line "depended, conceptually, on the ability to 'contain' a concept scheme," which it put out of scope, and it noted that as schemes evolve, related concepts in two schemes "could become aggregated into the same scheme, or vice versa" ([Baker et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.1224)). Bowker and Star record the price of staying comparable over time: ICD revisions minimized change "so that statistics based on the successive Lists should be as comparable as possible," which led to "the preservation of anachronistic categories" ([Bowker & Star 1999](https://hcommons.org/app/uploads/sites/1001532/2020/03/Bowker-1999-Sorting-Things-Out-Classification-and-Its-Consequences.pdf)). In none of these cases was the judgment of sameness computed. OntoView's authors state flatly that whether a change is conceptual or only a change in how the concept is specified "is not possible to determine automatically" ([Klein et al. 2002](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/29675824/EKAW02.pdf)), and TEI still calls transposed passages "harder to encode" ([TEI P5, ch. 13](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-doc/en/html/TC.html)).

## Re-affirmation and paradigm change both reduce to continuity tests

Feature (4) descends directly from Semantic Web ontology versioning, and the lineage's first lesson concerns authority. SHOE (Heflin and Hendler, 2000) let a data source pin the exact ontology version it committed to (`USE-ONTOLOGY … VERSION="1.0"`) and let a revision declare itself `BACKWARD-COMPATIBLE-WITH` an earlier one, so agents could use it in place of the old ([Heflin & Hendler 2000](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2000/AAAI00-068.pdf)). The authors saw the hijack at once: anyone could publish a revision of "a popular ontology that is owned by somebody else" and declare it compatible, and "unless there is a mechanism to determine if a revision is official," agents would substitute it. Their remedies were to accept only revisions that add, to require the same server path, or to have the original ontology authorize the revision with a `REVISED-BY` tag (same). OWL 2 kept `priorVersion`, `backwardCompatibleWith` and `incompatibleWith`, but only as annotations, which "have no effect on the logical aspects of an ontology" ([OWL 2](https://www.w3.org/TR/owl2-syntax/)). Klein and colleagues moved the judgment down to the individual concept and made it human: OntoView lets users "specify the conceptual relation between the different versions of concepts," distinguishing a "conceptual change," which gets a new identifier, from an "explication change," and the distinction is "basically a decision of the ontology engineer" ([Klein et al. 2002](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/29675824/EKAW02.pdf)). CRMinf states the same idea as a lifecycle, in which a belief ends when any of its values changes and the new belief "would use the original instance of I2 Belief as a premise" ([CRMinf v1.0](https://cidoc-crm.org/crminf/sites/default/files/CRMinf%20v1.0%28site%29.pdf)), so re-affirmation is a new, dated, attributable act rather than an edit to the old one. Nanopublications supply the storage discipline ("you can never edit nor delete it. You can however retract") ([nanopub docs](https://nanopub.readthedocs.io/en/latest/publishing/retraction.html)), and Robust Links the citation form, a `data-versiondate` giving "the date of the state of the resource that the linker wants the link visitor to be able to experience" ([Robust Links](https://mementoweb.org/robustlinks/spec/)). Bannon and Bødker's account of common information spaces explains why pinning helps comparison: such a space is "both open and closed," malleable inside a community but packaged and closed wherever it must travel between communities ([Bannon & Bødker 1997](https://dl.eusset.eu/bitstreams/2e165755-c4d2-4765-934a-0cbd66b527a6/download)), and the pinned revision is that closure. Variation-control research now treats revisions (change over time) and variants (parallel versions) as one model with a "feature revision" combining both ([Ananieva et al. 2022](https://pure.tue.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/218415271/s10664_021_10097_z.pdf)), which is the pairing of an embedding with a `ydoc_update_id`.

Hypertext supplies the notification half and shows its limits. Microcosm stamped every link with the dates of its source and destination documents and warned the user when they no longer matched, a solution its authors judged "keeps the user aware of any inconsistencies, but makes no attempt to fix them" ([CSTR 92-15](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/250713/3/index.html)). Walden's Paths built a Path Manager to help a curator discover "when relevant changes occur to linked resources," after a study in which users "indicated a strong desire to be notified of structural changes" ([Francisco-Revilla et al. 2001](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED459818); [Hypertext '01](https://doi.org/10.1145/504216.504266)). Østerbye identified naming new versions and choosing among them as major sources of mental overhead, along with making authors "decide explicitly for every update whether it should result in a new revision" ([Whitehead 2000](https://ics.uci.edu/~ejw/papers/whitehead_diss.pdf)). None of these systems stored the human's verdict as a first-class, attributable record, and every DH precedent found pins to an immutable source (a published book or a release), so re-affirmation against a live, collaboratively edited document has no precedent.

Philosophy of science offers the most developed model of a structured whole that evolves, and it was built for the proposal's Kuhnian use. The structuralist program of Sneed, Stegmüller, Balzer and Moulines represents a theory at one time as a **theory-net**, "a finite set of theory-elements (hierarchically) ordered by the (non-deductive) relation of specialization," and a theory over time as a **theory-evolution**, a sequence of nets in which every new net's elements are "specializations of some theory-elements of the previous theory-net" and whose domains of intended applications "have at least some partial overlapping" with the previous ones ([Lorenzano 2013](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/88249/CONICET_Digital_Nro.b0fd1bdd-1178-4d99-9670-e8910b55f747_A.pdf?sequence=2&isAllowed=y)). Those intended applications are a curated set "strongly depending on pragmatic and historical factors which, by their very nature, are not formalizable," and the theory's empirical claim, phrased notably as the claim that the domain "may actually be (exactly or approximately) subsumed (or embedded)" under its laws, is usually "strictly false" for the whole domain and true only "for a subclass" (same). That is close to feature (1), members assigned by people of which only some actually fit, and the continuity conditions are a formal ancestor of re-affirmation. The program also distinguishes kinds of change between theories: "crystallization, embedding, and replacement with (partial) incommensurability" (same). Kuhn doubted in 1975 that "any pair of historical theories separated by a revolution will satisfy your reduction relation," yet late in life judged the Sneed–Stegmüller version "the one that best fits with what goes on" because it can represent "revision of terminology and introduction of new terminology" (quoted in Lorenzano 2013). Sneed implemented theory-nets in PROLOG in 1989 to model research programmes ([Sneed 1989](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-009-3025-4_19)); what that program represented, and whether anyone reused it, could not be established. One consequence is terminological. Structuralism already uses "embedding" in two technical senses and machine learning in a third, so the proposal's term will collide for readers from either field.

The cognitive models grade how deep a fork runs. Thagard's scale ends with "branch jumping," shifting a concept from one branch of a hierarchical tree to another, and "tree switching," "changing the organizing principle of a hierarchical tree," as when Darwin changed "the organizational principle of the tree of life to be evolutionary history rather than similarity of features" ([Thagard 2006](http://cogsci.uwaterloo.ca/Articles/conc.change.life.pdf)). Gärdenfors and Zenker find that of five kinds of change to a conceptual space, "only the most extreme type—replacement of dimensions—comes close to a revolution" ([Gärdenfors & Zenker 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-011-0060-0)), and Andersen, Barker and Chen hold that incommensurability is "partial rather than total," does not "happen instantaneously," and does not "guarantee communication failure" ([Botterill, NDPR](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/the-cognitive-structure-of-scientific-revolutions/)). Galison's "intercalated periodization," known here only through secondary summaries, holds that theory, experiment and instrumentation each have their own breaks, "with the breaks coinciding only rarely" ([Galison, publications page](https://galison.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/image-and-logic)), which would make a paradigm shift a staggered series of forks across different kinds of articles rather than a single fork event. Lakatos and Laudan pose the identity question directly. A Lakatosian research programme has a "hard core" held irrefutable "by methodological fiat," so changing it means a new programme ([SEP: Lakatos](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/)), whereas Laudan's research traditions survive changes even to core elements, as absolute space and time were core to Newtonian physics but "no longer seen as such a century later" ([IEP: Scientific Change](https://iep.utm.edu/scientific-change/)). An embedding whose members can be re-affirmed through any change is Laudanian; one with a protected core, where changing the core forces a fork, is Lakatosian.

MacIntyre's account of traditions adds components none of these models or systems has. An epistemological crisis "is resolved … by the construction of a new narrative which enables the agent to understand both how he or she could intelligibly have held his or her original beliefs and how he or she could have been so drastically misled by them" ([Corfield, quoting *The Monist* p. 455](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/corfield/2006/04/more-about-macintyre.html)), and WJWR sets three requirements for the new scheme: it "must furnish a solution to the problems which had previously proved intractable in a systematic and coherent way," it "must also provide an explanation of just what it was which rendered the tradition, before it had acquired these new resources, sterile or incoherent or both," and it must do both "in a way which exhibits some fundamental continuity of the new conceptual and theoretical structures with the shared beliefs in terms of which the tradition of enquiry had been defined up to this point" (WJWR, p. 362). Progress is measured against the tradition's **problematic**, "that agenda of unsolved problems and unresolved issues by reference to which its success or lack of it in making rational progress toward some further stage of development will be evaluated" (p. 361), and continuity has a floor: "Some core of shared belief, constitutive of allegiance to the tradition, has to survive every rupture" (p. 356). Lineage also marks where comparison is tractable. Comparing thinkers within one tradition draws on "relatively unproblematic standards": how far the later thinker solves "the problems posed by or the problems found insoluble by the earlier thinker," resolves "incoherences in the work of the earlier," and supplies resources "which do not have the limitations of those of his predecessor"; Hume and Aristotle, by contrast, "simply do not stand in the kind of relationship to each other in which later and earlier adherents of the same tradition stand" (p. 328). On that account a fork can be judged against its parent's problematic, while comparison across lineages has no such shared measure, which splits "comparing is hard" into an easier case and a harder one. The nearest system analogue would be a narrative document attached to a fork or merge event, with PIE's recorded "basis for the merger" as its modest ancestor. No source proposes this. Targeted searches found no formal or computational model of a MacIntyrean tradition, and no ontology class for a Kuhnian paradigm beyond Benn's machine-derived ViewpointCluster. What has been built runs the other way: ConTraSt classifies 412 consciousness experiments against four fixed theories and reports, per a summary not checked against the paper, that support for a theory "can be predicted solely from methodological choices, irrespective of findings" ([Yaron et al. 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01284-5)). Labelling a corpus by school can expose school-shaped method even with a fixed list of schools.

## MacIntyre specified the museum in 1985, and it reads like a schema

The second question asks whether embeddings could get out of the predicament WJWR describes, "the confident belief that all cultural phenomena must be potentially translucent to understanding, that all texts must be capable of being translated into the language which the adherents of modernity speak to each other" (WJWR, p. 327), or would rebuild the "museum of texts" (p. 386). The sentence belongs to chapter 17, "Liberalism Transformed into a Tradition"; the passage as usually circulated splices it onto chapter 19's account of translation into "the internationalized languages of modernity" (pp. 384–386). The resources below concentrate on what a system's design would change. The wider reception of the passage in translation studies, museum studies and political theory is outside this report's scope.

The most precise statement of the museum objection comes from MacIntyre himself, three years before WJWR. His 1985 APA presidential address specifies the neutral "third language" that a way out of relativism would seem to need: its use "does not presuppose allegiance to either of the two rival sets of beliefs," yet it must "provide the resources for an accurate representation of the two competing schemes of belief." In it, "names in consequence will have to have become detached from those descriptions which, within some given and presupposed context defined by the beliefs of some particular community, uniquely identify person or place," so that "names having been Fregean will have become by a process of social change Kripkean"; that is, a name no longer carries a community's descriptions but simply labels its object. There will also be "the absence of texts which are canonical for its common use," and "where the text is in a foreign language, translation will be supplemented both by paraphrase and by scholarly gloss" ([MacIntyre 1985, pp. 13–14](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)). He then explains why it fails. Having no canonical texts, it accumulates all of them, "the whole finally to be assembled in that modern liberal arts college museum of academic culture, whose introductory tour is provided by those Great Books courses," and within such a culture "rational justification … becomes an essentially contested concept" (p. 15). Read as a specification, this describes the ordinary parts of a wiki with a comparison layer: globally unique identifiers, revision stamps any user can cite, a relation vocabulary with no home tradition, and summaries attached to foreign texts. MacIntyre concedes that strangers do use names without their community's presuppositions, but such uses are "parasitic upon its uses by the primary community" and remain "very specifically names-as-used-by-strangers-or-tourists" (p. 8). The design question this poses is whether a system's anchors and identifiers claim only that tourist status or serve as the terms in which traditions are compared.

WJWR carries the specification into three features that a wiki with a comparison layer would have. The first is reference. On Tory Island a personal name places its bearer in a kinship group and a community, and "'reference' is no more than a name for the unity in the diversity of use, and if the diversity of use were abstracted, what would remain would not be some pure referential relationship. Instead nothing at all would remain" (WJWR, p. 377). A stranger can use a name as "a referring expression for which an appropriate, definite description or in the right circumstances a gesture of pointing could be used instead," and "the conception of pure reference, of reference as such, emerges as the artefact of a particular type of social and cultural order, one in which a minimum of shared beliefs and allegiances can be presupposed" (p. 379). Naming "is not only naming as; it is also naming for," so of Doire Columcille and Londonderry, "to use either name is to deny the legitimacy of the other" (p. 378); a shared document's title is a name in this sense. The second is attribution. Modernity's languages let a tradition's texts be "reported on — not in the light of some other rival scheme of belief, by reference to which they would necessarily be exhibited as true or false, reasonable or unreasonable, but rather in detachment from all substantive criteria and standards of truth and rationality," with truth "assimilated, so far as is possible, to warranted assertibility" (p. 384). A record that an embedding held a claim as of a given revision has exactly that form, as does Wikipedia's rule to assert facts about opinions but not the opinions themselves; a tradition's own claim to truth has content "other than even idealized versions of assertions of warranted assertibility," because the tradition allows that it could be defeated (p. 388). The third is adjudication. Chapter 17 describes the liberal order in four levels: rival standpoints confronting one another "as assertion and counterassertion, rather than as argument and counterargument"; the tallying of preferences, "counting votes, responding to consumer choice, surveying public opinion"; perpetually inconclusive philosophical debate over the principles of that tallying; and the legal system, since "the lawyers, not the philosophers, are the clergy of liberalism" (pp. 342–344). Cross-perspective rating schemes such as Community Notes, ConsiderIt and Polis work at the second level, and a platform's moderation rules sit at the fourth. Power in such an order "lies with those who are able to determine what the alternatives are to be between which choices will be available" (p. 345), which on a platform means whoever designs the schema and lists the embeddings on a topic, and liberalism preempts its critics "by reformulating quarrels and conflicts with liberalism, so that they appear to have become debates within liberalism" (p. 392). Abstracting theses "from their contexts within traditions of enquiry" and evaluating them by "their rational justifiability to any rational person" makes the dialogue that could lead to accepting or rejecting a tradition "effectively impossible" (pp. 398–399), which offers a reason, beyond cost, for the fate of claim-level argument maps.

The literature on thick concepts (evaluative terms such as "chaste" or "cruel," where description and evaluation are fused) explains why mapping terms by what they refer to cannot replace the missing competence. McDowell's disentangling argument, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy reconstructs it, holds that "it isn't possible to anticipate the usage of E in a way required for mastering its extension without understanding the evaluative orientation of E," and the "Shapelessness" thesis adds that such extensions "aren't unified under independently intelligible non-evaluative relations of real similarity" ([SEP: Thick Ethical Concepts](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thick-ethical-concepts/)); even Williams, who allows an outsider to anticipate insider usage, requires the outsider to "grasp imaginatively" the evaluative point ([IEP: Thick Concepts](https://iep.utm.edu/thick-co/)). If that holds, a mapping between two embeddings' terms by shared extension is only as good as the insider competence of whoever wrote it. The debate is live (Blackburn, Elstein and Hurka, and Väyrynen resist the inseparability thesis), so this is a contested premise, not a settled result. Kuhn presses the same point for science: "reference-preserving translations may be incoherent, impossible to understand while the terms they employ are taken in their usual sense" ([Kuhn 1982, p. 681](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)), and for kind terms "the reference of a taxonomic term is not independent of human classification" ([Pirozelli 2020](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3842/384272794025.pdf)). Walzer denies that a neutral layer is available at all: "just as Esperanto is much closer to European languages than to any others, so minimalism when it is expressed as Minimal Morality will be forced into the idiom and orientation of one of the maximal moralities. There is no neutral (unexpressive) moral language" ([Walzer 1994, p. 9](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)). Kline's review of Stalnaker's comparison of Xunzi and Augustine shows the mechanism at work: the chosen "bridge concepts" (human nature, spiritual exercises, person, will) continue "an unfortunate tradition of examining Xunzi in terms of a conceptual framework rooted in the Augustinian tradition" ([Kline, NDPR](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/overcoming-our-evil-human-nature-and-spiritual-exercises-in-xunzi-and-augustine/)).

Two critiques target the side-by-side view specifically. Nagel's definition of objectivity is the aspiration a neutral comparison page embodies, since a view is more objective "if it relies less on the specifics of the individual's makeup and position in the world" ([Wikipedia: The View from Nowhere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_View_from_Nowhere)); Haraway replies that totalizing and relativist representations are both "god tricks," and that "relativism is a way of being nowhere while claiming to be everywhere equally" ([Haraway 1988](https://doi.org/10.2307/3178066); [notes on Haraway](http://www.ageofsignificance.org/documents/Haraway.pdf)). A page that shows every embedding equally, from above, commits the relativist version even if it ranks nothing. Strathern goes further, treating translation as a "fancy" and aiming to "get rid of the problem of 'comparison'" in favour of connections among parts "that cannot be compared insofar as they are not isomorphic with one another" ([Lebner 2017](https://www.berghahnbooks.com/downloads/intros/LebnerRedescribing_intro.pdf)). The Heterochronologies team makes the data-modelling version of the point: Linked Data "enable[s] interoperability at the expense of specificity" ([Drucker et al. 2022](https://digitalhumanities.org/dhq/vol/16/3/000624/000624.html)).

Heritage practice shows the museum concretely and shows who notices it. Zuni reviewers found museum catalogue descriptions "inadequate, many of them were incorrect, and in some cases there was no information at all" ([Hays-Gilpin et al. 2020](https://trajectoria.minpaku.ac.jp/articles/2020/vol01/01_1_2.html)), and estimated that museums have discrepancies in about 80% of their Zuni artifact descriptions, a figure that is the delegation's own estimate rather than an audit ([AMNH 2011](https://web.archive.org/web/2023id_/https://www.amnh.org/explore/news-blogs/news-posts/zuni-museum-enlists-amnh)). The national archives in Darwin had catalogued "images of people who were deceased … with no warnings" and divulged "pictures of sacred sites … with no connection to the ancestors who cared for those places" ([Christen 2012](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/1618/828)). The Library of Congress's Curtis collection acknowledges that some images portray rituals "not intended for viewing by the uninitiated," yet states that "no images have been excluded or specially labeled" ([Christen 2015](https://sustainableheritagenetwork.org/system/files/atoms/file/KimChristenWithey_TribalArchivesTraditionalKnowledgeandLocalContexts.pdf)). Boast set out to expose "the dark underbelly of the contact zone and, hence, the anatomy of the museum that seems to be persistently neocolonial," even in collaborative projects where community voices are added to a host record that remains the default ([Boast 2011](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Neocolonial%20Collaboration%20Museum%20as%20Contact%20Zone%20Revisited)). A multi-author platform that offers embeddings is itself a host, and its defaults, such as whose record appears first, are political choices.

The museum has a second form, which the fork data make likely: not one neutral hall but sealed rooms that a visitor strolls between. MacIntyre calls the doctrine behind it perspectivism, the proposal to understand rival traditions not "as mutually exclusive and incompatible ways of understanding one and the same world" but "as providing very different, complementary perspectives for envisaging the realities about which they speak to us" (WJWR, p. 352). Its mistake is to suppose "that one could temporarily adopt the standpoint of a tradition and then exchange it for another, as one might wear first one costume and then another," whereas "the multiplicity of traditions does not afford a multiplicity of perspectives among which we can move, but a multiplicity of antagonistic commitments, between which only conflict, rational or nonrational, is possible" (pp. 367–368). A reader switching between embeddings in a side-by-side view is in that position, and the vocabulary of "points of view" and "perspectives" that the earlier multi-POV survey took from its sources is perspectivist in exactly this sense. Cheap forking has its own version in the postmodern doctrine of the text: "The indefinite multiplicity of possible interpretations is matched by the indefinite multiplicity of translations," so that "mistranslation becomes in these circumstances increasingly difficult to achieve, since the canons of accuracy are necessarily relaxed in the name of creativity of interpretation" (pp. 386–387). MacIntyre's rationality of traditions requires encounter instead. When a tradition in crisis finds that a rival can explain its failure better than it can, "the rationality of tradition requires an acknowledgment by those who have hitherto inhabited and given their allegiance to the tradition in crisis that the alien tradition is superior in rationality and in respect of its claims to truth to their own," and "rationality, so understood, requires this acknowledgment of defeat in respect of truth" (p. 365); the second phrase, sometimes quoted as if from *Three Rival Versions*, is WJWR's. He also grants that "for very long periods traditions of very different kinds do indeed seem to coexist without any ability to bring their conflicts and disagreements to rational resolution" (p. 366), so long coexistence is the normal case rather than a sign of failure. Walzer's thin morality likewise appears only "in the course of a personal or social crisis or a political confrontation" ([Walzer 1994, p. 3](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)). A system in which each tradition can fork freely and never be confronted allows the opposite, and the software record says forks do not meet again without dedicated tooling: re-merge rates of 3.2% and 16.18%, and Lee's curator learning of the replacement only after the fact. Parallel embeddings that never address each other would be a museum of a different kind, each tradition kept intact in its own gallery.

## The same literature describes understanding owned by traditions

MacIntyre's own later work describes an architecture that embeddings could implement. In his 1991 essay on Confucians and Aristotelians, "we cannot find any legitimate standing ground outside the context of the points of view," so the comparative task "is not so much that of comparing Confucianism and Aristotelianism as that of comparing Confucian comparisons of Confucianism and Aristotelianism with Aristotelian comparisons of Confucianism and Aristotelianism" ([Strauss, quoting MacIntyre 1991](https://gideonstrauss.substack.com/p/the-key-to-comparative-studies-is); page unverified). A tradition comes close to vindication only by understanding its own standpoint "in a way that renders it from our own point of view as problematic as possible" (same). WJWR's chapter 10 gives controversy between traditions two stages. In the first, "each characterizes the contentions of its rival in its own terms, making explicit the grounds for rejecting what is incompatible with its own central theses"; the second is reached if each asks "whether the alternative and rival tradition may not be able to provide resources to characterize and to explain the failings and defects of their own tradition more adequately than they, using the resources of that tradition, have been able to do," a step that "requires a rare gift of empathy as well as of intellectual insight" in order "to view themselves from such an alien standpoint and to recharacterize their own beliefs in an appropriate manner from the alien perspective of the rival tradition" (WJWR, pp. 166–167). The competence is a "second first language" (p. 364): the learner has "to become a child all over again and to learn this language — and the corresponding parts of the culture — as a second first language," not by matching sentence to sentence (p. 374). Its mark is "to be able to recognize where and in what respects utterances in the one are untranslatable into the other," and the gaps can run both ways: Greek "lacked certain resources possessed by Hebrew before the Septuagint's translators partially transformed Greek," while "Hebrew till later still lacked philosophical resources which Greek itself had had to acquire" (p. 375). Chapter 19 ends on the attitude that makes such learning rational: "the only rational way for the adherents of any tradition to approach intellectually, culturally, and linguistically alien rivals is one that allows for the possibility that in one or more areas the other may be rationally superior to it in respect precisely of that in the alien tradition which it cannot as yet comprehend" (p. 388). The 1985 address gives the same competence an operational mark: "the ability to discriminate between those parts of each language which are translatable into the other and those which are not," because "that we can understand completely what is being said in some language other than our own never entails that we can translate what we understand" ([MacIntyre 1985, p. 10](https://pdfcoffee.com/macintyre-relativism-power-and-philosophy-1985-pdf-free.html)). Its example of asymmetry is that modern English can accurately represent Homeric uses of *eimi*, while "it would not have been possible within the Homeric linguistic community to represent accurately the modern English uses of 'exist'" (p. 14), so a rendering of A in B's terms licenses nothing about B in A's. On this reading an embedding can be one tradition's comparison, a mapping can be directional and can record "untranslatable here, and why," an embedding can list what it "cannot as yet comprehend" in a rival, and the one thing to avoid is a comparison that belongs to the system.

Kuhn gives the anchor a legitimate but limited role. Interpretation, meaning learning a new language, is possible even where translation fails, and "historians dealing with old scientific texts can and must use modern language to identify referents of out-of-date terms. Like the native's pointing to gavagais, these reference-determinations often provide the concrete examples from which historians may hope to learn what the problematical expressions in their texts mean," though Kuhn denies that this counts as translation ([Kuhn 1982, pp. 672–674](https://www.marcellodibello.com/commonground/readings/KuhnComComCom.pdf)). He adds that old texts deserve "at least the courtesy normally extended to works of fiction. They report what scientists of the past believed, independent of its truth-value" (pp. 674–675). A quote anchor into a pinned revision is a shared place to point while learning, which Kuhn treats as indispensable, not a shared meaning. The limit is that where one tradition holds a text canonical and another treats it as one source among many, which recension or edition counts as "the same passage" is itself contested, so anchors into it are not symmetric. WJWR presses harder than Kuhn. Pure reference is, on its account, the stranger's use of a name (p. 379), and "there are no preconceptual or even pretheoretical data," because "each theory of practical reasoning is, among other things, a theory as to how examples are to be described" (p. 333); an event placed on a shared timeline is already one tradition's description of it. The same book gives anchors a different and firmer footing. In tradition-bearing communities, asked "What is x?", "one standard way of answering is to quote a line or two from a poem," so that "the meanings of key expressions are fixed in part by reference to standard authoritative texts," texts treated "both as having a fixed meaning embodied in them and also as always open to rereading" (pp. 382–383). An anchor earns its place when an embedding uses it to mark its own defining passages, pinned to the text it holds canonical, rather than as a pointer that claims to be neutral between traditions.

Walzer and Zeitlyn give the thin layer a defensible shape. Walzer's thin morality is real but derivative: "minimalist meanings are embedded in the maximal morality," the minimum "simply designates some reiterated features of particular thick or maximal moralities," and "the minimum is not the foundation of the maximum, only a piece of it. The value of minimalism lies in the encounters it facilitates, of which it is also the product." As he puts it, "if we did not have our own parade, we could not march vicariously in Prague" ([Walzer 1994, pp. 3, 10, 18–19](https://www.philosophyandlaw.org/Philosophers/Walzer/Thick-and-Thin-Chapter%20One_Moral%20Minimalism.pdf)). Reiteration can be recorded: several embeddings independently point at the same passage and say, each in its own words, "this, too, we condemn." Zeitlyn's 2026 argument that "thin accounts provide the scaffolding within which thick descriptions sit," so that "anthropology consists of mosaics of thick descriptions, held within thinly described structures," takes silent trade, exchange "without direct communication so that there is literally no sharing of meanings," as its limiting case ([Zeitlyn 2026](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aman.70056)). Anchors, revision stamps and provenance make no claim about meaning, and the museum objection bites when that scaffold starts to categorize or explain the tiles. MacIntyre's own verdict on the universality that survives abstraction from every tradition is that it is "far too thin and meager to supply what is needed" to adjudicate between them (WJWR, p. 334), which fits a scaffold that holds the tiles but does not judge them.

Taylor offers the opposite pole. The adequate language for understanding another society "is not our language of understanding, or theirs, but rather what one could call a language of perspicuous contrast," in which "both our form of life and theirs could be perspicuously described as alternative such variations" relative to "some human constants," and "while challenging their language of self-understanding, we may also be challenging ours" ([Taylor 1985, quoted by Webster](https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/a5995656-9ac9-4a0a-a6d0-480b7188ede9/content)). Taylor thus allows a third language that MacIntyre's 1991 essay does not, but only one made in the encounter, revisable, and able to find either side distorted. A later Taylor essay locates the crucial moment where difference "escapes from its categorization as an error, a fault, or a lesser, undeveloped version of what we are" (page unverified) ([Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Understanding-the-other-:-A-Gadamerian-view-on-1-Taylor/ed38848c99c6e8ee83eb8d890cd299798cd2f1fd)), and MacIntyre, for his part, called Gadamer "the exemplary practitioner of the hermeneutic virtues, both intellectual and moral" ([NDPR review of *Gadamer's Century*](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/gadamer-s-century-essays-in-honor-of-hans-georg-gadamer/)). A system that treats any contrast language as one more attributed, forkable embedding could host both positions without deciding between them. That is an inference; neither author addresses systems.

Research on trading zones treats coordination without shared meaning as ordinary practice. Galison's formulation is that "two groups can agree on rules of exchange even if they ascribe utterly different significance to the objects being exchanged; they may even disagree on the meaning of the exchange process itself. Nonetheless, the trading partners can hammer out a local coordination, despite vast global differences" ([Wikipedia: Trading zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_zones)). Collins, Evans and Gorman's fractionated zone keeps forms of life distinct while sharing a boundary object or interactional expertise, and their ambassadorial model describes a rendering across embeddings precisely: selected individuals learn another group's language and "can then represent the thinking of the other group within the home group. Representing is not the same as translating; translation is always incomplete if not impossible across deep cultural divides" ([Collins, Evans & Gorman 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.06327)). Star and Griesemer's founding claim is that "consensus is not necessary for cooperation nor for the successful conduct of work," with coordination running through "a many-to-many mapping" rather than one obligatory point of passage ([Star & Griesemer 1989](https://lchc.ucsd.edu/mca/Mail/xmcamail.2012_09.dir/pdfuaCxVBhVe5.pdf)). Carlile marks the limit: where interests conflict, common knowledge is negotiated politically and knowledge must be transformed rather than translated. The risk also runs the other way, because a creole, the trading zone's end state, is a new common language, and on MacIntyre's terms that is where a third language would be reborn.

MacIntyre states the test of a second first language himself, and it is a passing test: "How far can he or she pass as a native?", or, for a culture known only from texts, "Can he or she, when acting in a play by Aristophanes, introduce a piece of comic improvisation in which the best scholarship could detect no relevant difference from the original?" (WJWR, p. 375). It tests the production of new text in the idiom, not the recognition of a summary of it. Collins and Evans supply the nearest empirical method. In their Imitation Game, insider judges try to pick out a genuine member from an outsider pretending to be one, and experiments found that "the linguistic performance of those well socialized in the language of a specialist group is indistinguishable from those with full blown practical socialization but distinguishable from those who are not well socialized" ([Collins et al. 2006, via PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/COLEWI)). The method has been run on pairs including "active Christians and secular students," comparing social relationships "across social and temporal distances in both a qualitative and a quantitative way" ([Collins & Evans 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0038038512455735)). The European Research Council funded the IMGAME project (2011–2016, €2,260,083, eight partners) as "a new method for cross-cultural and cross-temporal comparison of societies" ([Collins, IMGAME](https://sites.cardiff.ac.uk/harrycollins/the-imitation-game/)), and a replication repeated games about religion roughly twelve months later ([Evans et al. 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13645579.2021.1986316)). The country-level results could not be retrieved, and no published use of the game evaluates written renderings rather than live respondents. Caplan's informal "ideological Turing test" makes the same move for politics and adds a claim about truth: "If someone can correctly explain a position but continue to disagree with it, that position is less likely to be correct" ([Caplan 2011](https://www.econlib.org/archives/2011/06/the_ideological.html)). The gap between Collins and MacIntyre is real. Interactional expertise is fluency acquired "from spoken discourse alone," without practice or allegiance ([Collins & Evans 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.04423)), while MacIntyre's second first language ends in a judgment of truth that may be an admission of defeat. Passing the game is evidence that a rendering would be recognized, which on MacIntyre's criterion is necessary but not sufficient.

WJWR also limits where any recognition test applies. Between two traditions whose beliefs exclude each other, "one standard test of translation and of translatability cannot be applied": the judgment of someone fluent in both that translating back would reproduce the original, which fails "because adequacy of explanation is relative to the beliefs of those to whom something is being explained, and because each scheme of belief involves the rejection of the other" (WJWR, pp. 380–381). Horace's ode making Augustus a present god "could only have emerged in Hebrew as at once false and blasphemous," and "it is in the course of just this type of explanation that 'daimōn' is transformed into 'demon'" (p. 380). Such a rendering is the first stage of controversy described above, not a failed translation, and its subjects are not expected to accept it. The recognition criterion belongs to the other case, translation into an internationalized language, whose product "would not be recognized or accepted by speakers and writers for whom the original language-in-use was their first first language, but who had learned the particular internationalized language as their second first language" (p. 385); its judges are bilinguals of that kind, not adherents at large. Two kinds of artifact can therefore be checked by insiders: a rendering that claims to present a tradition as it understands itself, and a second-stage recharacterization of one's own beliefs "from the alien perspective of the rival tradition" (p. 167), which the rival's adherents can judge. Concepts held in this secondary way can be deployed only "as dramatic impersonators, speaking in a voice which is not our own" (p. 395), so a text written in another tradition's voice is impersonation and can be labelled as such.

Institutions that refuse total translatability already run on software. The CARE Principles for Indigenous data governance (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) are "people– and purpose-oriented," complementing the data-centric FAIR principles ([Carroll et al. 2020](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043)). Christen argues that treating openness "as an end in and of itself" has distracted advocates from "alternative access regimes that are neither oppressive nor controlling, but based on divergent social and ethical systems," and Mukurtu's protocols follow "family and place-based relations," so a user sees only content tagged with her own affiliations ([Christen 2012](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/1618/828)). Local Contexts' Traditional Knowledge Labels (Seasonal, Women Restricted, Secret/Sacred, Community Use Only and others) attach a community's conditions to an item as "sticky" metadata that travels with the record, and a redacted item that keeps its label and description is "not an incomplete record, but one that followed the viewing protocols of the tribe" ([Local Contexts](https://localcontexts.org/labels/traditional-knowledge-labels/); [Christen 2015](https://sustainableheritagenetwork.org/system/files/atoms/file/KimChristenWithey_TribalArchivesTraditionalKnowledgeandLocalContexts.pdf)). Srinivasan and colleagues framed the design problem in MacIntyre's own terms ("How can a museum present different and possibly conflicting perspectives in such a way that the tension between them is preserved?") and found that "narratives and objects in use are key omissions in traditional museum documentation" ([Srinivasan et al. 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0162243909357755)). For reading across traditions without a neutral idiom, Scriptural Reasoning has convened Jewish, Christian and Muslim groups since 1992 to read each other's scriptures closely "as opposed to theological discussions of religious doctrines" ([Wikipedia: Peter W. Ochs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_W._Ochs)). WJWR states the underlying proposal directly. Had the abolition of religious tests turned universities into places of ordered intellectual conflict, "unity of belief would have been replaced by a multiplicity of contending beliefs, each permitted to provide its own framework for enquiry"; instead they enforced "a conception of scholarly competence, independent of standpoint," and "became institutions committed to upholding a fictitious objectivity" (WJWR, p. 399). His later formulation, the university as "a place of constrained disagreement, of imposed participation in conflict" whose teachers would "ensure that rival voices were not illegitimately suppressed," is known here only through secondary summaries of *Three Rival Versions* ([Stolz 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-92276-8_3); [*Faith and Philosophy* review](https://place.asburyseminary.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1345&context=faithandphilosophy)), and no literature found treats either proposal, or his criteria for resolving a crisis, as a procedure a system could run.

The user's hypothesis about doxa, the taken-for-granted background, gets unexpected support from the ethnographies of scientific cyberinfrastructure, and the support cuts both ways. The hypothesis is that traditions under sustained contest write down what an unchallenged idiom leaves unwritten. GEON's participants found that "what had passed as the tacit accord of experts broke down in attempts to render knowledge explicitly" ([Ribes & Bowker 2009](https://ics.uci.edu/~gbowker/publications/Ribes%20Bowker%20-%20Between%20Meaning%20and%20Machine.pdf)), and Spider's managers found differences "undetected, sometimes for many years" once their perspectives were drawn as structures others could inspect ([Boland & Tenkasi 1996](https://ceo.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/1996/11/1996-14-g96_14-Distributed_Cognition.pdf)). Embeddings that make each school's relations explicit could do the same to modernity's unwritten criteria, putting them in a form a rival tradition can point at. The same studies record the cost, since "momentary agreements can become programmed commitments," and Bowker and Star's "naturalization," "stripping away the contingencies of an object's creation and its situated nature," is what happens to a classification that succeeds ([Bowker & Star 1999](https://hcommons.org/app/uploads/sites/1001532/2020/03/Bowker-1999-Sorting-Things-Out-Classification-and-Its-Consequences.pdf)). Controversy mapping states the aspiration at its best, pursuing "second-degree objectivity … attained by revealing the full extent of actors' disagreement," a practice that "does not boast impartiality" and aims "to show that [controversies] may be closed in many different ways" ([Venturini 2010](https://medialab.sciencespo.fr/publications/Venturini-Diving_in_Magma.pdf)). It leaves open whether the mapper, roaming "from cosmos to cosmos," is a participant or a curator.

## *Whose Justice?* ends at a fork, and names the forum it lacks

The last chapter of WJWR states the problem the proposal addresses. For anyone who would put liberalism in question, "one initial problem ... is that of either discovering or constructing some institutional forum or arena within which the terms of the debate have not already predetermined its outcome" (WJWR, pp. 392–393). The same chapters say what such a forum would have to hold if its units were traditions rather than tagged documents. A tradition confers authority on "certain texts and certain voices" (p. 354), has "its set of authoritative texts and its disputes over their interpretation" and "its own standards of rational justification" (p. 345), keeps a core of shared belief that "has to survive every rupture" (p. 356), measures itself against a problematic (pp. 167, 361), speaks a language with its own names and its own chains of translated terms (pp. 378, 383), has a history that its adherents rewrite after each crisis (p. 363), and is embodied "in particular types of social relationship" among people who give it their allegiance (pp. 389–391). Chapter 17 adds what a mature tradition's problematic takes in beyond its own unsolved problems: "an ability to recognize when one's conceptual resources are inadequate in such an encounter, or when one is unable to frame satisfactorily what others have to say to one in criticism and rebuttal," and "a sensitivity to the distortions which may arise in trying to capture within one's own framework theses originally at home in another" (p. 327). These are the parts of the "composite, non-aggregate structure" the proposal asks for, and a tag has none of them.

Continuity over time is what separates a tradition from a perspective. The perspectivist's philosophy is marked by "its temporariness; dwelling too long in any one place will always threaten to confer upon such philosophy the continuity of enquiry, so that it becomes embodied as one more rational tradition" (p. 369). In the proposal's terms, an embedding that keeps re-affirming its members against their revisions and keeps answering its critics is acquiring that continuity, and one that is forked, visited and abandoned is not.

The book's own ending is a fork. "There are, that is to say, at least four alternative ways of continuing the narratives of the earlier chapters, at least four alternative ways of moving this book toward further conclusions, but no one author could write more than one of them": Aristotelian, Augustinian, Thomist and Humean, and "it is here that we have to begin speaking as protagonists of one contending party or fall silent" (p. 401). Each continuation is tested by the history it can write of its rivals: "Can we by means of those resources understand the achievements and successes, and the failures and sterilities, of rival traditions more adequately than their own adherents can? More adequately by our standards? More adequately also by theirs?" (p. 402). And the verdict rests on those histories: "The rival claims to truth of contending traditions of enquiry depend for their vindication upon the adequacy and the explanatory power of the histories which the resources of each of those traditions in conflict enable their adherents to write" (p. 403). On this account the proposal's four purposes, comparing schools of thought, tellings of a history, theoretical framings and a discipline's trajectory, are one activity: each embedding writing the history of the discipline, its rivals included, for the others to test by both sets of standards. A multi-author system can host the continuations that no single author could write, and its own voice has to fall silent at the fork.

The reader is situated too. How to respond to rival traditions "will depend upon who you are and how you understand yourself," and "the wider the audience to whom we aspire to speak, the less we shall speak to anyone in particular" (pp. 393–394), which tells against a single comparison page addressed to everyone. MacIntyre distinguishes the reader who meets a tradition with a shock of recognition, "what I have always taken to be true" (p. 394); the alienated reader for whom every tradition is one of "a series of falsifying masquerades" (p. 395); and the majority, who live "betwixt and between" with "too many half-convictions and too few settled coherent convictions" (p. 397). The last can turn that incoherence to use "by requiring of each tradition that it supply an account of how these incoherences are best to be characterized, explained, and transcended" (p. 398), a way of reading rival embeddings that no surveyed system supports.

## Thirteen conditions separate a museum from constrained disagreement

The museum and the trading zone look alike, since both hold things from many traditions, and the literature distinguishes them by who acts. In the museum, curators recontextualize objects for a public that shares none of the objects' frameworks; in the trading zone, and in MacIntyre's university, parties who keep their frameworks coordinate and contend for their own purposes. The conditions below are inferences from the sources, since none of them addresses software, and each is paired with the failure it guards against.

| Condition | Grounds in the literature | Failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Every comparison is opened from, and labelled with, some embedding; there is no default view from all of them | MacIntyre's "comparison of comparisons" (1991); WJWR on the situated reader (pp. 393–394) and on the person outside all traditions as "a stranger to enquiry" (p. 367); Haraway's god trick | The 1985 third language; "being nowhere while claiming to be everywhere equally" |
| Inside an embedding, claims are asserted and a rival may be called false; attribution and revision stamps are the outsider's register, never the only one a reader is shown | WJWR on perspectivism (pp. 352, 367–368), on texts "reported on … in detachment from all substantive criteria" (p. 384), and on truth as more than warranted assertibility (p. 388) | Traditions reduced to "complementary perspectives"; every claim restated as "E held X as of revision N" |
| Shared anchors and identifiers are the stranger's labels: each embedding names shared things in its own terms, anchors mark the passages an embedding holds canonical, and a text canonical for one side only is recorded as such | WJWR on pure reference (pp. 377–379), naming as and naming for (p. 378) and meaning fixed by quotation (pp. 382–383); MacIntyre on "names-as-used-by-strangers-or-tourists" (1985); Kuhn on reference-determination (1982) | Names emptied of their community's meaning (Fregean made Kripkean); one side's name, title or recension becoming "the" one |
| Mappings between embeddings are attributed, directional and pinned to revisions, can assert "not the same" and "no counterpart as yet," and are presented as aids to reading, not as translations | WJWR on phrase books (pp. 381–382), same-saying versus linguistic innovation (p. 372) and dated untranslatability (p. 372); the *eimi*/"exist" asymmetry; SSSOM's `Not` and `NoTermFound`; DDL's directional bridge rules | A phrase book passed off as understanding; identity judgments with κ = 0.158 passed off as fact |
| Any bridging vocabulary is itself an attributed, forkable embedding, and rivals may propose other coordinates over the same anchors | Walzer's "no neutral (unexpressive) moral language"; WJWR on thin universality as "far too thin and meager" (p. 334); Taylor's contrast language made in the encounter; Kline on Stalnaker; GEON's working agreements | One tradition's framework serving as the axes; a creole hardening into a new third language |
| A rival rendered in one's own terms, with the grounds for rejecting it, is labelled as such and not submitted to its subjects; a rendering that claims to present a tradition as it understands itself, or a recharacterization of oneself from a rival's standpoint, carries that tradition's recorded verdicts, stamped with the revision judged | WJWR's two stages of controversy (pp. 166–167), the back-translation test that "cannot be applied" between rivals (pp. 380–381), the recognition criterion and its bilingual judges (p. 385), and the passing test (p. 375); Collins and Evans's Imitation Game | Explanations unacceptable to those whose text it was, presented as the tradition's own; rivals required to flatter each other |
| Communities govern circulation: which of their texts outsiders may anchor into, which renderings appear inside their embedding, and whether a labelled redaction stands in for comparison | CARE; Mukurtu's protocols; TK Labels; Zuni reviewers who held that some objects should not be displayed | The Curtis-collection default, "no images have been excluded or specially labeled" |

| Each embedding keeps a problematic: its unsolved problems by its own standards, the criticisms it cannot yet frame, and what it cannot as yet comprehend in a rival | WJWR on the problematic (pp. 167, 361), on the marks of a maturing tradition (p. 327) and on the only rational approach to alien rivals (p. 388) | A school with no measure of its own progress; a museum label where a live enquiry should be |
| The system never merges embeddings automatically, computes a consensus embedding, tallies ratings into a verdict between traditions, or infers identity from titles | Rolland et al. on integration; Kellogg et al.; Kuhn's no-overlap principle; WJWR on preference tallying (pp. 342–344) and on theses abstracted from their traditions (pp. 398–399); InPhO as the counter-model | Integration that reproduces fragmentation; "suppression of difference"; the second level of the liberal order |
| Encounter is induced: forks notify their parents, an embedding can write "how we look from their side," and a rival's change can prompt re-affirmation | MacIntyre's recharacterization "from the alien perspective of the rival tradition" (WJWR, p. 167); WJWR's "multiplicity of contending beliefs, each permitted to provide its own framework for enquiry" (p. 399); *Three Rival Versions*' "imposed participation in conflict" (secondary); Lee; Zhou et al. | Sealed rooms: parallel traditions that never test each other |
| Fork and merge events carry a narrative of why the embedding peeled away or pulled together, and an embedding can rewrite its history after a crisis while the pinned record stays | MacIntyre's crisis narrative (1977); WJWR on crises "recognized ... in retrospect" and histories rewritten (p. 363); PIE's recorded "basis for the merger" | Categories that break down and "leave no continuous trace back to the previous regime" ([Bowker & Star](https://hcommons.org/app/uploads/sites/1001532/2020/03/Bowker-1999-Sorting-Things-Out-Classification-and-Its-Consequences.pdf)) |
| Each embedding can write the history of the discipline, its rivals included, and those histories are what gets compared, by the rival's standards as well as its own | WJWR on the four continuations "no one author could write" (p. 401), the two-standard questions (p. 402) and vindication by "the explanatory power of the histories" (p. 403) | Positions compared without the histories that vindicate or defeat them |

Several of these conditions pull against each other, and the literature does not resolve the tensions. Community control over circulation limits the comparisons the first condition would open, and Christen's "alternative access regimes" run directly against the openness norms most wikis assume. Inducing encounter is what MacIntyre's university requires and what the fork data say will not happen by itself, but there is no evidence on how much inducement readers and authors will tolerate. The previous generation of debate wikis is not encouraging here: Wikiversity's Wikidebate was "discontinued due to a lack of moderation and policy implementation" ([Wikiversity: Wikidebate](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Wikidebate)), and a comparison of thirteen debate encyclopedias lists shallow argument depth and "lack of interlinking between debates" among the common failures ([Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias)). WJWR holds that liberal forums presuppose "the irrelevance of one's history to one's status as a participant in debate" (p. 400), while ConsiderIt's authors credited cross-stance inclusion partly to the fact that "ConsiderIt does not provide group cues to activate political identity" ([Kriplean et al. 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)); a design that shows participants' allegiances follows the first and risks losing what the second measured. A passing test measures whether a rendering would be recognized, not whether a tradition has been answered; MacIntyre's two-standard questions for a rival history (p. 402) are the nearest thing to an instrument for his "acknowledgment of defeat," and no system has tried to record one. Three unknowns remain open: no study has used insider-recognition tests on written renderings, no study has observed whether readers of rival structures shown side by side behave as tourists or as participants, and no work treats resolving an epistemological crisis as something a system could host rather than compute.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the seven literatures draw the same line through the design. The cheap, structural operations (membership, forking, pinning, diffing, clustering) have been built again and again, while the expensive, contested judgments (whether two things are the same, whether a revision still fits, whether a rendering would be recognized, whether a rival has been answered) resist computation wherever they were tried, from OntoView's "not possible to determine automatically" to GEON's unused uncertainty fields. That line turns out to be the line between MacIntyre's museum and his university. The museum is what results when a system makes those judgments on everyone's behalf in a neutral idiom, or reduces every tradition's judgments to reports of who held what; constrained disagreement is what results when embeddings make those judgments in their own voices, the system records who made each one, from which embedding and against which revision, and rival traditions' judgments, above all their histories of each other, are put in front of each other. Nothing in the record shows which a built system would become, because the combination has never been built and the deciding variable, who does the comparing, is social rather than technical.

The evidence yields three tests. For the comparison layer, Spider supplies the criterion: does putting two embeddings side by side reveal a difference their own members did not know they had? For the museum question, MacIntyre's own passing test supplies one, for renderings that claim to speak for a tradition: can an outsider write in its idiom so that its bilingual insiders detect no relevant difference, at the revision tested? For the contest itself, WJWR supplies the third: does one embedding's history of another explain that rival's achievements and sterilities more adequately than the rival's own history does, by the rival's standards as well as its own? None of the three has been run on written texts or on structures of the kind proposed. The one feature no literature anticipates at all is re-affirmation against a living source, since every surveyed precedent pins to an immutable book, release or snapshot. Yet that feature would let an embedding register the moment a tradition's own text moves out from under a rival's reading of it, which is where MacIntyre locates the tests that matter.
