## Prompt

Read the following from Alasdair MacIntyre's "Whose Justice? Which Rationality?" and then find scholarship which engages in the issues MacIntyre raises. I am particularly interested in the possibility that modernity takes a lot for granted which doesn't show up in literal text, whereas traditions might take less for granted by including more in literal text (especially over decades and centuries). But also find other directions scholars have taken, grouping by discipline.


 It is also part of the nature of traditions that their adherents cannot know in advance, whatever their own convictions or pretensions may be, how and in what condition their tradition will emerge from such conflicts and encounters. Their warranted confidence in their own tradition will of course increase as their tradition shows itself in successive encounters able to furnish the necessary resources and achieve the necessary transformations. But the perspective of a tradition originally rooted in contingent circumstance, arising out of problems, perplexities, and disagreements in some particular social order, articulated in terms of the particularities of the language and culture of that order, does not permit of any generalized confidence about the potential hospitality of other languages and cultures to the articulated statement of, let alone to allegiance to, that particular tradition. And that is to say that the standpoint of traditions is necessarily at odds with one of the central characteristics of cosmopolitan modernity: the confident belief that all cultural phenomena must be potentially translucent to understanding, that all texts must be capable of being translated into the language which the adherents of modernity speak to each other.

But the historical dimension creates another kind of difficulty of its own when the task of translation to be performed is from just such a language-in-use but into one of the internationalized languages of modernity.
 Just because it is characteristic of such languages that they are tied very loosely to any particular set of contestable beliefs but are rich in modes of characterization and explanation which enable texts embodying alien schemes of systematic belief to be reported on—not in the light of some other rival scheme of belief, by reference to which they would necessarily be exhibited as true or false, reasonable or unreasonable, but rather in detachment from all substantive criteria and standards of truth and rationality—the problems arising from a translation part of whose end product is an explanation of the meaning of a text unacceptable to those whose text it was or is, do not at first sight seem to arise.
 What do I mean by “in detachment from all substantive criteria and standards of truth and rationality”? Just because and insofar as the internationalized languages-in-use of late twentieth-century modernity have minimal presuppositions in respect of possibly rival belief systems, their shared criteria for the correct application of such concepts as “is true” and “is reasonable” must also be minimal. And in fact truth is assimilated, so far as is possible, to warranted assertibility, and reasonableness, so far as possible, is relativized to social context. Hence when texts from traditions with their own strong, substantive criteria of truth and rationality, as well as with a strong historical dimension, are translated into such languages, they are presented in a way that neutralizes the conceptions of truth and rationality and the historical context. How is this achieved?
 The conceptions of truth and rationality become not part of a presupposed framework of beliefs to which the author appeals in addressing an audience who shares or shared that same framework but are relegated to an explanation to an audience characterized as not possessing any such framework. The particular history out of which the author wrote and which it is his or her purpose to carry one stage further also disappears from view as the presupposed context of the work and appears instead, if at all, as an explanatory appendage to it. In these two ways a kind of text which cannot be read as the text it is out of context is nevertheless rendered contextless. But in so rendering it, it is turned into a text which is no longer the author’s, nor such as would be recognized by the audience to whom it was addressed. The task of translation into such a language has been achieved at the cost of producing something which would not be recognized or accepted by speakers and writers for whom the original language-in-use was their first first language, but who had learned the particular internationalized language as their second first language.
 This distortion by translation out of context—from the standpoint of those who inhabit the traditions from which the distorted texts are taken—is of course apt to be invisible to those whose first first language is one of the internationalized languages of modernity. For them it must appear that there is nothing which is not translatable into their language. Untranslatability—if cautious, they may say untranslatability in principle—will perhaps appear to them as a philosophical fiction.
 This belief in its ability to understand everything from human culture and history, no matter how apparently alien, is itself one of the defining beliefs of the culture of modernity. It is evident in the way in which the history of art is taught and written, so that the objects and the texts produced by other cultures are brought under our concept of art, allowing us to exhibit what were in fact very different and heterogenous kinds of objects under one and the same aesthetic rubric in new artificial neutral contexts in our museums, museums which in an important way have become the public buildings of this kind of educated modernity, just as the temple was for Periclean Athens or the cathedral for thirteenth-century France.
 The type of translation characteristic of modernity generates in turn its own misunderstanding of tradition. The original locus of that misunderstanding is the kind of introductory Great Books or Humanities course, so often taught in liberal arts colleges, in which, in abstraction from historical context and with all sense of the complexities of linguistic particularity removed by translation, a student moves in rapid succession through Homer, one play of Sophocles, two dialogues of Plato, Virgil, Augustine, the Inferno, Machiavelli, Hamlet, and as much else as is possible if one is to reach Sartre by the end of the semester. If one fails to recognize that what this provides is not and cannot be a reintroduction to the culture of past traditions but is a tour through what is in effect a museum of texts, each rendered contextless and therefore other than its original by being placed on a cultural pedestal, then it is natural enough to suppose that, were we to achieve consensus as to a set of such texts, the reading of them would reintegrate modern students into what is thought of as our tradition, that unfortunate fictitious amalgam sometimes known as “the Judeo-Christian tradition” and sometimes as “Western values.” The writings of self-proclaimed contemporary conservatives, such as William J. Bennett, turn out in fact to be one more stage in modernity’s cultural deformation of our relationship to the past.
 It is then perhaps unsurprising that recent postmodernist doctrines of the text, although at the level of theory they have marked a radical break from their immediate predecessors, nonetheless in their theory did no more than provide a rationale for practices which were of fairly long standing in peculiarly modern education. Every text, so the radical postmodernist proclaims, is susceptible of indefinitely many interpretative readings. The understanding of the text is not controlled by authorial intention or by any relationship to an audience with specific shared beliefs, for it is outside context except the context of interpretation. So Roland Barthes could assert that a work of literature is unlike an utterance with practical import in which pragmatic considerations drawn from the context of utterance disambiguate it: “That is not the case with a work (oeuvre): the work is without circumstance and it is indeed perhaps what defines it best: the work is not circumscribed, designated, protected, directed by any situation, no practical life is there to tell what meaning to give to it . . . in it ambiguity is wholly pure: however extended it may be, it possesses something of the brevity of the priestess of Apollo, sayings conforming to a first code (the priestess did not rave) and yet open to a number of meanings, for they were uttered outside every situation—except indeed the situation of ambiguity . . .” (Critique et Verité, Paris, 1966, p. 56). This is a splendid description of what traditional texts detached from the context of tradition must become, presented by Barthes as though it were an account of how necessarily texts always are.

## Response

Below is a map of the scholarship, starting with your specific hypothesis (which turns out to be a live and contested empirical question, not just a speculative one), then the other directions grouped by discipline.

---

### I. Your hypothesis: modernity's unstated presuppositions vs. tradition's accumulated explicitness

Nobody, as far as I can find, has stated your thesis in exactly that form. But the pieces of an argument for it — and a serious body of evidence against it — both exist.

**The mechanism your thesis needs (sociology).** Bourdieu supplies it directly. His distinction between *doxa* (the taken-for-granted, undiscussed universe) and *orthodoxy* is that doxa is the world of tradition experienced as natural and taken for granted, what "goes without saying because it comes without saying," while heresy is what challenges doxa and provokes an epistemological rupture — the dominant have an interest in defending doxa's integrity or, failing that, in establishing orthodoxy as its imperfect substitute. Doxa is the universe of the undiscussed and undisputed, the shared assumptions that let orthodox and heterodox argue with each other in the first place. That is precisely your asymmetry: an unchallenged position never has to write its premises down; a challenged one must. A tradition under centuries of pressure accumulates explicit articulation; a hegemonic idiom does not.

**Modernity's side of the ledger.** Charles Taylor is the obvious partner. His "social imaginary" and "immanent frame" are attempts to unearth the background understandings that inform late-modern social life, what people take for granted by virtue of being socialized into a particular imaginary; and he criticizes the modern social imaginary precisely for presenting itself as natural and self-evident while failing to recognize its constructedness. Talal Asad's *Secular Translations* (2018) is even closer to MacIntyre's exact claim: it works through translatability and untranslatability, giving particular importance to translations of religious ideas into nonreligious ones, and argues that translation opens the door for — or requires — the utter transformation of what is translated. In his earlier essay he names the underlying condition "the inequality of languages": the relationship between West and non-West is not equal but saturated by power asymmetries, and he attacks the anthropological habit of "reading the implicit" — treating the translator as able to reveal meanings hidden from the natives themselves. That is MacIntyre's "explanatory appendage" in another vocabulary.

**Tradition's side.** John Henderson's *Scripture, Canon, and Commentary* (1991) is the best comparative resource. He relates the Confucian commentarial tradition to Homeric, Vedāntic, rabbinic, Christian, and Qur'anic exegesis, isolating assumptions common to all of them — e.g. that the classics are comprehensive and contain all significant truth — and analyzing the strategies deployed to sustain those presuppositions. Crucially for you, his account of commentary's function is that it existed not only to illuminate obscurities but to defend against heterodox interpretations that might arise from naïve readings of the "plain meaning" — i.e., commentary is the written residue of presuppositions forced into the open by conflict. Brian Stock's *The Implications of Literacy* (textual communities) is the standard companion.

**The evidence that cuts against you — take this seriously.** Two literatures point the other way.

*Sociolinguistics.* Basil Bernstein's elaborated/restricted code distinction assigns explicitness to the *cosmopolitan* pole, not the traditional one. Elaborated codes orient users toward universalistic meanings; where meanings are particularistic, principles are linguistically implicit, context-bound, tied to a local relationship and restricted to those sharing a contextual history — whereas universalistic meanings are in principle available to all because the principles have been made explicit and public. Bernstein's account of the condition producing restricted code is a social relationship based on closely shared identifications and expectations, which reduces the need to verbalize intent. On this picture, a tight tradition *presupposes more* and writes less. Note the escape route, though: Bernstein was heavily criticized — Labov argued restricted code could express meaning and elaborate a point to the same extent, and that no code is better or worse, and commentators can't even agree whether Labov's own exemplar was speaking restricted or elaborated code.

*Literacy studies.* The Goody/Ong/Olson "autonomous text" thesis says written traditions push toward context-independence. Olson's version: logical connectives have diverse uses in speech, but through literacy and education they become explicit objects of thought and are formalized into logical operators as part of a literate rationality. Brian Street's rebuttal is your ally here, because it is essentially MacIntyre's point about invisible presuppositions: the autonomous model disguises the cultural and ideological assumptions underpinning it, which can then be presented as neutral and universal, and Street argued these universalist explanations privilege Western academic practices rooted in a British essayist tradition.

*Translation studies — the most directly testable strand.* Blum-Kulka's **explicitation hypothesis** claims translated texts show a higher level of cohesive explicitness, later broadened to the translator's tendency to explicate what is implicit in the source, and Klaudy's typology matters most for you: she distinguishes obligatory, optional, pragmatic, and translation-inherent explicitation, noting that some additions arise specifically to supply cultural or background knowledge. That is exactly the operation MacIntyre describes and rejects — the presupposed framework demoted to supplied background. But be warned the empirical status is shaky: Becher argues the hypothesis has grave theoretical problems, that the supporting quantitative studies don't in fact support it, and calls for abandoning the theory, and others have concluded explicitation-as-universal should be debunked given the vagueness of the concept itself.

**Where that leaves the hypothesis.** The strongest defensible version isn't "traditions put more in literal text" *tout court* — Bernstein is a real obstacle to that — but something narrower and more interesting: *contested* traditions with continuous commentarial institutions convert doxa into orthodoxy over time, producing an unusually high ratio of articulated-to-presupposed content, while an idiom that faces no serious rival has no such pressure and leaves its criteria of truth and reasonableness unwritten. That version has Bourdieu, Taylor, Asad, Henderson and Street behind it, and it's falsifiable: you could compare presupposition load across a commentarial corpus versus a modern "neutral" scholarly idiom.

### II. Other directions, by discipline

**Philosophy (directly on MacIntyre)**
- Jennifer Herdt, "Alasdair MacIntyre's 'Rationality of Traditions' and Tradition-Transcendental Standards of Justification," *Journal of Religion* 78 (1998): 524–46 — the key piece arguing MacIntyre needs standards not dependent on any particular tradition. She surveys the field, including Ian Markham's claim that MacIntyre's commitment to inter-tradition dialogue is itself indebted to liberalism, and Jeffrey Stout's observation that "castigation-by-lumping" is the main function of the label "liberalism" in MacIntyre, since not all liberalism seeks a standpoint above tradition.
- Jeffrey Stout, *Ethics After Babel* (1988) and "Homeward Bound," *Journal of Religion* 69 (1989) — the pragmatist counter-position; moral bricolage against MacIntyre's tradition-purism.
- John Haldane on the anti-relativism argument: Haldane argued one needn't assume a neutral ground from which the relativist claim is issued, and MacIntyre conceded the case against relativism in *Whose Justice?* needs amending.
- Tom Angier, "Alasdair MacIntyre's Analysis of Tradition," *European Journal of Philosophy* (2014) — the best single-article critical synthesis, with a bibliography that will save you weeks.
- Tony Burns, "MacIntyre and Hegel on the possibility of resolving philosophical disagreements" (*Philosophy & Social Criticism*, 2025) — on MacIntyre's refusal of Hegelian synthesis; notes MacIntyre's Kuhn-echoing claim that rival traditions should not be understood as incompatible ways of understanding one and the same world.
- The Davidson problem, which is the sharpest technical objection: Davidson argues that belief in non-intertranslatable languages violates a correct understanding of interpretability — if we succeed in interpreting, there's no need to speak of two schemes; if we fail, there's no ground for speaking of two. MacIntyre's response develops a distinctive account of the relation between translation and interpretation, arguing that partial failures of translatability illustrate the incommensurability he means. See also Xinli Wang's defense of conceptual schemes against Davidson.
- On MacIntyre's own worked example of cross-tradition encounter (Confucians and Aristotelians on *li*), there's a critique arguing that MacIntyre's proposed solutions to the translation problem are inadequate, and that reaching the point where two incommensurate ethical systems can actually be compared requires a very different process from the one MacIntyre envisions.

**Philosophy of science**
- Kuhn's mature position is the closest analytic analogue to MacIntyre's "second first language." In "Commensurability, Comparability, Communicability" (1982), the defense of untranslatability rests on distinguishing interpreting a theory in a later language from translating it into that language — the former possible and essential, the latter neither. Incommensurability, he stresses, means there is no language, neutral or otherwise, into which both theories can be translated without residue — but lack of common measure does not make comparison impossible.
- Mary Hesse's reply in the same volume argues Kuhn's critique of extensionalist translation must be pushed further into a theory of interpretation that addresses ostensive and analogical language learning and the pervasively metaphoric character of natural language.
- Howard Sankey's series of papers ("Translation failure between theories," "Incommensurability, translation and understanding," 1991) is the systematic treatment.
- Amani Albedah, "A Gadamerian Critique of Kuhn's Linguistic Turn" — for the hermeneutic route.

**Anthropology**
- Asad, "The Concept of Cultural Translation in British Social Anthropology," in Clifford & Marcus, *Writing Culture* (1986) — built as an extended critique of Gellner's "Concept and Society," questioning whether contextual interpretation leads to excessive charity, and arguing that the practice is institutional: scholars habitually translate field material into the terms of their own society and of the academy.
- Viveiros de Castro, "Perspectival Anthropology and the Method of Controlled Equivocation," *Tipití* 2 (2004) — the sharpest formulation of your problem: direct comparability does not necessarily signify immediate translatability, just as ontological continuity does not imply epistemological transparency; translation means situating oneself in the space of the equivocation and dwelling there — communicating by differences. Companion figures: Strathern (partial connections), Wagner, de la Cadena, Holbraad & Pedersen on the politics of ontology.
- Recent bridge work: a 2025 paper pairing Davidson's rejection of incommensurable conceptual schemes with Viveiros de Castro's translation-as-equivocation, criticizing Davidson's trouble accounting for interpretative asymmetry.

**Comparative literature / translation studies**
- Emily Apter, *Against World Literature: On the Politics of Untranslatability* (2013) — a polemic against World Literature models on the grounds that they build curricula on an assumption of translatability, leaving incommensurability insufficiently built into the literary heuristic. This is MacIntyre's "museum of texts" complaint transposed to the syllabus. Note that Apter ties untranslatability to the theological — the insistence that a sacred text not be, even cannot be, translated — and takes its sacral dimension seriously.
- Barbara Cassin (ed.), *Dictionary of Untranslatables: A Philosophical Lexicon* (Eng. 2014) — the reference work behind Apter.
- The skeptical reply matters: one reviewer holds that terms lacking one-to-one correspondences are no more untranslatable than "word" or "armchair"; David Bellos makes the same case at book length in *Is That a Fish in Your Ear?*
- Also worth pursuing: Lawrence Venuti on domestication/foreignization, Naoki Sakai on "homolingual address," Lydia Liu on translingual practice and hypothetical equivalence.

**Political theory / religion in the public sphere**
This is where MacIntyre's argument has the most direct contemporary bite, because Habermas actually institutionalized the operation MacIntyre describes. His "translation proviso" holds that religious citizens may use religious reasons in the public arena, but beyond the institutional threshold these must be translated into secular reasons if they are to justify coercion — a translation that "salvages" the relevant content and yields reasons "equally accessible to all."
- Critics: Cristina Lafont; Nicholas Wolterstorff ("readers cannot miss noticing that Habermas operates with a certain anthropological ontology"); Maeve Cooke, "Salvaging and Secularizing the Semantic Contents of Religion" (2006); and the *Review of Politics* article arguing against the proviso that religious language be translated into an allegedly neutral vocabulary as a condition of full inclusion in public reasoning.
- Directly on your text: "Jürgen Habermas's Translation of the Human Being as Created in the Image of God: Perspectives from Joseph Ratzinger and Alasdair MacIntyre," *Religions* 15/1 (2024) — argues the proviso equates secular language with philosophical atheism and is predicated on the assertion that secular communication alone is legitimate beyond the institutional threshold. Start here if you want MacIntyre applied rather than discussed.
- Also: "Taking Religion Seriously? Habermas on Religious Translation and Cooperative Learning in Post-secular Society," *APSR* (2017).

**Intellectual history / method**
- Quentin Skinner, "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas" (*History and Theory*, 1969) is the methodological twin of MacIntyre's attack on Great Books courses, written twenty years earlier. Skinner targets exactly the assumption that one can concentrate on what each classic writer said about "fundamental concepts" and "abiding questions," reappraising the classics apart from the context of historical development as perennially important attempts at universal propositions; without a properly historical method, the result is mythology, not history.
- The new critical volume — Adrian Blau (ed.), *Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas and Beyond* (open access, OAPEN) — includes a chapter you'll want: "Meaning, Context, Interpretations in the Islamic Tradition: Provincialising Skinnerian Contextualism."

**Literary studies / canon and curriculum**
- John Guillory, *Cultural Capital* (1993) — reframes the canon wars away from representation toward the distribution of cultural capital in schools, which regulate access to literacy. His reading of Gray's *Elegy* is unexpectedly close to MacIntyre: he shows the poem functioning as linguistic capital by imitating the compositional method of the commonplace book, decontextualizing and distilling literary sententiae. The 2023 *Genre* symposium reassesses it at thirty years.
- Lawrence Levine, *The Opening of the American Mind* (1996) — the historian's reply to Bloom/Bennett, whom MacIntyre also names.

**Museum studies / art history** (MacIntyre's own example)
- James Clifford, "On Collecting Art and Culture," in *The Predicament of Culture* — the "art-culture system," which explores how exotic objects have been given value and contextualized in the West, and contests the redemptive narrative underwriting appropriation.
- Carol Duncan, *Civilizing Rituals* (1995) — the museum as a space where "civilizing rituals" are enacted, with architecture, collection structure, and display shaping perception and forming the viewer's cultural identity. MacIntyre's "public buildings of educated modernity" claim in one book.
- For the current state of the debate: Wijnia & Bielo (eds.), *Museums as Ritual Sites: Civilizing Rituals Reconsidered*, which critically examines the assumption that museums inherently function as ritual sites.

**Comparative law** — a self-contained parallel debate few philosophers know about
- Pierre Legrand, "The Impossibility of Legal Transplants," *Maastricht Journal* 4 (1997): law taken from one jurisdiction and placed in another loses its proper meaning, because outside its home it cannot be interpreted correctly; rules lack meaning without their social background. Against Alan Watson, *Legal Transplants*, who documents successful borrowing as the normal mode of legal development. Gunther Teubner's "Legal Irritants: Good Faith in British Law, or How Unifying Law Ends Up in New Divergences" (*MLR* 1998) is the productive middle position — the transplanted rule neither transfers nor fails, but irritates the host into new divergence.

**Philology / area studies**
- Sheldon Pollock, *The Language of the Gods in the World of Men* (2006) and "Cosmopolitan and Vernacular in History" (*Public Culture*, 2000). This is the historical test case for MacIntyre's "internationalized languages": Pollock draws parallels with Latin and the Roman empire and with the vernacular literatures of late-medieval Europe, asking whether these histories challenge current theories of culture and power, and looks ahead to a globalizing world in which vernaculars are again yielding to the cosmopolitan. If MacIntyre is right, the Sanskrit cosmopolis should show the same neutralizing effects; Pollock's answer is more complicated than that.

### One caution about the passage itself

MacIntyre's use of Barthes is doing more work than it can bear. The *Critique et Vérité* line is a claim about *literary* works specifically — Barthes is contrasting the *oeuvre* with practically-situated utterance — and MacIntyre reads it as a general theory of the text, then treats it as unwitting testimony about what modern reading does to traditional texts. That's rhetorically effective but exegetically thin, and it's the seam where a critic would open the passage. There's surprisingly little scholarship on MacIntyre-and-Barthes specifically; it may be an open niche.

If you want, I can go deeper on any one branch — the Bourdieu/Henderson combination is the most promising route to actually building your hypothesis into an argument, and the Habermas proviso literature is where it would have the sharpest contemporary application.
