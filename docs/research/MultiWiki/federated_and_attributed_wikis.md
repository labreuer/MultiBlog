# Federated and attributed wikis: systems where several authored articles on one topic coexist by design

Scope: Smallest Federated Wiki / Federated Wiki (2011–), Google Knol (2007–2012), Everything2 / h2g2 / Scholarpedia / Stack Exchange / Quora (mechanism only), git-based and ActivityPub wikis (Gollum, TiddlyWiki, Ibis, XWiki), and Xanadu's parallel-document ideas with their implemented descendants. Dates are given wherever the source gave one. Research done 2026-09-21.

Access notes for the report writer: `fed.wiki.org`, `ward.fed.wiki.org`, `wiki.c2.com`, `proxy.c2.com` and `sustasis.net` all refused connections during this session, `news.ycombinator.com` returned HTTP 429, `wiki.p2pfoundation.net` returned 403, and `web.archive.org` is unreachable from this tool. Ward Cunningham's own fedwiki pages are therefore cited through a federated mirror (wiki.ralfbarkow.ch, which is itself a fork — see below), the GitHub READMEs, the 2013 PLoP paper, and second-hand but contemporaneous write-ups (Udell, Caulfield, Cronin).

## Key question 1 — Federated Wiki: motivation, the fork model, the neighborhood, the journal, twins, and what practice showed

### Takeaway
Federated Wiki (shown at IndieWebCamp 2011, first software release 2013-12-16) replaces "one page edited by everyone" with "one site per author, every page forkable to your site, and the journal of each page recording where it came from"; a reader sees rival versions through the *twins* line (same slug found in the sitemaps of the sites currently in their *neighborhood*). Practice (the December 2014 Fedwiki Happening, Caulfield's classrooms, Wikity 2015–16) showed vigorous forking and idea-linking among a few dozen people but also a steep learning curve, no diff/merge, no fork notification, neighborhood-bounded discoverability, and no adoption beyond the enthusiasts.

### Cited Findings

**Origin, dates, code**
- Wikipedia: Federated Wiki "was launched at IndieWebCamp in 2011, with the initial software release on December 16, 2013"; written in CoffeeScript, MIT licensed; "WikiWikiWeb began using Federated Wiki in 2015." — [Wikipedia: Federated Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_Wiki)
- IndieWeb wiki: the project "was discussed at IndieWebCamp 2011"; it "federates using fork history" so that sites find and connect with each other; it uses "a simple JSON schema and the CORS headers that enable wiki-like sharing in the browser"; there are Sinatra+Ruby and Express+Node implementations; "drag-and-drop journal merging" was added at the 2014 Portland camp. — [IndieWeb: Smallest Federated Wiki](https://indieweb.org/Smallest_Federated_Wiki)
- Ward's stated design lineage (2013 PLoP paper with Michael Mehaffy): "In the open-source software development community, a new approach developed known as federation. Typified by the 'Git' methodology of Linus Torvalds, it allowed an entire copy of a system … to be freely copied over in toto to a new site, and treated as though it were the original copy. (This is known as 'forking' a copy.) … A new generation of wiki, called 'Smallest Federated Wiki,' is now based on this federated model. The wiki can be forked to new sites, and shared with other collaborators. Thus it can not only proliferate over many individual websites, but also evolve in unique ways in response to new and local conditions. On occasion these benefits prove valuable for older versions, in which case they can be recombined through a pull request." — [Cunningham & Mehaffy, "Wiki as Pattern Language", PLoP 2013 (PDF)](https://hillside.net/plop/2013/papers/Group6/plop13_preprint_51.pdf)

**Stated motivation: the "chorus of voices" vs. the single consensus page**
- Wikipedia: "Federation supports what Cunningham has described as 'a chorus of voices' where users share content but maintain their individual perspectives"; this "contrasts with wikis such as Wikipedia that have a single article for each topic managed by consensus decision-making." — [Wikipedia: Federated Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_Wiki)
- Ward in his own words (PLoP 2013): "Of course not all knowledge is as well-established or shareable as that of an encyclopedia. There are many spheres of life where differences of perspective, valuation and judgment are important, even essential. … In this sense a 'federated' body of knowledge (along with the tool to share it) can function as a kind of 'chorus' – a larger network of voices that are not stating exactly the same thing, but that contribute, through their very diversity, to a larger whole. From that larger whole, a working consensus can emerge. Out of that consensus comes what we would commonly recognize as 'encyclopedia knowledge.'" Figure caption: "Conjectures, concerns and beliefs form a 'chorus' of information from which evaluation and consensus can produce 'encyclopedia knowledge' over time." — [PLoP 2013 PDF](https://hillside.net/plop/2013/papers/Group6/plop13_preprint_51.pdf)
- Ward's fedwiki page "chorus of voices" (read via a fork on wiki.ralfbarkow.ch) frames the design as four concerns; among its to-be-explained points: "Explain how each participant brings pages which may or may not be consulted." "Explain how copying and then modifying pages advances one's work without detracting from that already done." "Explain how success in one neighborhood will encourage participation and then success among more remote contributors." "Explain how fork history distributes credit of even small innovations." — [chorus-of-voices.json (fork on wiki.ralfbarkow.ch)](https://wiki.ralfbarkow.ch/chorus-of-voices.json); original at [ward.fed.wiki.org/view/chorus-of-voices](http://ward.fed.wiki.org/view/chorus-of-voices) (unreachable this session)
- Caulfield's summary of Ward's 2011 turn: "Ward looked at these problems in 2011 and proposed a new direction for wiki, one he termed the 'chorus of voices.' The idea of the chorus was that a wiki page's title could form a hub for a number of individual, personal takes on a single idea, the way a hashtag can form the hub of a conversation on the web." — [Caulfield, "Federated Wiki" project page](https://checkplease.neocities.org/older-projects/federated-wiki/) (as surfaced in search; the page itself is a project summary)
- Caulfield, Nov 2014, on why the single page fails early ideas: wiki is "a relentless consensus engine"; "you're arguing with pedants, and even where the conversation stays amicable, is this really how you want to start your day?"; when a contribution loses a deletion battle "your contribution disappears. It's easy to say that it's all in the revision history, but in practice what Google can't see does not exist." Federated wiki "inverts" the shared-server model: "everyone has their own server which stores the records associated with them. But the meaning is made in your browser." — [Hapgood, "Federated Education: New Directions in Digital Collaboration", 2014-11-06](https://hapgood.us/2014/11/06/federated-education-new-directions-in-digital-collaboration/)
- Hacker News commenter's compression of the model (2015 thread): "if I disagree with a page instead of editing it and starting an edit-war I can fork it." — [HN item 8983288](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=8983288) (surfaced via search; the page itself returned 429)

**The fork model**
- Udell, 2014-12-29: "In SFW there is no canonical page. We all create our own pages and edit them exclusively. But we can also copy pages from others, and make changes." The vocabulary is GitHub's — "you 'fork' a page from an 'origin site'" — but SFW "operates more loosely" with no pull-request feature. — [Udell, "Individual voices in the Federated Wiki chorus"](https://blog.jonudell.net/2014/12/29/individual-voices-in-the-federated-wiki-chorus/)
- Caulfield 2015-10-17: fedwiki means "Documents that choose proliferation over centralization. Page and paragraph level-forking." and "Github has taught a generation of programmers that copies are good, not bad, and as we noted, it's copies that are essential to the Garden." — [Hapgood, "The Garden and the Stream: A Technopastoral"](https://hapgood.us/2015/10/17/the-garden-and-the-stream-a-technopastoral/)
- During the Happening, forks were used two ways: some "included edits", others functioned "halfway between a 'like' and a bookmarking action." — [Hapgood, "Helping the Right Ideas Find One Another", 2014-12-21](https://hapgood.us/2014/12/21/helping-the-right-ideas-find-one-another-fedwiki-happening/)
- Udell 2015-01-22, describing forking as a first-class social act: forkers' "changes may or may not be noticed. If they are noticed they may or may not be merged. But they belong to the network graph that grows around the page. They are discoverable." He also notes the granularity: "every paragraph or paragraph-like thing has an ID and a history." — [Udell, "A federated Wikipedia"](https://blog.jonudell.net/2015/01/22/a-federated-wikipedia/)

**Page format and the journal (provenance)**
- The Node server README: pages are JSON "containing story items with unique identifiers and a journal documenting actions (create, add, edit, move, remove, fork) with site attribution"; the server offers a **Neighborhood** ("Displays connected wiki sites; can be seeded with `--autoseed` or `--neighbours`"), a **Sitemap**, **Twins** ("Pages appearing on multiple federated sites display 'This page is also on…' attribution"), and **Lineage** ("Tracks page origins and relationships across the federation"). Security "Defaults to read-only"; datastores are flat files or leveldb. — [GitHub: fedwiki/wiki README](https://github.com/fedwiki/wiki)
- In the client code, a paragraph dragged from another page (possibly on another site) carries its provenance into the journal: `dragAttribution = {page: $sourcePage.data().data['title']}` and, if the source is remote, `dragAttribution['site'] = $sourcePage.data().site`, then the action is recorded as `{id: item.id, type: 'add', item, after: before?.id, attribution: dragAttribution}`. — [fedwiki/wiki-client lib/refresh.coffee](https://github.com/fedwiki/wiki-client/blob/master/lib/refresh.coffee)
- Udell read fork provenance directly out of a page's journal: "Early in the life of my scratch page I forked Paul Rodwell's scratch page from fedwikihappening.rodwell.me." — [Udell, "How Federated Wiki neighborhoods grow and change", 2015-01-02](https://blog.jonudell.net/2015/01/02/how-federated-wiki-neighborhoods-grow-and-change/)

**The neighborhood (the scope within which variants are visible)**
- Udell 2015-01-02: three things add a site to your neighborhood — navigating to it, a *reference* item ("A FedWiki reference looks like a paragraph, but its blue tint signals that it's special … It also adds that page's origin site to the neighborhood"; created by dragging a page's favicon into a factory placeholder), and forking a remote page. "Sites that are within your current neighborhood are special in two ways: you can link to them by names alone (versus full URLs), and you can search them." "From each site in the neighborhood, FedWiki retrieves a summary called the *sitemap*. It is a list of all the pages on the site." Search "uses sitemaps which means that it only sees the titles and first paragraphs of the pages in your neighborhood." "A row of *flags* (icons) in the bottom right corner of the screen indicates that there are five sites in this neighborhood." The neighborhood accumulates across a browser session and does not shrink until refresh, which Bill Burcham noted breaks ordinary web-app expectations. — [Udell, 2015-01-02](https://blog.jonudell.net/2015/01/02/how-federated-wiki-neighborhoods-grow-and-change/)
- Caulfield's classroom trick for seeding a neighborhood: a shared "circle page" that "anyone can load … and see all the class activity (to be technical: it pulls class sites into their 'neighborhood')." — [Hapgood, "Using Federated Wiki in the Classroom: Getting Started", 2014-09-01](https://hapgood.us/2014/09/01/using-federated-wiki-in-the-classroom-getting-started/)

**Twins: how a reader learns that other sites hold the same title**
- Client code: for each site in the neighborhood the client scans that site's sitemap and matches `if item.slug == slug`; matches are grouped by whether the remote copy is newer, the same age, or older than the local one, rendered as flag icons plus a legend (`twins.push "#{flags.join '&nbsp;'} #{legend}"`) into `$page.find('.twins').html "<p><span>#{twins.join ", "}</span></p>"`. — [fedwiki/wiki-client lib/refresh.coffee](https://github.com/fedwiki/wiki-client/blob/master/lib/refresh.coffee)
- Caulfield 2014-09-01: "Any student or teacher can easily use the 'twins' notification up top (that part that shows links to older and newer versions) to pull up different student work on the same subject." — [Hapgood, 2014-09-01](https://hapgood.us/2014/09/01/using-federated-wiki-in-the-classroom-getting-started/)
- Identity was deliberately understated: contributors first appeared as "neutral 'flags' made of color gradients" ("Discoverable But Not Obvious"); during the Happening Alex North found the favicon upload path and people replaced gradients with photos. — [Udell, 2014-12-29](https://blog.jonudell.net/2014/12/29/individual-voices-in-the-federated-wiki-chorus/)

**What practice showed (Fedwiki Happening, Dec 17 2014 – Jan 1 2015; classrooms; Wikity)**
- Scale: planned for 15, ran with about 36 participants worldwide over 14 days; participants did daily "idea-mining", drilling "down to what the connectable *idea* of the thing is" rather than sharing links. Worked example: Kate's "Small Public Spaces" → Caulfield's Whyte video → David Jones's "Digital Renovators"/"Concrete Lounge", so "now, if you click that Concrete Lounge link you get what I think is a very relevant connection to Whyte's work." — [Hapgood, 2014-12-21](https://hapgood.us/2014/12/21/helping-the-right-ideas-find-one-another-fedwiki-happening/)
- Output and experience: "Nearly 2,000 wiki pages created in two weeks"; "Federated Wiki is hard to learn, easy to use" (Caulfield's warning) — Cronin: "I'm not sure I got to the 'easy to use' part, but I sure learned a hell of a lot"; participants "struggled with forking mechanics and when/why to fork pages"; she raised the attribution question "How important is it to know who has written a particular passage? Is this an absolute judgment or is it contextual?" — [Catherine Cronin, "Reflections on Federated Wiki Happening 2014", 2015-01-01](https://catherinecronin.net/reflecting/fedwiki2014/)
- Udell: the system "lacks key mechanisms for orientation and navigation", some of which emerged ad hoc (photo flags, the fedwiki.org/trending.html "conversation club" view); the open question is "whether collaborative work can thrive in a federated system that favors the chorus over the individual voice." — [Udell, 2014-12-29](https://blog.jonudell.net/2014/12/29/individual-voices-in-the-federated-wiki-chorus/)
- Udell/Neylon, Jan 2015: FedWiki "lacks effective diff/merge capability" and "lacks notification that your page was forked" — gaps he proposed filling with Webmention-style protocols. Caulfield's proposed reader affordance for a federated Wikipedia: "shift hover, or click or something and get a list of alternates" for contested paragraphs. — [Udell, "A federated Wikipedia", 2015-01-22 and comments](https://blog.jonudell.net/2015/01/22/a-federated-wikipedia/)
- Adoption: Caulfield 2015-10-17: federated wiki "made zero sense to people even two years ago, but I can feel a sea change now when I describe it"; "I'm still starting the ball from the back of the field, but at least I'm on the field." — [Hapgood, 2015-10-17](https://hapgood.us/2015/10/17/the-garden-and-the-stream-a-technopastoral/)
- Wikity (Caulfield's WordPress re-implementation of the forkable-card idea, announced 2015-12-09) was pitched as combining "the individual control of blogging with the permissionless improvement of wiki"; blogs "tend towards conversational and quotative reuse … Wiki feeds forward into a consensus process … at the expense of personal control." — [Hapgood, "Introducing Wikity"](https://hapgood.us/2015/12/09/introducing-wikity/). One year later (2016-11-20): "after a year and about 300(!) hours of work, is something I love and I use every day" (2,000+ cards), but "I have to admit, I thought early on that there would be larger appetite for Wikity"; testers found it "easy to use, valuable, cool — and completely non-addictive"; hosting shifted from a central wikity.cc toward "an aggregation site" over independent installs. — [Hapgood, "Wikity, One Year Later"](https://hapgood.us/2016/11/20/wikity-one-year-later/)
- Classroom usability details: pages with unsaved changes show a yellow border; students must be told how to "fork the page to the server to save your offline edits" and to check login status. — [Hapgood, 2014-09-01](https://hapgood.us/2014/09/01/using-federated-wiki-in-the-classroom-getting-started/)
- A widely-quoted criticism from Caulfield's federated-wiki writing (surfaced by search, exact post not verified this session): the interface "really needs to be improved, as complex network structures are forced down to a linear sequence of pages with almost all of the underlying structure hidden from the user, causing confusion among first-time users." — [Hapgood federated-wiki tag](https://hapgood.us/tag/federated-wiki/)

### Inferences
- Fedwiki's answer to "several perspectives under one title" is *not* a hub page listing variants; it is a per-viewer, per-session computation: the same slug is looked up in the sitemaps of whatever sites the reader has wandered into (or been seeded with). Discoverability is therefore bounded by the neighborhood, which is why Caulfield needed a "circle page" and Udell wanted a trending view — the design has no global index by construction.
- Provenance is carried in the page (journal actions with `site` attribution, and `attribution` on dragged paragraphs), not in a central graph; that makes credit survive across servers but leaves "who forked me" unanswered — hence the missing-notification complaint.
- Comparison is at the granularity of *versions of one lineage* (older/newer twins, journal replay), not semantic diff; the platform assumes readers will open the twin and eyeball it. This is the mechanism the fedwiki community itself identified as missing (diff/merge), and the one a new system can most obviously improve on.
- The paragraph-with-ID data model is what makes paragraph-level forking, drag-and-drop with attribution, and Caulfield's imagined "list of alternates per paragraph" feasible; a system that stores whole-page text cannot offer any of these.

### Gaps
- Ward Cunningham's own spoken explanations (InfoQ interview April 2013; QCon London 2013 keynote "A Forward Look at Federated Wiki", July 2013; TEDxPortland 2012; Splash 2014 notes on sustasis.net) could not be transcribed: InfoQ pages hold only metadata, sustasis.net refused connection. The PLoP 2013 paper is the only first-person written source obtained.
- Ward's HN comments (threads 8983288 from 2015 and 12715927 from 2016) were rate-limited (429).
- fed.wiki.org's own documentation (the "How to Wiki" pages, the JSON schema page) was unreachable; the JSON format description here comes from the GitHub README and client code.
- No quantitative adoption figures for federated wiki beyond the Happening's ~36 people / ~2,000 pages were found.

## Key question 2 — Google Knol (2007–2012): many knols per topic, named authors, ratings, moderated collaboration, and why it failed

### Takeaway
Knol (announced 2007-12-13, public 2008-07-23, shutdown announced 2011-11-22, closed 2012-04-30/05-01, content export until 2012-10-01) made competing authored articles on one topic an explicit design goal ("Competition of ideas is a good thing"), with real-name authorship, three per-article collaboration modes (open / moderated / closed), reader ratings and reviews, and AdSense revenue share; critics from launch onward said it dumped the synthesis burden on readers, killed micro-contributions, let articles rot with their owner, and rewarded spam — it plateaued at ~175k–260k monthly visitors and ~100k+ (later 700k metadata records) knols.

### Cited Findings

**Design as announced**
- Udi Manber (VP Engineering), Dec 2007: "The key idea behind the Knol project is to highlight authors." "For many topics, there will likely be competing Knols on the same subject. Competition of ideas is a good thing." "Google will not edit Knol in any way or bless any of the content." "We hope that Knols will include the opinions and points of view of the authors who will put their reputation on the line." Google promised a "substantial revenue share" from ads if the author opted in, and Web 2.0 tools for readers to "submit comments, questions, edits and additional content." — [Computerworld, 2007-12-14](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1569371/google-launches-its-own-wikipedia-with-a-twist.html)
- From the official announcement as quoted: "Our goal is to encourage people who know a particular subject to write an authoritative article about it." "We believe that knowing who wrote what will significantly help users make better use of web content." "Once testing is completed, participation in knols will be completely open, and we cannot expect that all of them will be of high quality. Our job in Search Quality will be to rank the knols appropriately when they appear in Google search results." Community could rate, review, and suggest edits. — [Google Operating System blog, 2007-12-14, quoting Manber](http://googlesystem.blogspot.com/2007/12/google-knol-encyclopedia-written-by.html)
- Wikipedia's summary: "any contributor could create and own new Knol articles, and there could be multiple articles on the same topic with each written by a different author"; contributions required real names via Google accounts; authors could opt into AdSense; licence choices were CC-BY-3.0 (default), CC-BY-NC-3.0, or all-rights-reserved. Timeline: announced 2007-12-13; launched 2008-07-23 with "hundreds of health/medical articles"; closure announced Nov 2011; closed 2012-04-30; content deleted 2012-10-01. — [Wikipedia: Knol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knol)
- Collaboration modes: Google introduced "moderated collaboration" so that "any reader [can] make suggested edits that the author may choose to accept, reject, or modify before these contributions become visible to the public"; authors could also open a knol to "open collaboration" (Wikipedia-style) or keep it closed. — search summary of Google's Knol help/announcements, corroborated by Tim Anderson, who on 2008-07-24 "opened my Visual Basic Knol to 'open collaboration' – just like wikipedia" and had a commenter test the revision tracking — [Tim Anderson, "Open collaboration on Knol"](https://www.itwriting.com/blog/753-open-collaboration-on-knol.html)
- Later feature: "Knol's moderated collection feature allowed designated editors to manage expert review processes" (search summary; see Annotum below for where that went). — [search result set; Annotum at JATS-Con 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK63828/)

**Contemporary critiques of the competing-articles design**
- GroupLens (Reid), 2007-12-15, two days after the announcement: "It is left to the reader to make sense of the several competing articles on a particular topic." "It's impossible to make a tiny contribution (e.g. fixing a typo)." "If an author loses interest in an article, it's difficult or impossible for others to take over and continue work." Ratings and suggestions exist but "the owner retains sole editorial control." — [GroupLens, "Underwhelmed by Google's Knol"](https://grouplens.org/blog/underwhelmed-by-googles-knol/)
- danah boyd, 2008-08-01: "Knol's entire structure is built around single authors, control and individualism"; collaboration tools "extremely limited"; "a system that is driven by individualism quickly becomes a tool for self-promoters"; "collective creations tend to result in much better content than that which is created by an individual hermit"; content is "being forgotten and left to rot", "a lot more crap than gems", "no common format or standards"; most promoted knols were health-related with suspected plagiarism; grade "D", "abysmal failure." — [danah boyd, "knol: content w/out context, collaboration, capital, or coruscation"](https://www.zephoria.org/thoughts/archives/2008/08/01/knol_content_wo.html)
- Plagiarism Today, 2008-09-23: "Since articles cannot be easily edited, people tend to upload their own articles rather than try to build off ones that exist, this leads to topics with dozens of competing articles." "In order to edit an existing Knol, you have to get approval from the original author, this reduces community involvement and puts anonymous editors at the mercy of the 'experts' that wrote the article." Ad revenue "attracted people with bad intentions"; "Quantity over Quality"; "Even if the theory worked perfectly, most of the content on Knol would be complete and utter garbage." — [Plagiarism Today, "Why Google Knol Has Failed"](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2008/09/23/why-google-knol-has-failed/)
- Gwern (2009, updated to 2011): searching one prolific author's name "brings up 809 entries; since most Knol users appear to write their own entries rather than add to others (for which no compensation is forthcoming), the proliferation of entries is inevitable." "paying only some contributors can 'crowd out' other unpaid contributions." Knol had ~100,000 articles at an age when Wikipedia had ~6,000, yet Wikipedia's count "more than doubled to >13,000" five months later while Knol stalled. October 2011 sample: only 10 knols edited over half a day (≈168 active users); traffic ~260k monthly visitors. — [Gwern, "Wikipedia & Knol: Why Knol Already Failed"](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol)

**Outcome**
- Traffic "about 175,000 views a month" by mid-2009; "Google stopped promoting Knol"; TechCrunch's writer said the shutdown "came as a surprise to me – because I figured Google had already shut it down." — [Wikipedia: Knol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knol); [TechCrunch, 2011-11-22](https://techcrunch.com/2011/11/22/google-announces-plans-to-shutter-knol-friend-connect-and-more/)
- Archive Team: "2009-01-16: 100,000th knol published"; archive holds "700,000+ knols metadata"; "Offline since 2012-05-01"; "After May 1, you will no longer be able to create, view, enter or edit knols, but you will be able to export your knols to WordPress.com and download them to file through October 1st, 2012." — [Archive Team: Knol](https://wiki.archiveteam.org/index.php/Knol)
- Exit path: Google worked with Solvitor and Crowd Favorite on Annotum, "an open-source platform based upon WordPress that allows you to continue authoring and publishing scholarly articles" — i.e. the authored-article-with-review model survived as a single-author WordPress theme rather than as a multi-article encyclopedia. — [Archive Team: Knol](https://wiki.archiveteam.org/index.php/Knol); [Annotum launch post, 2011-11-22](https://annotum.wordpress.com/2011/11/22/launch/)

### Inferences
- Knol had *no comparison mechanism at all* between competing knols beyond search ranking and star ratings: the announcement's own plan was "rank the knols appropriately when they appear in Google search results." Every critic's complaint (reader burden, dozens of near-duplicates) follows from ranking being the only synthesis layer.
- The three collaboration modes are a per-article gate on write access, not a fork model: a rejected suggestion vanished, and there was no way to publish your version *as a variant linked to the original*. Combined with ad revenue that accrued only to owners, this pushed people to start fresh articles, which is exactly the proliferation Gwern measured.
- Knol demonstrates the failure mode of "multiple attributed articles" when the system provides neither a lineage relation between them nor a curated hub: the reader sees a search-results list, not a chorus.

### Gaps
- The original Google blog posts (2007-12-13, 2008-07-23, 2011-11-22) could not be fetched in full (the blogspot pages render only navigation; archive.org is unreachable); the Manber quotes above come from Computerworld and Google Operating System's contemporaneous reporting.
- No Larry Sanger commentary specifically on Knol was found; searches returned only his 2008 Citizendium material. Treat "Sanger on Knol" as unsourced.
- No primary Knol help-centre text on the exact semantics of "open / moderated / closed" was retrieved; the description rests on Wikipedia, Anderson's 2008 test, and search summaries.

## Key question 3 — Everything2, h2g2, Scholarpedia, Stack Exchange, Quora: the mechanism of "several attributed entries under one title"

### Takeaway
Everything2 (1999–) is the cleanest instance of the mechanism: a *node* is a title, and any number of *writeups* by different authors hang under it, each editable only by its author, ranked by votes and editor "cools"; Stack Exchange (2008–) and Quora do the same under a question, with a single accepted answer (SE) or one-answer-per-account (Quora) as the only synthesis; h2g2 and Scholarpedia instead converge on one edited/curated entry with named authorship.

### Cited Findings
- Everything2: "E2 users called *noders* create entries called *nodes* and add information in multiple *writeups*"; only logged-in users can author; "only the author of a writeup or an editor appointed by the site administrators can edit a writeup"; thirteen writeup types (person, place, idea, thing, dream, personal, fiction, poetry, review, log, recipe, essay, event); "Every time a user creates a writeup, he or she earns five XP"; "A positive vote grants the writeup's author one experience point while also having a roughly one-third chance of giving one GP to the voter"; the "cool" ("C!") "rewards the author with 20 XP and sends the writeup to the 'cool user picks' column on the front page"; "The site's editors may remove writeups that do not meet editorial standards from public view" (to the author's drafts, not deleted). Launched 1999; new writeups fell from "9976 … in the month of August 2000" to "93 … in February 2017." — [Wikipedia: Everything2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everything2)
- The editing paradigm "prioritizes non-revertible additions, prohibiting users from altering or deleting others' writeups to preserve historical contributions and encourage additive collaboration." — [search summary drawing on E2's own policy pages](https://everything2.com/title/Writeup)
- h2g2 (founded 1999-04-28 by Douglas Adams; BBC Jan 2001 – 2011-10-03; Not Panicking Ltd from 2011-10-16): two tiers — the open Guide of contributor entries and "an 'Edited Guide' being steadily created out of factual articles that have been peer reviewed"; "Researchers retain the copyright to their articles, but grant Not Panicking Ltd a non-exclusive licence." — [Wikipedia: h2g2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H2g2)
- Scholarpedia: "Each article has a single Curator who is ultimately responsible for the article's contents"; "For a revision to become visible in an article, it must either be explicitly or implicitly approved by the article curator"; rationale: "if an article can have multiple curators, then responsibility for the article's upkeep is diluted." — [Scholarpedia: Curator](http://www.scholarpedia.org/article/Scholarpedia:Curator); [Help: Curators](http://www.scholarpedia.org/article/Help:Curators)
- Stack Overflow (founded 2008-09-15): users "vote questions and answers up or down similar to Reddit"; "10 reputation points for receiving an 'up' vote"; "Users unlock new privileges with an increase in reputation like the ability to vote, comment, and even edit other people's posts." — [Wikipedia: Stack Overflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_Overflow). Only one answer can be accepted per question; the accepted answer is pinned first with a green marker; research questions whether the asker's choice tracks the popular vote. — [search summary incl. IBM Research paper on voting vs. acceptance](https://research.ibm.com/publications/the-synergy-between-voting-and-acceptance-of-answers-on-stackoverflow-or-the-lack-thereof)
- Caulfield's framing of these as "choral explanations" (2016-05-13): unlike older Q&A sites, on Stack Exchange/Quora "individual control of writing is preserved, and multiple unique passes at a subject are appreciated"; multiple answers "combine to push me to a deep understanding no single explanation can" and "give me multiple routes into the content" — some "above your head, and some ridiculously simplified. Some exercise metaphorical thinking, others dive into math." He credits Ward's "chorus of voices" and proposes fly-out buttons "Show me more: Explanations, Examples, Activities." — [Hapgood, "Choral Explanations"](https://hapgood.us/2016/05/13/choral-explanations/)
- Quora: one answer per question per (non-anonymous) account; answers ranked by an opaque helpfulness algorithm; duplicate questions are merged ("a particular question only exists once … good writers don't want to answer the same question over and over"). — [Quora blog, "Introducing Question Merging"](https://quorablog.quora.com/Introducing-Question-Merging); [Quora help threads](https://www.quora.com/How-does-the-ranking-of-answers-on-Quora-work)

### Inferences
- All the working "many entries under one title" systems share three properties: a title-level container distinct from the entries; entries that are *append-only from others' point of view* (author-only editing); and a ranking layer (votes, cools, acceptance) as the sole comparison aid. None offers diff or cross-linking between entries — the entries are siblings, not versions.
- E2's decline and Knol's collapse contrast with SE's persistence; the difference is that a *question* naturally invites several answers, while an encyclopedic *topic* invites one, so readers of E2/Knol experienced multiplicity as duplication.

### Gaps
- No source was found on how Everything2 *orders* writeups within a node (by reputation? date?) or on "nodeshells"; the Wikipedia article doesn't say and everything2.com's policy pages were not fetched.
- h2g2's handling of two Edited-Guide entries on the same topic (whether allowed) was not established.

## Key question 4 — Git-based and federated/branching wikis: Gollum, TiddlyWiki, Ibis, other ActivityPub wikis

### Takeaway
Git-backed wikis (Gollum, 2009–) inherit branches but do not surface them as forks of a page in the UI; TiddlyWiki "federation" is tiddler import/fetch between wikis rather than tracked forks; Ibis (announced 2024-03-14, by the Lemmy developer) is the one ActivityPub wiki with explicit semantics — each article has one origin instance, other instances follow and mirror it, remote edits are diffs sent to the origin and rejected on conflict, and the *same title on two instances is deliberately two different articles* with no linkage.

### Cited Findings
- Gollum: "wiki software that uses Git as the backend storage mechanism"; "Gollum wikis are simply Git repositories that adhere to a specific format"; full revision history is inherent; GitLab uses it but plans "a complete move away from Gollum"; released Feb 2009, v6.1.0 on 2024-12-23, MIT. The Wikipedia article "does not explicitly detail whether branches or forks of pages are exposed in the user interface." — [Wikipedia: Gollum (software)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gollum_(software)). Gollum lets you "specify the git branch to serve, with the default being main," i.e. one branch is served at a time. — [GitHub: gollum/gollum](https://github.com/gollum/gollum/)
- TiddlyWiki: "The primitive operation underlying federation is the ability to pull in tiddlers from an external wiki, potentially with the ability to transform them as they are imported"; Node's `fetch` command imports tiddlers "selected by a filter" and can transform titles; a postMessage/iframe scheme bundles tiddlers from a remote wiki; TW5-Bob supports "inter-server federation where different Bob servers can communicate to share tiddlers." — [TiddlyWiki5 discussion #5908](https://github.com/TiddlyWiki/TiddlyWiki5/discussions/5908); [TiddlyWiki group thread](https://groups.google.com/d/topic/tiddlywiki/09PvFOgCKC4); [OokTech/TW5-Bob](https://github.com/OokTech/TW5-Bob)
- Ibis (announced 2024-03-14): "Each article belongs to a single origin instance, the one where it was originally created." Other instances follow it; a remote edit sends "an activity `Update/Edit` … to the origin instance. If the diff applies cleanly, the origin instance sends the new text in an `Update/Article` activity to its followers." "In case there is a conflict, a `Reject` activity is sent back, the editor needs to resolve and resubmit the edit." On multiplicity: "The same topic can be treated in completely different ways. For example `geology.wiki/article/Mountain` may be completely different from `poetry.wiki/article/Mountain`." A commenter: "so, wait, articles are considered accounts?" — [SocialHub announcement](https://socialhub.activitypub.rocks/t/announcing-ibis-the-federated-wikipedia-alternative/3997)
- Ibis README: "Edits are stored as diffs which means that conflicts can be resolved through merges, similar to git"; "Federation ensures that articles get mirrored across many servers, and can be read even if the original instance goes down"; Rust + WebAssembly + PostgreSQL; AGPL-3.0; no forking feature documented. — [GitHub: Nutomic/ibis](https://github.com/Nutomic/ibis)
- Ibis 0.3.0: "When creating a new article, users can choose which instance it should reside on"; "Ibis can finally federate with other Fediverse platforms such as Lemmy"; OAuth; admins can remove articles. — [Lemmy: Ibis 0.3.0](https://lemmy.ml/post/30535155). Ibis 0.3.2: per-URL and bulk Wikipedia import (`/api/v1/article/import`), UI translation via Weblate. — [Lemmy: Ibis 0.3.2](https://lemmy.ml/post/46272156)
- Other ActivityPub wiki: XWiki's ActivityPub Application "aims at allowing to connect XWiki with other instances of the fediverse", making users reachable by other services; nothing about page forking across instances. — [XWiki ActivityPub Application](https://extensions.xwiki.org/xwiki/bin/view/Extension/ActivityPub%20Application/)

### Inferences
- Ibis is the mirror-image of fedwiki: fedwiki has *no* canonical page and many copies; Ibis has *exactly one* canonical copy per article and many read-only mirrors, with git-style diff/merge on the single lineage. Its "same title, different instance" story is deliberate namespace separation, not a chorus — there is no twins-style indicator, no fork lineage between geology.wiki/Mountain and poetry.wiki/Mountain.
- Git-backed wikis show that having branches in storage does not give readers variant discovery: without a UI that lists sibling branches of a page, the mechanism is invisible.

### Gaps
- No ActivityPub or IPFS wiki that exposes *forks of a page* as a first-class, linked object was found; searches for such a thing returned only XWiki's federation extension and Ibis. Everipedia (2017–, blockchain fork of Wikipedia) was not researched.
- Obsidian/Roam were not searched; the premise that they lack a fork model is unverified here.
- Ibis's full changelog (ibis.wiki/article/Ibis_Changelog) was not fetched; later versions may have added features not reflected above.

## Key question 5 — Xanadu: parallel documents, transclusion, side-by-side comparison, and implemented descendants

### Takeaway
Nelson's "transpointing windows" (concept 1965, first interactive implementation by John Walker at Autodesk 1988, demo 1998) show two parallel documents side by side with visible bands connecting shared or linked passages; the distinction he insists on is that transclusion connects *the same* content in two places while links connect *different* things. Implemented descendants that show two texts in parallel with drawn connections are Ka-Ping Yee's PYXI viewer over Udanax Green (1999), XanaduSpace (2007), and OpenXanadu (2014, a document built from excerpts of eight sources with each excerpt visibly connected to its origin).

### Cited Findings
- "Parallel documents are everywhere, but are not generally acknowledged" (translations, commentaries, versions). Transpointing windows were "first published in our 1965 paper"; "We believe that the first functioning interactive screen implementation of transpointing windows was created by John Walker at Autodesk in 1988"; a 1998 demonstration by Nelson and Ian Heath showed "windows move and scroll, with connections following the content as it moves on the screen." "Transclusions are not copies and they are not instances, but the same thing knowably and visibly in more than once place." The two connection types: "connections between things which are different, and connections between things which are the same." Links survive editing: "link 2053 remains attached to all of these surviving characters in any new version." Implementations: Udanax Green and PYXI (open-sourced 1999); Ka-Ping Yee's PYXI viewer showed transclusions by colour highlighting on drafts of the Declaration of Independence. — [Nelson, "Xanalogical Structure, Needed Now More Than Ever"](https://xanadu.com.au/ted/XUsurvey/xuDation.html)
- Xanadu's literary constructs "include parallel hypertext (showable in transpointing windows) and showing links and transclusions (appearing as bands stretching between parallel documents and versions)"; "two forms of visible cross-connection between parallel documents and versions: re-use (trackable automatically) and user-created links." Nelson's Little Transquoter "creates a new format built on portion addresses from Web pages where each portion remains click-connected to its original context." — [Wikipedia: Transclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transclusion); [Wikipedia: Project Xanadu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Xanadu)
- Timeline: Udanax source released 1998–99; XanaduSpace 1.0 in 2007; OpenXanadu 2014 "a working deliverable" (not fully open source); the tumbler addressing scheme (Gregory, Miller) lets "any content portion be referenced with metadata about author, version, and associated links." — [Wikipedia: Project Xanadu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Xanadu)
- OpenXanadu (implemented by Nicholas Levin, April 2014; publicised June 2014): "you see a central xanadoc which is built of excerpts from other documents (sourcedocs), and each excerpt is visibly connected to its source"; the demo is "a simple document created using quoted sections from eight other works, including the King James Bible and the Wikipedia page on Steady State Theory." Nelson's stated aim: "an entire form of literature where links do not break as versions change; where documents may be closely compared side by side and closely annotated; where it is possible to see the origins of every quotation." — [Slashdot, 2014-06-06](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/14/06/06/1757210/xanadu-software-released-after-54-years-in-the-making); [search summary of xanadu.com demo pages](https://xanadu.com/xUniverse-D6)

### Inferences
- Xanadu is the only design in this survey whose *primary* reading surface is comparison: two perspectives side by side with the shared spans drawn between them. Fedwiki's twins and Knol's rankings answer "are there other versions?"; Xanadu answers "which parts do they share and where do they diverge?" — which is what a multi-author publishing tool with quote-anchored comments is closest to needing.
- Nelson's persistence of links across versions ("remains attached to all surviving characters") is the same problem as anchoring a comment to text that moves; his solution (stable content addresses rather than positional offsets) is the transclusion-based strategy, distinct from the search-and-rederive strategy.

### Gaps
- The OpenXanadu demo page itself (xanadu.com/xanademos/MoeJusteOrigins.html) returned only a "sources only" stub; the description above is from Slashdot and search summaries.
- No source was found for a *wiki* that adopted transpointing windows for comparing rival articles; the descendants are Xanadu's own demos.

## Key question 6 — What each system does for comparison between parallel versions

### Takeaway
Only Xanadu draws the relationship between two texts; fedwiki offers per-lineage version navigation (twins older/newer, journal replay, drag-to-merge paragraphs) but no diff; Knol, Everything2 and Stack Exchange offer ranking only; Ibis offers git-style diff *within* one article's single lineage and nothing across instances.

### Cited Findings (cross-reference table; each cell's source is cited above)
- **Federated Wiki** — variant discovery: twins line computed from neighborhood sitemaps by slug, grouped newer/same/older ([wiki-client refresh.coffee](https://github.com/fedwiki/wiki-client/blob/master/lib/refresh.coffee)); provenance: journal actions with site attribution, `attribution` on dragged paragraphs ([fedwiki/wiki README](https://github.com/fedwiki/wiki)); merging: "drag-and-drop journal merging" (2014) ([IndieWeb](https://indieweb.org/Smallest_Federated_Wiki)); diff: "lacks effective diff/merge capability", "lacks notification that your page was forked" ([Udell 2015-01-22](https://blog.jonudell.net/2015/01/22/a-federated-wikipedia/)); proposed but unbuilt: per-paragraph "list of alternates" (Caulfield comment, same post).
- **Knol** — discovery: search ranking ("rank the knols appropriately when they appear in Google search results" — [Google Operating System 2007-12-14](http://googlesystem.blogspot.com/2007/12/google-knol-encyclopedia-written-by.html)) and star ratings/reviews ([Wikipedia: Knol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knol)); no lineage between competing knols; no diff; critics: "left to the reader to make sense of the several competing articles" ([GroupLens 2007](https://grouplens.org/blog/underwhelmed-by-googles-knol/)).
- **Everything2** — sibling writeups under one node, votes and C! ranking, editor removal to drafts ([Wikipedia: Everything2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everything2)); no diff, no cross-links generated by the system.
- **Stack Exchange / Quora** — vote ordering plus one accepted answer (SE) / one answer per account and question merging (Quora) ([Wikipedia: Stack Overflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_Overflow); [Quora: question merging](https://quorablog.quora.com/Introducing-Question-Merging)); no diff.
- **Scholarpedia / h2g2** — convergence to one curated/edited entry, so no parallel versions to compare ([Scholarpedia: Curator](http://www.scholarpedia.org/article/Scholarpedia:Curator); [Wikipedia: h2g2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H2g2)).
- **Ibis** — one origin per article, diffs applied at the origin, `Reject` on conflict; same title on another instance is unrelated ([SocialHub announcement 2024-03-14](https://socialhub.activitypub.rocks/t/announcing-ibis-the-federated-wikipedia-alternative/3997)).
- **Gollum / TiddlyWiki** — git history / tiddler import; no variant UI ([Wikipedia: Gollum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gollum_(software)); [TiddlyWiki discussion #5908](https://github.com/TiddlyWiki/TiddlyWiki5/discussions/5908)).
- **Xanadu** — transpointing windows with bands between parallel documents and versions; transclusion vs link; links persist across versions ([Nelson, Xanalogical Structure](https://xanadu.com.au/ted/XUsurvey/xuDation.html); [Slashdot 2014](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/14/06/06/1757210/xanadu-software-released-after-54-years-in-the-making)).

### Inferences
- The two successful-in-practice patterns are opposite corners: rank siblings (SE) or fork with provenance (fedwiki). Nobody has shipped the combination the fedwiki community asked for in January 2015 — variants of one lineage surfaced *per paragraph*, with a diff and a fork-notification channel — and Xanadu's visible-connection rendering is the closest existing picture of what the reading side of that would look like.
- For a multi-author publishing system with stable paragraph IDs and quote-anchored comments, the cheap wins these systems point to are: (a) a twins-style "this passage/page also exists on…" computed from lineage rather than from a browsing session; (b) journal-style provenance stored *in* the forked artifact; (c) a side-by-side view that draws shared spans, which the fedwiki JSON model would have permitted and never got.

### Gaps
- No usability study or measured reader outcome comparing "many attributed articles" against "one consensus article" was found for any system; all outcome evidence is anecdotal (blog retrospectives) or traffic counts.
