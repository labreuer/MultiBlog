# Argument-mapping, debate and deliberation systems (the "debate wiki" lineage), 1970–2026

Scope: how each system represents positions/viewpoints, labels and attributes them, lets a reader compare them, what moderation/curation it used, and what became of it. Dates given wherever a source states one. Where a fact came from a search-engine summary rather than a page I could open, that is said explicitly.

## Key question 1 — Rittel's IBIS and its software descendants (gIBIS, QuestMap, Compendium, Cohere)

### Takeaway
IBIS is a four-node grammar (Question → Idea → Pro/Con, with anything questionable) whose fifty-year software lineage converged on one lesson: the representation is trivial to learn but expensive to *capture*, so it survived only where a trained facilitator drives it live in meetings (Dialogue Mapping / Compendium); every attempt to make asynchronous, unfacilitated crowds keep such structure has either simplified the model (Cohere) or stalled. The software line itself is dormant since 2014.

### Cited Findings
- IBIS originated with Kunz & Rittel (1970) and Rittel (1972) as an argumentation scheme for "wicked" planning problems; Conklin's Dialogue Mapping text reproduces the core rules: maps "generally start with Questions", "Ideas respond to one and only one Question", "Ideas are neutral", and "The place for rationale, opinion, facts, data, rhetoric, etc. is in the Pros and Cons of IBIS, generically known as Arguments." A Decision "is not really a separate element in IBIS" but an Idea marked as the answer; "anything in the map – a Question, an Idea, a Pro, a Con – can be questioned." — [Conklin, *IBIS: A Tool for All Reasons* (CogNexus)](https://www.cognexus.org/IBIS-A_Tool_for_All_Reasons.pdf)
- Conklin on the notation's limits and social requirement: arguments "are the place – indeed, the only place – in the IBIS notation for opinion, clever rhetoric, and hand waving"; and "knowing the rules of poker doesn't make you a good poker player. The trick to using IBIS as a facilitation notation is fluency … Seeing the 'deep structure' of a conversation on the fly takes practice." Notes (neutral information) and References (links) are the only other node types. — [Conklin, CogNexus](https://www.cognexus.org/IBIS-A_Tool_for_All_Reasons.pdf)
- gIBIS was built in the mid-1980s by Jeff Conklin's team at MCC as a hypertext tool for capturing software design rationale; QuestMap was its commercial successor from Corporate Memory Systems; Compendium came from Selvin/Sierhuis at NYNEX plus Buckingham Shum at the Open University's KMi, "rooted in Rittel's wicked problem conceptualization and IBIS approach." — [search summary citing Buckingham Shum et al.](https://oro.open.ac.uk/3032/), [Compendium (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compendium_(software))
- Compendium's data model: ten default node types — question, answer, list view, map view, pro, con, note, decision, reference, argument — and three relationship kinds: associative, transclusive, categorical (tags). It supports transclusion of one node into many maps, keyword tagging, Derby or MySQL storage. Source released under LGPL 13 Jan 2009; final release 2.1.3 on 21 Jan 2014; "remains downloadable but is no longer actively maintained"; a CompendiumNG fork was started in 2012. — [Compendium (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compendium_(software)); [Compendium Institute support page](http://www.compendiuminstitute.org/support/)
- Buckingham Shum & Hammond (1994), "Argumentation-based design rationale: what use at what cost?", IJHCS 40(4): research into semiformal argumentation "has failed to substantiate convincingly either of the two claims" (that expressing rationale as argumentation is useful, and that designers can use such notations), while noting "emerging indications" that it helps "certain kinds of design reasoning by turning the representational effort to the designer's advantage." — [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581984710299)
- The lineage's retrospective ("Hypermedia support for argumentation-based rationale: 15 years on from gIBIS and QOC") describes "the challenge of negotiating the overheads of capturing this form of rationale, with approaches maintaining a strong emphasis on keeping the representational scheme as simple as possible to enable real time meeting mediation and capture." — [search summary of Buckingham Shum et al.](https://oro.open.ac.uk/3032/) (the page itself returned 403 to me)
- A practitioner reading of that retrospective: tools evolved toward "less formality, resulting in simpler structures"; "the 'hard' stuff is left for experts"; Compendium's strength lies "not in how knowledge is represented … but in the social processes around how the tool is used", requiring "an expert in knowledge management" to guide in real time; participants needed "more immediate value" for any time investment. — [mccricks, "20+ years on from gIBIS and QOC" (2011)](https://mccricks.wordpress.com/2011/12/02/20-years-on-from-gibis-and-qoc/)
- Cohere (Buckingham Shum, COMMA 2008, Toulouse, 28–30 May 2008) reframed argument mapping as "social bookmarking, idea-linking, and argument visualization": *Ideas* linked by *Connections* whose link labels are user-definable and grouped into positive / neutral / negative valences; websites/clips attached as evidence; explicitly aimed at "sharing—and contesting—interpretations" across distributed teams and at reconciling "Web 2.0" norms with argumentation research. — [Buckingham Shum, "Cohere: Towards Web 2.0 Argumentation" (blog, 3 Mar 2008)](https://simon.buckinghamshum.net/2008/03/cohere-towards-web2-argumentation/); [paper PDF](https://oro.open.ac.uk/10421/1/Cohere.COMMA2008.pdf)
- Buckingham Shum (COMMA 2010) proposed software agents to reduce the human cost of argument mapping. — [Software agents in support of human argument mapping (PDF)](https://oro.open.ac.uk/22344/1/BuckinghamShum-COMMA2010.pdf) (title/abstract only; the PDF did not open for me)

### Inferences
- The IBIS lineage's stable product is a *meeting record*, not a *reader-facing encyclopedia*: every success story involves a "chauffeur" building the map live, and the model that stuck (Q/Idea/Pro/Con) is the smallest that still distinguishes "an answer" from "a reason for an answer." A publishing system that wants positions-with-reasons can safely borrow exactly that split — positions are neutral answers to a question; arguments are the only place for advocacy — and should expect capture cost, not reader confusion, to be the constraint.
- Cohere's move from a fixed grammar to user-labelled links with only a *valence* fixed (positive/neutral/negative) is the lineage's own admission that fixed link ontologies don't survive contact with volunteers.

### Gaps
- I could not open the 15-years-on paper (403) or Conklin's industrial case study chapter (Springer login), so I have no primary numbers on how many maps/meetings the industrial Dialogue Mapping deployments produced or how often maps were re-read later.
- No source found stating when Cohere went offline or what happened to its data.

## Key question 2 — Debate wikis and pro/con trees: Debatepedia, Debate.org, Wikidebate, Argüman, Kialo, DebateGraph, TruthMapping, Rationale, Argunet, AIF

### Takeaway
Almost every public "debate wiki" uses a *binary* labelling of sides (yes/no, pro/con, support/attack) attached to a root question or thesis and recursed down a tree; the ones that survived (Kialo, DebateGraph, ProCon) either paid moderators/editors or were absorbed by an institution, while volunteer-wiki variants (Debatepedia, Wikidebate, Argüman, Debate.org) went read-only or offline. Unsigned, collectively-owned argument text was the wiki norm; attribution to named proponents appears only in the editorially-run products.

### Cited Findings

**Debatepedia (2006/2007 – read-only Nov 2011)**
- Founded by Brooks Lindsay at Georgetown in 2006; merged in 2007 with the International Debate Education Association (IDEA, a 501c3), inheriting "roughly 500 refined debate articles" from IDEA's Debatabase; funded by Open Society Institute, UN Foundation, Ethics and Excellence in Journalism Foundation. Built by modifying MediaWiki (Debatewise, by contrast, built its own platform). — [Lindsay, "Creating 'the Wikipedia of pros and cons'", WikiSym 2009 panel abstract](https://www.opensym.org/ws2009/procfiles/p135-lindsay.pdf); [IDEA launch note](https://idebate.org/news/debatepediaidea-launch-0)
- Purpose as stated: to "centralize arguments and quotations found in millions of different articles into a single encyclopedia" so citizens can "weigh all the pros and cons"; the 2009 panel framed the field as "the in-depth citizen-journalism of public debates" and named the open question as "how well the community can self-regulate and maintain neutrality in the face of emotionally heated issues." — [Lindsay 2009](https://www.opensym.org/ws2009/procfiles/p135-lindsay.pdf)
- Page structure: "logic trees" — a two-column layout with pros and cons side by side "for each argument and its attendant sub-questions"; each debate starts with a main yes/no question, split-screen pros left / cons right, with sub-questions (economic, social, legal…) chunking large debates; a "fairly strict citations policy" made each article a link repository. The hierarchy Debate → Question → Side → Argument is reported by a search summary of Debatepedia's own help page, which I could not open (site unreachable). — [if:book, "an encyclopedia of arguments" (21 Feb 2007)](https://futureofthebook.org/blog/2007/02/21/an_encyclopedia_of_arguments/); [Debatepedia: Manipulating debate pages (unreachable, hierarchy via search snippet)](http://debatepedia.idebate.org/en/index.php/Debatepedia:Manipulating_debate_pages)
- Contemporary critique of its presentation (2007): the two-column format renders arguments as "logic switches, flipped on or off, left or right—a crude reduction of what an argument really is"; it inverts Wikipedia — "Instead of 'neutral point of view,' with debates submerged, you have an emphasis on the many-sidedness of things" — but "there has to be some way to present a more shaded spectrum of thought—to triangulate multiple perspectives." — [if:book 2007](https://futureofthebook.org/blog/2007/02/21/an_encyclopedia_of_arguments/)
- Fate: "Debatepedia (2007): Read-only since November 2011." — [Wikidébats, Comparison of Existing Debate Encyclopedias](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias)

**Other 2000s pro/con sites and their fates** (same comparison page unless noted)
- Hyperdébat (2002): read-only since Jan 2017. Debatewise (2007): "no longer updated" per the comparison, though the debatewise.org site is still reachable in 2026 as "an online database of expert-curated debates." Riyarchy (2012): closed 2016. Argumentrix (2011): inactive since 2013. Idebate (2011–): tree-like interface, "arguments lack depth and proponents aren't identified." Proversi (2015–): tables with expert video responses, not free-licensed. Wikidébats (2016–): arguments grouped into families with quotes and citations, CC-licensed. — [Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias); [DebateWise about page](https://debatewise.org/about-us/)
- ProCon.org (2004–): two-column pro/con with "numerous resources, glossaries, timelines, and proponent lists", in-house journalists rather than a wiki; acquired by Encyclopaedia Britannica on 29 May 2020 with a commitment to keep it running 25 years; 80+ issues. — [Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias); [Lindsay 2009](https://www.opensym.org/ws2009/procfiles/p135-lindsay.pdf); [Britannica press release (PDF)](https://images.procon.org/wp-content/uploads/eb-procon-press-release.pdf); [Hawley Troxell note on the 25-year commitment](https://hawleytroxell.com/2020/07/encyclopedia-britannica-acquires-procon-org-commits-to-running-for-25-years/)
- Debate.org (2007–2022): one-on-one pro/con debates judged by member votes; shut down 5 June 2022, with a "Download Data" export offered to members. — [DebateArt forum thread "DDO is shutting down"](https://www.debateart.com/forum/topics/7604-ddo-is-shutting-down)

**Wikiversity Wikidebate (2016 – discontinued)**
- Model: "A debate tree is a tree structure in which every node is an argument. The root node is called the main argument and the rest are called objections. Each objection is aimed at either the main argument, or another objection." A recursive "debate algorithm" computes SUSTAINED/REFUTED: "an argument should be considered refuted when it has unrefuted objections, and sustained otherwise." — [Wikiversity: Debate algorithm](https://en.m.wikiversity.org/wiki/Debate_algorithm)
- Guidelines: arguments are **unsigned** ("Signing arguments discourages others from improving them"); third person, no "I"/"we"; "If two arguments are essentially the same, merge them"; "If one argument is essentially two, split them apart"; and — distinctive — "If someone posts an objection pointing out a flaw in an argument, try to fix the flaw and remove the objection, rather than answering the objection." Contributors are asked to add arguments for both sides, "dialectic rather than eristic." — [Wikidebate/Guidelines](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Wikidebate/Guidelines)
- Fate: "the project has been discontinued due to a lack of moderation and policy implementation"; "No new arguments or debates will be included" pending a new Community Review. — [Wikiversity: Wikidebate](https://en.m.wikiversity.org/wiki/Wikidebate)
- Wikidébats' comparison describes Wikidebate as "box-based … copyright-free content but lacking detail and citations." — [Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias)

**Argüman (2014 – site down 2023)**
- Open source (AGPL, Python/Django), started 2014, "largely built by developers in Turkey." Three premise types — "because", "but", "however" — instead of Kialo's two-valued Pro/Con; users can flag arguments for named fallacies; the whole argument is rendered "as a large tree which may be harder to navigate than other formats." arguman.org "is down as of 2023"; code remains. — [Argüman (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arg%C3%BCman)

**Kialo (Aug 2017 –)**
- Founded by Errikos Pitsos; launched August 2017; Brooklyn and Berlin. Data model: a debate is a tree rooted in a thesis; each claim ≤ 500 characters, may link sources; each non-root claim is Pro or Con relative to its parent; users cast **impact votes** on a 1–5 scale; a "Perspectives" feature shows impact ratings filtered by supporters, opposers, moderators, contributors; a sunburst diagram shows debate topology. As of July 2023: 18,000+ public debates, 720,000+ claims, 1M+ votes. — [Kialo (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kialo)
- The research dataset framing: "a tree is rooted in an argument thesis, and each node corresponds to an argument claim. Along a path … every claim except the thesis was made to either support or oppose its parent claim"; impact labels are NO / LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH / VERY HIGH IMPACT, and "the same claim can have different impact labels depending on the context in which it is presented." 47,219 claims from 741 topics in one dataset. — [Durmus et al., "The Role of Pragmatic and Discourse Context in Determining Argument Impact" (arXiv 2004.03034)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.03034)
- Moderation: "Due to Kialo's strict moderation policy, each piece of text submitted to a debate is a self-contained argument with a clear claim backed by reasoning. Moderators vet every piece to make sure that it is relevant to the thesis and that the argument has not been covered by other parent arguments." Hierarchical permissions; arguments have "to get past the gatekeepers"; moderators can request edits/evidence, link duplicates, archive claims. — [GraphNLI (arXiv 2202.08175)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.08175); [Kialo (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kialo)
- Balance as an emergent property: as of 28 Jan 2020 a dataset of 1,560 discussion threads showed "the vast majority of discussion trees having between 40% to 60% of its edges as supporting edges." — [arXiv 1906.04148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.04148)
- Criticisms on record: "oversimplified argument structure limiting nuance"; closed platform, data not reusable; "insufficient capacity for proposing middle-ground solutions"; potential moderator bias. Wikidébats' comparison adds that Kialo "lacks quotes and named references." — [Kialo (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kialo); [Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias)
- Kialo Edu: private, locked classroom debates; teachers can view votes by class, individual, or by thesis supporters/opponents; anonymous student contributions. — [Kialo (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kialo)

**DebateGraph (Mar 2008 –)**
- Founded March 2008 by Peter Baldwin (former Australian minister) and David Price. Node types: issues, positions, supporting arguments, opposing arguments — "a graph structure rather than hierarchical tree." Content CC BY-SA 3.0. Used by the White House Open Government initiative (2009), UK FCO on nuclear policy (2010), CNN Amanpour (2010), The Independent; still a social venture. — [Debategraph (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debategraph)
- Provides "a 'windowed' view of a debate and allows construction of expressive graphs of interconnections, unlike tools that impose a 'tree' structure"; Wikidébats' comparison: card-based visualisation with multiple display modes "but has a complex interface." Both Debategraph and Rationale "use ad hoc and idiosyncratic data representation methods that make it difficult to reuse and represent the data." — [Springer chapter (search summary)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-642-23333-3_6.pdf); [Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias)

**TruthMapping**
- Self-described as "a focused, rational method for adversarial discussion (patent pending)" reducing debates to premises and conclusions; a MetaFilter thread discussed it as "the ultimate attempt to rationalize debate," with the criticism that discourse would only be elevated "if arguments were presented in first-order symbolic logic." The site did not respond to my fetch. — [MetaFilter thread](https://www.metafilter.com/43730/Truthmapping-The-Ultimate-Attempt-to-Rationalize-Debate)

**Rationale / Reason!Able (single-user, pedagogical)**
- Van Gelder and Bulka built Reason!Able (2000) and Rationale (2006); Rationale and bCisive were later bought by Dutch company Kritisch Denken BV (ReasoningLab). Van Gelder's "LAMP" (lots of argument-mapping practice) reported critical-thinking gains of ~0.8 SD in 12 weeks. — [Tim van Gelder (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_van_Gelder); [ReasoningLab, "Argument mapping"](https://www.reasoninglab.com/critical/argument-mapping/)

**Argunet**
- "Arguments are reconstructed as premise-conclusion structures and visually linked with arrows indicating support and attack relationships" (classical reconstruction, not IBIS). — [Springer chapter (search summary)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-642-23333-3_6.pdf)

**AIF / ArgDF**
- The Argument Interchange Format grew from a 2005 Budapest colloquium, first published as a draft in 2006 (Chesñevar, McGinnis, Modgil, Rahwan, Reed, Simari, South, Vreeswijk, Willmott). Graph with two node kinds: I-nodes (claims, premises, data) and S-nodes (applications of schemes: inference, preference, conflict). ArgDF, OVAgen and Araucaria are built on it. — [Argument Interchange Format (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_Interchange_Format); [Chesñevar et al. 2006](http://arg-tech.org/people/chris/publications/2006/aif_final.pdf)

### Inferences
- The one structural choice shared across the survivors is that the *edge*, not the node, carries the side label (Pro/Con relative to parent). That lets a claim be reused as a con in one branch and a pro in another without duplicating it — the property Debate Map (below) later made explicit with a DAG. Debatepedia's page-level "Yes column / No column" is the least reusable form.
- Every volunteer-wiki instance in this set (Debatepedia, Wikidebate, Argüman, Debatewise's wiki phase) died of moderation, not of technology; both surviving open-contribution platforms (Kialo, DebateGraph) gate every contribution through moderators or a small editing team.
- Unsigned text was the wiki norm precisely because signatures discourage improvement (Wikidebate's stated reason); named attribution ("proponent lists") appears in ProCon, which pays journalists to assemble it. A system that wants *both* editable position text and named advocates has to separate "who holds this position" from "who wrote this sentence."

## Key question 3 — MIT Deliberatorium, ConsiderIt / Living Voters Guide, Reflect, Wikum, Polis

### Takeaway
These research systems split into two mechanisms: *structure-first* (Deliberatorium enforces one-post-one-node IBIS maps with pre-publication moderator certification; ConsiderIt lets each person build a personal pro/con list and then aggregates by stance group) and *clustering-first* (Polis skips argument structure entirely and clusters people by vote pattern; Wikum layers editable summaries over an existing thread). The measured results are consistent: structure roughly quadruples moderation efficiency versus a forum at the cost of ~15–30% of enrolled participants actually posting, and the personal-list design got 41–53% of participants to include both a pro and a con.

### Cited Findings

**MIT Deliberatorium / Collaboratorium (Mark Klein, 2007–)**
- Model: "Each online posting is limited to only one point. It should be either a specific issue (question to be answered), an idea (possible answers for a question), or an argument (statement that support or detract from an idea)"; "Every issue, idea, or argument appears once to reduce repetition." Moderators certify posts before publication, making "simple changes, such as fixing where a post belonged, or whether it was incorrectly labelled as a pro or a con," and sending educational messages rather than rejecting. — [Participedia, "Combined Application of Deliberatorium and Doparie in Italy"](https://participedia.net/case/application-of-deliberatorium-in-italy)
- The single-instance rule stated for readers: "each logical point appears only once on the map—even if a million people have the same idea. That way, participants can critique and refine each point in an open market divorced from other biases—such as the number of people who hold a particular view." — [The Walrus, "How Science Can Help Us Disagree"](https://thewalrus.ca/how-science-can-help-us-disagree/)
- Naples biofuels test (Dec 2007, 220 graduate students): a non-expert community "was able to create a remarkably comprehensive map of the current debate on biofuels … in just a couple of weeks"; off-topic posts ≈0.1%; 72% of certified posts were arguments, 70% of those attached to someone else's post, but 70% of arguments were pros rather than cons. — [Iandoli, Klein & Zollo, "Can We Exploit Collective Intelligence…" (search summary)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228139817_Can_We_Exploit_Collective_Intelligence_for_Collaborative_Deliberation_The_Case_of_the_Climate_Change_Collaboratorium) (Wikipedia dates the Naples run December 2008: [Deliberatorium (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deliberatorium) — the two sources disagree by a year)
- Italian Democratic Party deployment (1–21 Apr 2012, 3-week deliberation): 640 enrolled, 373 logged in (58%), 194 posted (30%); average age 48, 67% male. Map vs. forum treatment: forum needed 3 h moderation + 160 h post-processing = 163 h; map needed 42 h moderation and no post-processing; forum produced 290 ideas with few arguments each, map 78 ideas with ~2× arguments per idea; ratings "significantly higher in the map treatment"; participation "increased over time and did not plateau"; users "more respectful … due to the tree's logical mechanism." Stated limits: interface "bulky and difficult to navigate," unknown maximum tree size, self-selected sample, moderator-bias risk; authors' conclusion that argument mapping "might not be appropriate for small environment that focus on brainstorming, while it is more appropriate for environments with hundreds of participants." Team of 10 moderators. — [Participedia case](https://participedia.net/case/application-of-deliberatorium-in-italy)
- Governance lesson from the Naples prototype: "Moderators played a crucial role … by ensuring a logically-organized argument map, helped users rapidly locate the contexts where their piece of knowledge can best be placed"; the "moderate-as-you-go" approach "saved time during the post-processing of content." — [Management Innovation eXchange, "Making Large-Scale Deliberations Better, Online"](https://www.managementexchange.com/hack/deliberatorium-hack)
- Klein's page says the system "has been used by thousands of individuals in such institutions as Intel, the Federal Bureau of Land Management, and the Italian Democratic Party," developed "since 2007." — [deliberatorium.org research page](https://deliberatorium.org/homepage/research.html)

**ConsiderIt / Living Voters Guide (Kriplean et al., CSCW 2012)**
- Design: a neutral description of the ballot measure; a stance slider ("strongly support" to "strongly oppose"); a personal "chalkboard" pro/con list; in the margins, four pro and four con points written by others, each with an arrow to *include* it in one's own list, replaced by an unseen point when included; points are ≤140-char summary plus ≤500-char optional description; a second slider after the list prompts stance re-evaluation (linked to the first). "The focus is on augmented personal deliberation, rather than direct discussion." — [Kriplean et al., "Supporting reflective public thought with ConsiderIt" (CSCW 2012, PDF)](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)
- Comparison mechanism: the results page shows a **stance histogram** (seven stance groups derived from slider values) and a ranked pro/con slate; "clicking on a bar changes the points shown … revealing a ranked list of the most important points for those who took that particular stance." Ranking (**PointRank**) has three terms: *persuasiveness* (includes ÷ views), *diverse appeal* (entropy of inclusion across the seven stance groups — highest when includers are spread evenly), *raw appeal* (count); the diverse-appeal term is dropped when drilling into one stance group. A threaded discussion can be pegged to any point. Designed against brigading: masquerading points fail the diverse-appeal term, and drilling into the strong-oppose bar exposes who authored them. — [Kriplean et al. 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)
- Deployment numbers (launched 21 Sep 2010, WA state, 9 ballot measures; City Club seeded one pro and one con per measure): 12,979 visits from 8,813 unique visitors to 2 Nov; 468 people submitted a position; 184 con + 160 pro points by 147 users (max 10 per user); 298 users included points 2,687 times into 678 non-empty lists (503 lists left empty); ~38 points per measure. — [Kriplean et al. 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)
- Balance results: "of the 678 positions that included at least one point, 41.4% of them included both a pro and a con"; excluding one-point lists, 53.0%; of the 40 users who authored more than one point on a measure, 45.0% wrote both a pro and a con; of 599 positions where a non-neutral user included another's point, 33.7% included one written by someone of the opposing stance; the stronger the stance, the less likely both pros and cons were included (r² = .249). 46.3% of survey respondents said they changed a stance on at least one measure. The authors attribute cross-stance inclusion partly to the absence of identity cues: "ConsiderIt does not provide group cues to activate political identity." — [Kriplean et al. 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)
- Stated biggest problem: "the biggest challenge users faced was evaluating the trustworthiness of these claims … Making sense of claims seriously constrained the ultimate utility of the LVG as an information resource"; they proposed linking sources to individual points for fact-checking, which the 2012 LVG did with Seattle Public Library librarians. Redundancy was found to be "more complex … than it appears": duplicates "add value by elaborating on an existing argument, reframing it in less (or more) inflammatory language." — [Kriplean et al. 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf); [UW News, 17 Oct 2012](https://washington.edu/news/2012/10/17/living-voters-guide-adds-fact-checking-by-seattle-librarians-for-2012-election)
- Status: Consider.it continues as Kriplean's bootstrapped company; AGPLv3 open source; 2023 updates added permanently-anonymised forums. — [GitHub Considerit/ConsiderIt](https://github.com/Considerit/ConsiderIt); [Kriplean LinkedIn post, Aug 2023](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/travis-kriplean_considerit-now-has-deeper-anonymity-features-activity-7094718311904546817-l_q4)

**Reflect (Kriplean et al., CHI 2012)**
- "A space next to every comment where others can summarize the points they hear the commenter making." Deployed on Slashdot: users created about one restatement bullet per comment on average, and content analysis found bullets "were on balance used to restate the meaning of the comments, rather than to reply to the comment or flame." — [ACM DL record](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2207676.2208621); [Reflect project page](https://engage.cs.washington.edu/reflect/)

**Wikum (Zhang, Verou, Karger — CSCW 2017)**
- Model: a **summary tree** over an existing threaded discussion. Editors pick a small subtree (or a k-means cluster of same-level comments), write a ≤250-word summary (or half the source length, whichever is smaller); the summary node replaces those comments in the view; higher-level summaries summarise summaries; every summary carries clickable citations back to comments and can quote them; readers see orange summary nodes and expand down to blue original comments. — [Wikum paper (PDF)](https://people.csail.mit.edu/axz/papers/wikum.pdf)
- Results: with 20 participants working 20-minute shifts on 7–8k-word discussions, all three Wikum summaries finished while none of the Google Doc controls did; summarisation rate 51.9 words reduced/min vs 36.3; "Users were reluctant to edit others' summaries in both conditions" — in the Doc condition 12/20 only appended to one ever-growing summary. The cost argument: total editor reading is at most ~W/4 extra over the discussion's own length. 13 additional readers found the tree "helpful for quickly getting an overview." — [Wikum paper](https://people.csail.mit.edu/axz/papers/wikum.pdf)
- Follow-on: a Wikipedia RfC case study (CSCW 2018) used Wikum as a "discussion summarization and decision support tool." — [Meta-Wiki research page](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:Discussion_summarization_and_decision_support_with_Wikum); [ACM record](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3274343)

**Polis (Computational Democracy Project, 2012–)**
- Model: participants submit ≤140-character statements and vote agree / disagree / pass on others' statements served semi-randomly; there are no replies. The data is an "opinion matrix"; Polis is "language agnostic" — no NLP. Dimensionality reduction by PCA (and UMAP); clustering by k-means, Leiden, or hierarchical methods; the report shows consensus statements and divisive statements in real time; deployments "ranging from 40 to 40,000 people"; AGPL3. — [compdemocracy.org, Polis overview](https://compdemocracy.org/Polis/); [compdemocracy.org, Algorithms](https://compdemocracy.org/algorithms/)
- vTaiwan: first Polis use August 2015 on Uber regulation; "200,000+" cumulative participation; the Ministry of Transportation pledged to ratify the Polis consensus; statements such as a fair regulatory regime and vehicle registration reached >80% agreement across groups. — [compdemocracy vTaiwan case](https://compdemocracy.org/case-studies/2014-vtaiwan/); [Democracy Technologies, "Lessons From Consensus Building in Taiwan"](https://democracy-technologies.org/participation/consensus-building-in-taiwan/)
- A 2023 study of LLMs on Polis found "LLM context limitations have a significant impact on insight and quality" of automated summarisation. — [Small et al., arXiv 2306.11932](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11932)

### Inferences
- Deliberatorium's numbers are the only direct forum-vs-map comparison in this set: the map costs about a quarter of the human hours *once post-processing is counted*, but because the moderation happens up front it is felt as a barrier by contributors (only 30% of enrollees posted). For a publishing product that is a strong argument for structure at *authoring* time by a few editors rather than at *contribution* time by everyone.
- ConsiderIt is the closest existing analogue to "present schools of thought side by side for a reader": its comparison view is not pro-vs-con but *stance-group-vs-stance-group* ("what do strong opposers find persuasive?"), which is a different and arguably more useful axis than side labels. Its two reusable ideas: rank shared content by *cross-group* appeal, and hide identity cues to reduce tribal filtering.
- Polis demonstrates that "positions" can be *discovered* from votes rather than declared; but its output is a cluster of people, not a written position, which is why its deployments pair it with a human drafting stage (vTaiwan).

### Gaps
- No Deliberatorium retention data after the Italian deployment; the Intel and BLM uses are only named, never quantified in anything I could reach.
- The ConsiderIt survey had a 21.6% response rate and a liberal-skewed sample (100% liberal on the two polarised measures); the paper itself flags this, so its opinion-change numbers are weak.
- Polis's specific "group-informative" statement statistic and vTaiwan's per-case participant counts were not on the pages I could open.

## Key question 4 — The Society Library, the Great American Debate, Debate Map, Canonical Debate Lab

### Takeaway
The Society Library (Jamie Joyce, 501(c)3) is the most exhaustive current attempt: a single Diablo Canyon map holds 5,862 argument/claim/evidence nodes from 5,000+ references, built in about 8,000 research hours over 8 months, structured around emergent "fundamental questions" with every answer (position) linked by 20+ logical relation types, and stored in Stephen Wicklund's open-source Debate Map (a DAG of claim/argument nodes with truth and relevance ratings). Its declared audience is policymakers, researchers and AI labs rather than casual readers, and its own answer to the maintenance problem is an LLM extraction pipeline ("Rose" top-down, "Juniper" bottom-up).

### Cited Findings
- Organisation and method: "a 501(c)3 collective intelligence organization founded by Jamie Joyce focused on truth-seeking by modelling the logic of debates about policy issues"; "extracts arguments, claims, and evidence from various forms of media to compile databases." Its "Debate Mapping program is branded 'The Great American Debate'." Method is "descriptive emergent structuring": "first collect arguments, claims, opinions, and evidence from various media and from 'all' points of view to discover the questions which society is arguing about," then "flesh out all possible answers … then logically link argumentation together via 20+ logical relationships, which structures a debate." Topics contain 80–500+ sub-topics (climate change 270+). "Many of these debate libraries are not yet built." — [Society Library, Debate Maps program page](https://www.societylibrary.org/debate-mapping-program); [YANSS 292 (Jul 2024)](https://youarenotsosmart.com/2024/07/22/yanss-292-how-one-organization-is-attempting-to-end-analysis-paralysis-by-creating-a-library-of-our-collective-arguing/)
- Node semantics: "Debate maps represent the ontological structure of the data itself, and the Society Library infrastructure supports linking multimedia expressions of a concept into one node, such that a single claim like 'nuclear energy is the safest form of energy production' can be expressed through multiple linguistic registers and media formats including podcasts, videos, and images." — [search summary attributed to the Jim Rutt Show transcript, Currents 076](https://jimruttshow.blubrry.net/the-jim-rutt-show-transcripts/transcript-of-currents-076-jamie-joyce-on-the-society-library/) (the transcript page returned 503 twice; not verified first-hand)
- Diablo Canyon map (published Oct 2023 per the updates page; project page dated 17 Aug 2022): 5,862 arguments, claims and evidence from 5,000+ references, "performed in an 8 month period" and "suddenly stopped at the ending of the grant period"; the public view "shows only the first three layers"; reader formats offered: "paper," "outline," "map," "cloud," and "search"; corrections are by writing to the Library. Joyce later cites ~8,000 research hours for it. — [Society Library, Diablo Canyon Debate](https://www.societylibrary.org/diablo-canyon-debate); [Society Library Updates](https://www.societylibrary.org/society-library-updates); [Joyce, Substack, 6 May 2026](https://justjamiejoyce.substack.com/p/your-attempt-to-refute-argument-maps)
- Positions on Diablo Canyon: a search summary of the Society Library's nuclear-energy page reports "nine different positions on whether the nuclear power plant should remain open or closed, including that it should close as scheduled, close immediately, remain open, remain open only as a poly generation facility, or become a different type of reactor." When I fetched that page myself it showed instead six framing questions ("What is Nuclear Energy? Is Nuclear Energy Safe? Is Nuclear Energy Ethical? Can Nuclear Power Meet Energy Needs? Is Production Efficient? Should the U.S. Pursue Nuclear Power?"), a status of "Paused, pending funding," and no position list — so treat the nine-position figure as unverified. — [Society Library, Nuclear Energy Project](https://www.societylibrary.org/nuclear-energy-project)
- Tooling: the maps live in Debate Map (Stephen Wicklund / Venryx), whose development "is partially supported by The Society Library, which is co-developing a separate infrastructural standard within Debate Map for its use"; the Library hosts an instance at debatemap.societylibrary.org. — [GitHub debate-map/app](https://github.com/debate-map/app); [Society Library Debate Map instance](https://debatemap.societylibrary.org/)
- Debate Map's model (from its README): claims (gray nodes) and arguments (green = supporting, red = opposing) built by "Breaking down lines of reasoning into single-sentence 'nodes'"; every node has truth and relevance ratings; nodes carry multiple **phrasings**; the graph is a DAG so "nodes can be reused across multiple parent nodes"; access policies and permissions per content area with a reputation system in development; TypeScript/React front end, Rust back end, PostgreSQL; MIT licence. — [GitHub debate-map/app](https://github.com/debate-map/app)
- AI pipeline (dated): Oct 2023 fine-tuning task-specific LLMs to automate the pipeline; Nov 2023 "Rose," a top-down debate-modelling tool using multi-stage reasoning prompts; Jan 2024 "Juniper," a bottom-up tool extracting arguments from multimedia and clustering them into "idea bundles"; Feb 2024 an AI-mapped Leahy vs. Jezos debate; Oct 2023 a $150k matching grant for AI/AGI debate mapping; Feb 2026 two $10k bounties; Nov 2025 an initiative on multiple "epistemological modalities" with Yale's Center for Environmental Justice (2025). — [Society Library Updates](https://www.societylibrary.org/society-library-updates)
- Audience and purpose, in Joyce's own words (6 May 2026, replying to Scott Alexander): the maps serve "policymakers, researchers, and decision-makers—not Twitter combatants" — city council members, policy analysts and mediators, university libraries, AI labs (OpenAI and xAI named), senators and think tanks; "We are not in the business of helping you win arguments" but of mapping "the entire landscape of what humans have said, claimed, argued, and evidenced about a contested issue." She says she has reviewed 168 such projects. — [Joyce, "Your Attempt To Refute Argument Maps Will Not Work"](https://justjamiejoyce.substack.com/p/your-attempt-to-refute-argument-maps)
- Editorial stance on sources: "the truth may come from any source: a conspiratorial website, a harsh rant online, a weaponized piece of content, or even a meme"; the Library actively seeks "fringe, isolated, or distant ideas and opinions." — [YANSS 292](https://youarenotsosmart.com/2024/07/22/yanss-292-how-one-organization-is-attempting-to-end-analysis-paralysis-by-creating-a-library-of-our-collective-arguing/)
- Canonical Debate Lab (white paper created 11 Jun 2018, MIT licence, "a community project of Democracy Earth Foundation"): a **Claim** is a true/false statement; an argument is a **Support** object with a Conclusion (Claim) and a Premise (Claim), so arguments are edges between canonical claims; **Context** (geography, timeframe, specificity) as a first-class attribute — "context is not used at all" in existing systems; a Request-for-Information mechanism; scoring split into objective, belief and popular-vote perspectives with the rule that "scores should not be biased by content" (an unopposed strong argument must not out-score its intrinsic strength); arguments may be "pro AND con" simultaneously; roles are Debaters and **Curators**, whose actions are create/delete, merge duplicates, split broad claims, group arguments, attach premises; it names "insufficient curation" as the standing weakness of existing platforms and critiques Kialo as usable but non-transparent and centralised. A 2021 note records agreement on a minimal "base layer" model to which richer models map. — [GitHub canonical-debate-lab/paper](https://github.com/canonical-debate-lab/paper); [High, Hangout Highlights 14 May 2021](https://medium.com/canonical-debate-lab/hangout-highlights-14-may-2021-4cbce3e64725)

### Inferences
- The Society Library is the one project in this survey whose "positions" are genuinely plural rather than binary: positions are the enumerated *answers* to an emergent question, and side-labelling happens only at the argument→claim edge. That is the IBIS Question/Idea split scaled up, with Debate Map's DAG letting one claim serve many positions.
- Its economics (≈8,000 hours for one plant's debate; work stopping when a grant ends; "paused pending funding" on the general nuclear map) are the clearest available evidence that exhaustive multi-position mapping does not sustain itself on volunteers, which is why the organisation pivoted to LLM extraction.
- Canonical Debate Lab's contribution is conceptual rather than deployed: its insistence on *context* and on curators as a distinct role from debaters is the clearest statement of what the wiki era lacked.

### Gaps
- The list of the "20+ logical relationships" is not enumerated on any page I could open (the Society Library Medium post and the Debate Map Medium post both returned 403).
- The Jim Rutt transcript (503) would likely give staffing, hours and reader-use detail; unverified.
- No reader analytics for the Society Library maps; Joyce's evidence of use is a list of institutional relationships, not traffic.
- Canonical Debate Lab's current software status: the May 2026 Medium post exists but was blocked (403); the GitHub "Active" flag is stale metadata, so I cannot say whether any CDL software runs.

## Key question 5 — Contrast mechanisms: Community Notes' bridging ranking and the Habermas Machine

### Takeaway
Both are *viewpoint-aware without representing viewpoints as content*: Community Notes learns a latent one-dimensional viewpoint axis from rating behaviour and promotes only notes whose approval is not explained by that axis; the Habermas Machine generates a single group statement, using a per-person reward model and critique rounds to keep minority views in the text. Neither presents multiple positions to a reader — they are ways of choosing *one* thing to show that several factions accept.

### Cited Findings
- Ovadya's "Bridging-Based Ranking" (Belfer Center, published 17–18 May 2022): current recommenders "reward people who seek to divide us more than those who aim to bridge divides"; the proposal is ranking that promotes "areas of common ground" instead of "divisive content that triggers tribal instincts." — [Belfer Center, Bridging-Based Ranking](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/bridging-based-ranking)
- Community Notes model: r̂ᵤₙ = μ + iᵤ + iₙ + fᵤ·fₙ (global intercept, user intercept, note intercept, dot product of latent factor vectors). A note is Currently Rated Helpful when its intercept iₙ ≥ 0.40; Not Helpful when iₙ < −0.05 − 0.8·|noteFactorScore|; at least 5 ratings needed for any status, with further minimums such as ≥10 helpful ratings from raters on each side of the factor. "Extra regularization on the intercept terms in practice requires that notes are rated by raters with diverse factors before a note gets a label." Factors are one-dimensional "for now, to avoid overfitting," though "We can represent multidimensional viewpoint spaces by increasing the dimensionality of the factors." — [Community Notes docs, ranking-notes.md (GitHub)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/twitter/communitynotes/main/documentation/under-the-hood/ranking-notes.md)
- An independent explainer: the axis is not pre-set — the model finds "some latent factor of users and posts that divides the community," which "could represent politics, cat-vs-dog preferences, or any divisive dimension"; the intercept "reflects hypothetical voting behavior in a politically balanced population," and the mechanism more precisely finds posts rated highly "regardless of diversity of perspective" than "because of" it. — [Warden, "Understanding Community Notes and Bridging-Based Ranking"](https://jonathanwarden.com/understanding-community-notes/)
- Habermas Machine (Tessler et al., *Science*, 18 Oct 2024; arXiv precursor Nov 2022): small UK groups (up to five) wrote opinions on issues such as Brexit, immigration, minimum wage, climate and childcare; a generative LLM proposed candidate group statements; a personalised reward model predicted each member's ranking; members ranked statements and wrote critiques; a revised statement was generated and selected again. One study reported n = 2,110 participants. Result: "AI mediation indeed helps participants find common ground, outperforming human mediation"; "more than 56% of participants preferred AI statements"; after the initial round the machine "fairly represent[ed] majority and minority viewpoints" but after critique incorporation it "tended to over-weight minority viewpoints"; groups were "less divided on the issue than they were prior to deliberation." — [Knight First Amendment Institute, "Can AI Mediation Improve Democratic Deliberation?"](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/can-ai-mediation-improve-democratic-deliberation); [MIT Technology Review, 17 Oct 2024](https://www.technologyreview.com/2024/10/17/1105810/ai-could-help-people-find-common-ground-during-deliberations/); [arXiv 2211.15006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15006); [Science record](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adq2852)
- The 2022 precursor: "when consensus statements were constructed from only a subset of group members, those excluded were more likely to dissent, revealing sensitivity to individual contributions"; >65% preferred model statements over the best human-written opinion. — [arXiv 2211.15006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15006)

### Inferences
- For a multi-position publishing system, the transferable idea is Community Notes' *test*, not its output: "is approval of this text explained by which camp you are in?" That is computable from any per-reader reaction data and could rank a *position's supporting text* by cross-camp acceptability, the way ConsiderIt's diverse-appeal term already did by hand in 2010.
- The Habermas Machine's over-weighting of minority views after critique rounds is a warning about iterative "please everyone" drafting: it converges on text nobody wrote and nobody owns, the opposite of attributed positions.

### Gaps
- The Science paper's full participant total across all studies (a figure of 5,734 is often quoted) is not confirmed by any page I could open; only the n = 2,110 sub-study figure is sourced.

## Key question 6 — Do readers use structured multi-position presentations, and what is the maintenance burden?

### Takeaway
The direct evidence is thin and mostly about *contributors*, not readers: reading an argument map improves recall but not comprehension versus prose (Dwyer et al. 2010, n = 400); ConsiderIt's readers spent ~10½ minutes and 6 pages per non-bounce visit; Deliberatorium halved-to-quartered curation hours but only ~30% of enrollees posted. Every volunteer-maintained debate wiki in this survey went read-only or offline, and the best-resourced current effort measures a single map in thousands of paid hours and stops when the grant does — so the maintenance evidence is unambiguous even though the reader-benefit evidence is not.

### Cited Findings
- Dwyer, Hogan & Stewart (2010), *Thinking Skills and Creativity*: 400 undergraduates studied a colour map, black-and-white map, or text of a 30- or 50-node argument ("can computers think?") for 10 minutes; map readers "scored higher than those who studied text, on tests of memory, though not comprehension." — [ERIC record EJ872591](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ872591); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1871187109000224)
- ConsiderIt reader behaviour: 12,979 visits / 8,813 unique visitors over six weeks; 6,082 sessions saw only the homepage; the rest "stayed an average of 10 minutes 39 seconds and visited 6.1 pages"; "Many visitors opted to simply browse the guide." Survey: reading opposing points was rated significantly more helpful than reading points when undecided (p < .05), with "much greater variance for opposing points." — [Kriplean et al. 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)
- Classroom self-report on two-sided display: 87% of students agreed "Seeing people's ideas on two opposing sides helped deepen my understanding of the debate topics" (Kialo, Japanese university EFL course). — [Mahoney, "Integrating Kialo to Deepen Critical Thinking in Debate", JFLER 2022](https://fler.rikkyo.ac.jp/journal/jc0e3e00000000a9-att/JFLER(2022_Vol.3)_13_Mahoney.pdf)
- Maintenance: Deliberatorium map = 42 h moderation vs forum = 163 h including post-processing, for 194 posting participants over three weeks; "Most map moderation concentrated in week one; users became more proficient afterward." — [Participedia case](https://participedia.net/case/application-of-deliberatorium-in-italy)
- Maintenance: Wikum's editors would not edit others' summaries (12/20 appended instead); the recursive design sidesteps this by letting higher summaries *overlay* rather than replace. — [Wikum paper](https://people.csail.mit.edu/axz/papers/wikum.pdf)
- Maintenance: Wikidebate "discontinued due to a lack of moderation and policy implementation." Debatepedia read-only since Nov 2011; Argüman down 2023; Debate.org closed 5 Jun 2022; Compendium unmaintained since Jan 2014. — [Wikiversity: Wikidebate](https://en.m.wikiversity.org/wiki/Wikidebate); [Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias); [Argüman (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arg%C3%BCman); [DebateArt thread](https://www.debateart.com/forum/topics/7604-ddo-is-shutting-down); [Compendium (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compendium_(software))
- Maintenance: the Society Library's Diablo Canyon map took ~8,000 research hours across 8 months and "was suddenly stopped at the ending of the grant period"; its broader nuclear map is "Paused, pending funding." — [Joyce Substack 2026](https://justjamiejoyce.substack.com/p/your-attempt-to-refute-argument-maps); [Diablo Canyon Debate page](https://www.societylibrary.org/diablo-canyon-debate); [Nuclear Energy Project page](https://www.societylibrary.org/nuclear-energy-project)
- The sceptical case, stated in full (Scott Alexander, 28 Apr 2026): "'Debate' almost never corresponds to mappable arguments"; most disputes hinge not on false facts or fallacies but on people who "weigh theoretical and empirical evidence differently"; the demographic "people who want to argue on the Internet, but are looking for a better way" scarcely exists; r/changemyview is "the closest thing" to a working format and is a community norm, "not any kind of mechanical change to the argument format." He says he turns down every ACX grant application of this kind. — [Astral Codex Ten, "Your Attempt To Solve Debate Will Not Work"](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/your-attempt-to-solve-debate-will)
- Replies: Joyce (above) concedes the mass-reader premise and relocates the audience to institutions and AI training; Timothy High notes the Canonical Debate Lab "was founded after multiple encounters with people online trying to solve the same problem, in much the same way." — [Joyce Substack](https://justjamiejoyce.substack.com/p/your-attempt-to-refute-argument-maps); [High, Medium, May 2026](https://medium.com/canonical-debate-lab/response-to-scott-alexanders-your-attempt-to-solve-debate-will-not-work-2c2d330a3082)
- Older literature framing: Buckingham Shum & Hammond 1994 found neither usefulness nor usability of argumentation notations "convincingly" substantiated outside design, thirty years before the same complaint recurred. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581984710299)

### Inferences
- What readers demonstrably get from structure is *navigation and recall*, not persuasion or comprehension; the ConsiderIt finding that opposing points help most when a reader already leans one way suggests the useful reader-facing feature is "show me the other side's best case," not "show me the whole tree."
- Every project that survived either (a) narrowed the model to two node kinds and paid gatekeepers (Kialo), (b) kept editorial control in-house (ProCon → Britannica), or (c) declared that readers are not the audience (Society Library). A publishing product with human authors is closest to (b): a small number of authors each *owning* a position's text, with the structure limited to "position ↔ question" and "argument ↔ position, pro/con," is the shape the evidence supports; open-contribution trees are the shape it does not.
- The one mechanism with measured reader-side comparison value — ConsiderIt's stance-group drill-down — needs reader reactions (inclusions/votes), so a system that wants comparison rather than mere juxtaposition has to collect some per-reader signal.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures whether *general readers* of a multi-position page (Debatepedia, Kialo, DebateGraph, Society Library) read more than one side, how long they stay, or what they take away — the Dwyer study is a lab recall test and the ConsiderIt analytics are aggregate.
- No published operating-cost figures for Kialo's moderation team or DebateGraph's editors.
- "Reason Against the Machine" (arXiv 2107.12711), a survey of mass online deliberation, likely aggregates participation figures across these systems, but only its abstract was reachable.
