# Labeled Viewpoints Exist; Comparison Layers Don't

**Exactly one wiki has run "several labeled points of view per topic" in production — Fred Bauder's Wikinfo (2003–c.2017) — and it needed no software beyond stock MediaWiki: a title convention (`[[United States:A critical viewpoint]]`), a "sympathetic" default for the main article, and a rule that rival pages link to one another at the top.** Every other built system relocates plurality away from the article: to the whole site (Conservapedia, RationalWiki, the Catholic Encyclopedia, Grokipedia), to the publisher (Sanger's Encyclosphere), to a signed entry under a shared title (Everything2, Google Knol, Stack Exchange, Federated Wiki), or to a typed argument node whose label is a pro/con *edge* rather than a school of thought (IBIS, Kialo, MIT's Deliberatorium, the Society Library). Where products label perspectives, they label the *outlet* on a five- or seven-bucket left–right axis that their own operators call lossy (AllSides, Ground News), and only four designs anywhere make the perspectives answer the same question (ProCon, Verity, Zondervan's Counterpoints, comparison charts). Comparison is almost always ranking or juxtaposition; passage-level alignment of rival texts exists only where a canonical coordinate system pre-exists (verse numbers in BibleHub, Sefaria and Perseus; a thesis node in Kialo) or in Xanadu's never-adopted transpointing windows. The evidence is lopsided: maintenance cost is documented everywhere — every volunteer debate wiki went read-only, and the best-funded exhaustive map (the Society Library's Diablo Canyon debate) took roughly **8,000 research hours** and stopped when its grant did — while reader benefit is thin (argument maps improve recall, not comprehension; ConsiderIt got **41–53%** of participants to include both a pro and a con). The academic record explains the pattern: Wikipedia's neutrality is a convergence process that works where many editors show up and leaves a residue of never-ending-war and low-attention articles, and the tools that succeeded with users put plurality *beside* the single article (Omnipedia, Manypedia, WikiGap) rather than inside it. No paper in the corpus reports a deployed wiki with several labeled perspectives inside one article page.

## Nine places the label has lived, and the gap they all share

The lines of work sort cleanly by *what carries the label*. The table is the map for the rest of the report; each row is developed, with sources, in the section named.

| Where the label lives | Examples | How a POV is labeled | How a reader compares | What practice showed | Section |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A rival page on the same wiki | Wikinfo (2003–c.2017) | Title suffix `Subject:A critical viewpoint`; main article "sympathetic" by rule | Reciprocal links at the top of each page; no index | Ran a decade on stock MediaWiki; ~45.7k articles; no count of how often the mechanism was used; site drifted to another host and vanished | Wikinfo |
| The whole site | Conservapedia, RationalWiki, Justapedia, Grokipedia, Catholic/Jewish Encyclopedia, Theopedia | Masthead / declared mission | None — two tabs | Longest-lived pattern; no comparison at all | Wikinfo; Products |
| The publisher | Encyclosphere / ZWI (2019–) | `Publisher` metadata field; "declared framework" proposed 2025, unbuilt | Aggregator sorts by ratings; per-topic UI undocumented | ~3M ZWI files from 66 encyclopedias; ratings are publisher-supplied | Wikinfo |
| A signed sibling entry under one title | Everything2, Knol, Stack Exchange, Quora | Author name | Votes, "cools", accepted answer, search rank | Knol collapsed under "dozens of competing articles"; E2 fell from 9,976 to 93 writeups/month; SE persists because a *question* invites several answers | Sibling entries |
| A forked copy with provenance | Federated Wiki (2011–), Wikity | Site flag; journal records origin site per paragraph | "Twins" line computed from the reader's neighborhood; no diff | ~36 people, ~2,000 pages in a two-week Happening; "hard to learn"; no fork notification; no adoption beyond enthusiasts | Sibling entries |
| A typed argument node | IBIS → Compendium, Debatepedia, Kialo, Deliberatorium, Society Library | Pro/con relative to a parent claim; positions = answers to a question | Tree or DAG; impact/truth ratings | Volunteer instances died of moderation; survivors gate every contribution; ~8,000 h per exhaustive map | Argument systems |
| The news outlet | AllSides, Ground News, Ad Fontes | 5 or 7 left–right buckets, or −42…+42 coordinates | Three columns of whole articles | Operators concede buckets are lossy; sides rarely answer the same question | Products |
| A parallel signed essay on one question | Zondervan Counterpoints, ProCon, Verity | View name in the table of contents; Pro/Con; per-axis narrative | Cross-responses (Counterpoints); two columns; Facts block above Narratives | Only formats where the perspectives address each other; editorially staffed | Products |
| A commentary aligned to a canonical segment | BibleHub, Sefaria, Perseus, Talmud page, Hexapla | Author or work, never tradition | Verse-level stacking or side-by-side columns | Cheap alignment because the coordinate system pre-exists; reader must know Haydock is Catholic | Products |
| The reader's own profile | Infogalactic (promised 2016) | Reader "preference"; Fact/Context/Opinion tiers | Algorithmic filtering | Never built; site later served an unrelated company's landing page | Wikinfo |

Every row shares one absence: no system pairs an *author-declared* label with *passage-level* comparison. The segment-aligned tools have the comparison and lack the label; Wikinfo and Counterpoints have the label and leave the reader to hold two texts in mind. That combination — the thing the question is really asking after — is unbuilt.

## Wikinfo ran labeled rival pages for a decade on zero software

Wikinfo "was launched in July 2003 by Fred Bauder, as a 'fork' of Wikipedia," and "unlike Wikipedia, which uses 'neutral point of view', the main article on each subject in Wikinfo adopts a 'sympathetic point of view', points of view can be added, usually as articles linked to the main article" ([Talk:Wikinfo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk:Wikinfo)). The policy page states the mechanism in full: the main article "should be written from a positive point of view," critical material "can, and should be, covered in auxiliary articles," and "**auxiliary articles should be prominently linked to at the top of the main, positive, article on the subject, and vice-versa**" ([Wikinfo:Sympathetic point of view, Wayback](http://web.archive.org/web/2009id_/http://www.wikinfo.org/index.php/Wikinfo:Sympathetic_point_of_view)). The label is the page title: dissent "written under a title in the form: `[[United States:A critical viewpoint]]`. In that article one can feel free to fully express whatever negative light one might see the United States in. (This is kind of like a debate)" ([same](http://web.archive.org/web/2009id_/http://www.wikinfo.org/index.php/Wikinfo:Sympathetic_point_of_view)). Several critical pages per subject were expected — "Additional articles may take various critical viewpoints" — with the instruction to "link all such articles together at the top of each page, so that a reader of Wikinfo can consider all the viewpoints," and the split was reserved for cases where "various perspectives cannot coexist in a single article"; otherwise "a typical 'pro-and-con' treatment within an article" sufficed ([same](http://web.archive.org/web/2009id_/http://www.wikinfo.org/index.php/Wikinfo:Sympathetic_point_of_view)). The site rejected NPOV in so many words as "unattainable in many cases, leading to edit wars," and claimed the payoff directly: "we don't have edit wars, and rarely have formal disagreements over content, since everyone has a clear outlet," at **45,690 articles** ([same](http://web.archive.org/web/2009id_/http://www.wikinfo.org/index.php/Wikinfo:Sympathetic_point_of_view)). Meta-Wiki's own survey calls this "the simplest dual-article model: sympathetic content in one article, critical analysis in another" ([Meta: Point of view](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Point_of_view)).

What practice showed is mostly absence. The site had "more than 34000 pages in January 2006" ([Talk:Wikinfo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk:Wikinfo)), but the notes found no count of how many `X:A critical viewpoint` pages ever existed, so the rate at which the mechanism was actually exercised versus simply importing Wikipedia articles is unknown. By 2015–2017 the policy page was being served from a subpath of an unrelated domain ([Wayback, 2015 capture](http://web.archive.org/web/2015id_/http://www.wikinfo.org/English/index.php/Wikinfo:Sympathetic_point_of_view)), and wikinfo.org now serves unrelated content. Two design facts matter more than the fate. First, the scheme is asymmetric: the "sympathetic" main article is a privileged default POV — the subject's own — with critics as satellites, not a symmetric set of schools. Second, discovery is offloaded entirely onto hand-maintained top-of-page links; there is no index of "all viewpoints on X," which is the first thing a software implementation would add.

Wikipedia itself keeps exactly one article and moves plurality *inside the sentence*: "biased statements of opinion can be presented only with in-text attribution," weighted "in proportion to the prominence of each viewpoint," with debates integrated into the narrative rather than segregated into pro/con sections ([WP:NPOV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Neutral_point_of_view)). A second article "to evade the neutrality policy" is a POV fork; "Creating multiple articles of the same type covering identical subjects" is prohibited, and "Criticism of X" pages survive only when sources treat the criticism as a topic and the page carries "suitably-weighted positive and negative opinions, and/or rebuttals" ([WP:Content forking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Content_forking)). Notably, the idea has never reached even "perennially rejected" status: the raw wikitext of the Perennial proposals page has **no entry** on multiple versions of an article ([WP:Perennial proposals](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wikipedia:Perennial_proposals&action=raw)); the closest artifact is an orphaned 2006 essay theorizing an "MPOV" evolution without specifying a mechanism ([WP:Multiple points of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Multiple_points_of_view)). Within Wikimedia the formal departures are on Wikiversity: a proposed Disclosures policy that puts a bias box at the head of any page exempted from NPOV, with a controlled vocabulary ("NPOV, left-wing, right-wing, Christian conservative, libertarian, atheist, socialist") but no prescribed linkage between differently-disclosed pages on one topic ([Wikiversity:Disclosures](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Wikiversity:Disclosures)); and Wikidebate, which replaced the viewpoint with the *argument* as its unit, kept arguments unsigned so "others can improve them," and is now "discontinued due to a lack of moderation and policy implementation" ([Wikiversity: Wikidebate](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Wikidebate); [Guidelines](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Wikidebate/Guidelines)).

Larry Sanger's line is the closest modern restatement of Wikinfo's model, but what shipped labels the *publisher*, not a framework. The 2019 Encyclosphere proposal wanted "all of humanity" to write and rate articles in "a completely decentralized knowledge network" ([Sanger 2019](https://larrysanger.org/2019/10/ksf/)); what exists is an aggregation format, ZWI, whose `metadata.json` carries `Publisher` and a two-number `Rating` ([ZWI file format](https://wiki.encyclosphere.org/ZWI_file_format)), with "close to **3 million ZWI files from 66 encyclopedias**" by early 2024 and no statement of how duplicate topics are presented ([KSF Q4 2023 newsletter](https://blog.encyclosphere.org/q4-2023-newsletter)). The framework label arrived only as a proposal in September 2025: Thesis 2 of the Nine Theses asks Wikipedia to "enable competing articles written within explicitly declared frameworks, each aiming at neutrality within its own framework," and Thesis 7 to "let the public rate articles" ([Sanger, Nine Theses](https://larrysanger.org/nine-theses/)); a reviewer ranked both among his "three worst ideas," calling the first "a mess" and the second a recipe for "brigading and manipulation" ([Edit History](https://edithistory.substack.com/p/larry-sangers-nine-theses-on-wikipedia)). Infogalactic promised the other extreme — the label on the *reader*: "select their preferred perspective and automatically see the version of the subject page that is closest to it," with tri-level Fact/Context/Opinion content ([Infogalaxians, Oct 2016](https://infogalactic.blogspot.com/2016/10/an-online-encyclopedia-without-bias-or.html)). Its own roadmap, last modified May 2017, marks only speed, ads and editor tiers complete ([Roadmap, Wayback](https://web.archive.org/web/2018/https://infogalactic.com/info/Infogalactic:Roadmap)); a 2020 audit found it still on MediaWiki 1.27.1 with "zero technical progress" since ([Camestros Felapton](https://camestrosfelapton.wordpress.com/2020/09/15/return-to-voxopedia/)). No definition of its "Relativity" algorithm was ever published; it was personalization without a mechanism.

Every POV-motivated fork after Wikinfo chose one POV per site, and the label migrated from the page title to the domain name: Conservapedia, RationalWiki's "snarky point of view," Justapedia (launched August 2023 as "Just an encyclopedia") ([Quillette](https://quillette.com/2023/12/11/introducing-justapedia/)), and Grokipedia, which launched 27 October 2025 with "over 800,000 articles," reached "5.6 million articles by early 2026," accepts no direct human edits, and "stopped reviewing suggested changes" around April 2026 ([Grokipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grokipedia)); an LLM-judge audit rated it "less neutral than Wikipedia by all four AI judges" ([arXiv 2607.15146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15146)). Site-level POV is cheap to run — no cross-linking, no rival-page discovery — and gives the reader nothing to compare, which is the gap the Encyclosphere tries to fill from outside. Reagle treats NPOV as "the intentional stance contributors should take while interacting," warns specifically against "POV forking in which two articles with opposing points of view arise in place of a single NPOV article" ([Reagle, ch. 3](https://reagle.org/joseph/2010/gfc/chapter-3.html)), and predicts ideological encyclopedias will keep being founded "though many soon fall into disuse" ([ch. 7](https://reagle.org/joseph/2010/gfc/chapter-7.html)); Tkacz reads forking as the sanctioned exit valve of "openness," which lets a project treat any internal policy intervention as illegitimate "closure" ([THE review](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/books/wikipedia-and-the-politics-of-openness-by-nathaniel-tkacz/2017640.article)), and the leader of the 2002 Spanish fork said it "was not intended to last. It was merely a form of pressure" ([Enciclopedia Libre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enciclopedia_Libre_Universal_en_Español)). The structural lesson both books imply: a single NPOV article is a *social* device that forces co-editing of one text, and once rival texts are allowed the forcing function is gone — a multi-POV scheme needs a replacement discipline (Wikinfo's link rule, Wikiversity's box, Sanger's declared frameworks) that an "open" project may be unable to enforce.

## Signed sibling entries coexist, but only a ranking tells them apart

The second family lets many authored texts sit under one title and leaves comparison to a score. Google Knol made competition explicit at launch: "For many topics, there will likely be competing Knols on the same subject. Competition of ideas is a good thing," with real-name authors who "put their reputation on the line" and an AdSense revenue share ([Computerworld, Dec 2007](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1569371/google-launches-its-own-wikipedia-with-a-twist.html)); synthesis was to be Google's job — "rank the knols appropriately when they appear in Google search results" ([Google Operating System](http://googlesystem.blogspot.com/2007/12/google-knol-encyclopedia-written-by.html)). Authors could set a knol to open, moderated or closed collaboration, but a rejected suggestion vanished and there was no way to publish a variant *linked* to the original ([Wikipedia: Knol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knol)). Critics saw the consequence within days: "It is left to the reader to make sense of the several competing articles on a particular topic," and "if an author loses interest in an article, it's difficult or impossible for others to take over" ([GroupLens](https://grouplens.org/blog/underwhelmed-by-googles-knol/)). Because edits needed the owner's approval, "people tend to upload their own articles rather than try to build off ones that exist, this leads to topics with dozens of competing articles" ([Plagiarism Today](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2008/09/23/why-google-knol-has-failed/)); one author's name returned **809 entries**, and an October 2011 sample found only ten knols edited in half a day ([Gwern](https://gwern.net/wikipedia-and-knol)). Traffic plateaued around 175,000 monthly views and the service closed on 30 April 2012 ([Wikipedia: Knol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knol)). Knol is the clean failure case for "multiple attributed articles" with neither a lineage relation between them nor a curated hub: the reader sees a search-results list, not a chorus.

Everything2 is the durable instance of the same shape: a *node* is a title, any number of *writeups* by different authors hang under it, "only the author of a writeup or an editor appointed by the site administrators can edit a writeup," and votes plus editor "cools" rank them; new writeups fell from **9,976 in August 2000 to 93 in February 2017** ([Wikipedia: Everything2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everything2)). Stack Overflow keeps the pattern alive with vote ordering and a single accepted answer ([Wikipedia: Stack Overflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_Overflow)); Quora enforces one answer per account and merges duplicate questions ([Quora blog](https://quorablog.quora.com/Introducing-Question-Merging)). Caulfield's reading of why Q&A works where Knol did not — multiple "choral explanations" give "multiple routes into the content," some "above your head, and some ridiculously simplified" ([Hapgood, 2016](https://hapgood.us/2016/05/13/choral-explanations/)) — points at the real variable: a *question* naturally invites several answers, whereas an encyclopedic *topic* invites one, so readers of E2 and Knol experienced multiplicity as duplication. All the working sibling-entry systems share three properties — a title-level container distinct from the entries, entries that are append-only from everyone else's point of view, and a ranking layer as the sole comparison aid — and none offers diff or cross-linking between siblings.

Ward Cunningham's Federated Wiki is the one system that made *forking a page* the answer to disagreement. Shown at IndieWebCamp 2011 and released 16 December 2013 ([Wikipedia: Federated Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_Wiki)), it was designed for "spheres of life where differences of perspective, valuation and judgment are important, even essential," so that "a 'federated' body of knowledge … can function as a kind of 'chorus' – a larger network of voices that are not stating exactly the same thing," from which "a working consensus can emerge" ([Cunningham & Mehaffy, PLoP 2013](https://hillside.net/plop/2013/papers/Group6/plop13_preprint_51.pdf)). Caulfield's motivation was the failure mode of the single page — wiki as "a relentless consensus engine" where a losing contribution "disappears" and "what Google can't see does not exist" ([Hapgood, Nov 2014](https://hapgood.us/2014/11/06/federated-education-new-directions-in-digital-collaboration/)). The mechanism is a page as JSON "containing story items with unique identifiers and a journal documenting actions (create, add, edit, move, remove, fork) with site attribution," plus a Neighborhood, Sitemap, and Twins ("Pages appearing on multiple federated sites display 'This page is also on…'") ([fedwiki/wiki README](https://github.com/fedwiki/wiki)); a paragraph dragged from another site carries its origin into the journal as an `attribution` record ([wiki-client refresh.coffee](https://github.com/fedwiki/wiki-client/blob/master/lib/refresh.coffee)). Crucially, rival versions are found not by a hub page but by a per-session computation — the same slug looked up in the sitemaps of whatever sites the reader has wandered into ([Udell, Jan 2015](https://blog.jonudell.net/2015/01/02/how-federated-wiki-neighborhoods-grow-and-change/)) — so discoverability is bounded by the neighborhood by construction.

Practice was vigorous and small. The December 2014 Fedwiki Happening ran with **about 36 participants** and produced "nearly 2,000 wiki pages" in two weeks ([Hapgood, Dec 2014](https://hapgood.us/2014/12/21/helping-the-right-ideas-find-one-another-fedwiki-happening/); [Cronin](https://catherinecronin.net/reflecting/fedwiki2014/)), but participants "struggled with forking mechanics and when/why to fork pages," and Udell concluded the system "lacks effective diff/merge capability" and "lacks notification that your page was forked" ([Udell, "A federated Wikipedia"](https://blog.jonudell.net/2015/01/22/a-federated-wikipedia/)). The comparison affordance the community asked for and never got — Caulfield's "shift hover, or click or something and get a list of alternates" for a contested paragraph — is exactly what the paragraph-with-ID data model would have permitted ([same](https://blog.jonudell.net/2015/01/22/a-federated-wikipedia/)). Caulfield's WordPress reimplementation, Wikity, was after a year "something I love and I use every day," but testers found it "easy to use, valuable, cool — and completely non-addictive" ([Hapgood, Nov 2016](https://hapgood.us/2016/11/20/wikity-one-year-later/)). Ibis (2024), the ActivityPub wiki, is fedwiki's mirror image — exactly one origin per article, diffs applied there, a `Reject` on conflict, and "`geology.wiki/article/Mountain` may be completely different from `poetry.wiki/article/Mountain`" with no linkage between them ([SocialHub](https://socialhub.activitypub.rocks/t/announcing-ibis-the-federated-wikipedia-alternative/3997)); git-backed wikis such as Gollum serve one branch at a time and expose no sibling variants in the UI ([gollum](https://github.com/gollum/gollum/)).

Only Xanadu ever made comparison the *primary* reading surface. Nelson's transpointing windows — "first published in our 1965 paper," first implemented interactively by John Walker at Autodesk in 1988 — show two parallel documents with visible bands connecting shared passages, distinguishing "connections between things which are different, and connections between things which are the same," with links that survive editing because they "remain attached to all of these surviving characters in any new version" ([Nelson, Xanalogical Structure](https://xanadu.com.au/ted/XUsurvey/xuDation.html)). The 2014 OpenXanadu demo built a document from excerpts of eight sources, "each excerpt … visibly connected to its source," in pursuit of "an entire form of literature … where documents may be closely compared side by side and closely annotated" ([Slashdot, 2014](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/14/06/06/1757210/xanadu-software-released-after-54-years-in-the-making)). Fedwiki's twins and Knol's rankings answer "are there other versions?"; Xanadu answers "which parts do they share and where do they diverge?" — and no wiki adopted it.

## Argument systems swap the viewpoint for the claim, and pay for it in moderators

The third family stops labeling viewpoints at all and labels *claims* with a stance toward a parent. Rittel's IBIS (1970) is the grammar everything descends from: maps "generally start with Questions," "Ideas are neutral," and "the place for rationale, opinion, facts, data, rhetoric, etc. is in the Pros and Cons" ([Conklin, IBIS: A Tool for All Reasons](https://www.cognexus.org/IBIS-A_Tool_for_All_Reasons.pdf)). Its software line — gIBIS, QuestMap, Compendium — converged on a single lesson: the notation is trivial to learn and expensive to capture, so it survived only where a trained facilitator drives it live in meetings; Buckingham Shum and Hammond found in 1994 that the research "has failed to substantiate convincingly" either that argumentation-structured rationale is useful or that designers can use it ([IJHCS 1994](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581984710299)), and Compendium's last release was January 2014 ([Wikipedia: Compendium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compendium_(software))). Cohere's move to user-labelled links with only a positive/neutral/negative valence fixed ([Buckingham Shum, 2008](https://simon.buckinghamshum.net/2008/03/cohere-towards-web2-argumentation/)) is the lineage's own admission that fixed link ontologies do not survive volunteers.

The debate wikis carried the same grammar to the public and mostly died of it. Debatepedia (2006/7, on modified MediaWiki) laid out a yes/no question with pros left and cons right ([if:book, 2007](https://futureofthebook.org/blog/2007/02/21/an_encyclopedia_of_arguments/)) — a format a contemporary critic called "logic switches, flipped on or off … a crude reduction of what an argument really is," calling for "some way to present a more shaded spectrum of thought" ([same](https://futureofthebook.org/blog/2007/02/21/an_encyclopedia_of_arguments/)). It has been read-only since November 2011; Hyperdébat since 2017, Argumentrix since 2013, Debate.org closed June 2022, Argüman offline 2023 ([Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias); [DebateArt](https://www.debateart.com/forum/topics/7604-ddo-is-shutting-down); [Argüman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arg%C3%BCman)). The comparison of thirteen debate encyclopedias credits what worked — "recursive argument levels," "rich citations and quotations" — and what failed: "shallow argument depth," weak citations, "lack of interlinking between debates," and "skeletal interfaces" ([Wikidébats comparison](https://en.wikidebates.org/wiki/Project:Comparison_of_Existing_Debate_Encyclopedias)). The survivors gate everything. Kialo (August 2017) roots a debate in a thesis; each claim is Pro or Con *relative to its parent*, users cast 1–5 impact votes, and a "Perspectives" feature filters ratings by supporters, opposers and moderators; by July 2023 it held **18,000+ public debates and 720,000+ claims** ([Wikipedia: Kialo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kialo)), with "moderators vet[ting] every piece" ([GraphNLI, arXiv 2202.08175](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.08175)); across 1,560 discussion trees "the vast majority" had 40–60% supporting edges ([arXiv 1906.04148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.04148)). Its recorded criticisms are "oversimplified argument structure limiting nuance," closed data, and — from the comparison — that it "lacks quotes and named references" ([Wikipedia: Kialo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kialo)). Two structural facts transfer. The *edge*, not the node, carries the side label, so one claim can be a con in one branch and a pro in another. And unsigned text was the wiki norm precisely because "Signing arguments discourages others from improving them" ([Wikidebate/Guidelines](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Wikidebate/Guidelines)), while named "proponent lists" appear only at ProCon, which pays journalists to build them — so a system that wants both editable position text and named advocates must separate "who holds this position" from "who wrote this sentence."

The research deployments supply the only measured numbers. MIT's Deliberatorium enforces one point per post — an issue, an idea, or a pro/con argument — with "every issue, idea, or argument appear[ing] once," moderator-certified before publication ([Participedia](https://participedia.net/case/application-of-deliberatorium-in-italy)). In its April 2012 Italian Democratic Party deployment, **640 enrolled, 373 logged in, 194 posted (30%)**; the forum condition cost 163 hours of moderation plus post-processing against the map's 42, and the map produced 78 ideas with roughly twice the arguments per idea against the forum's 290 thinly argued ones — with the interface judged "bulky and difficult to navigate" ([same](https://participedia.net/case/application-of-deliberatorium-in-italy)). ConsiderIt's Living Voters Guide (Washington ballot measures, September 2010) is the closest existing analogue to "schools of thought side by side": each reader builds a personal pro/con list from others' points, and the results page shows a **stance histogram of seven groups** — clicking a bar reveals "a ranked list of the most important points for those who took that particular stance," ranked by a *diverse appeal* term (entropy of inclusion across stance groups) that also defeats brigading ([Kriplean et al., CSCW 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)). Of 678 non-empty lists, **41.4% included both a pro and a con** (53.0% excluding one-point lists), 33.7% of cross-inclusions came from an author of the opposing stance, and the authors attribute this partly to the absence of identity cues — "ConsiderIt does not provide group cues to activate political identity" — while naming "evaluating the trustworthiness of these claims" as the biggest problem readers faced ([same](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)). Polis goes the other way and *discovers* positions from votes on ≤140-character statements with no replies at all, clustering people rather than writing positions ([compdemocracy: Polis](https://compdemocracy.org/Polis/)); vTaiwan's Uber consultation in 2015 got statements past 80% cross-group agreement and a ministry pledge to ratify the result ([vTaiwan case](https://compdemocracy.org/case-studies/2014-vtaiwan/)), which is why every Polis deployment pairs it with a human drafting stage.

The most exhaustive current attempt is the Society Library, whose positions are genuinely plural rather than binary: it first collects arguments "from 'all' points of view to discover the questions which society is arguing about," then enumerates "all possible answers" and links them by "20+ logical relationships" ([Society Library, Debate Mapping](https://www.societylibrary.org/debate-mapping-program)). Its Diablo Canyon map holds **5,862 arguments, claims and evidence from 5,000+ references**, built "in an 8 month period" and "suddenly stopped at the ending of the grant period" ([Diablo Canyon Debate](https://www.societylibrary.org/diablo-canyon-debate)); its founder later put the cost at about 8,000 research hours and relocated the audience from readers to "policymakers, researchers, and decision-makers—not Twitter combatants," including AI labs ([Joyce, May 2026](https://justjamiejoyce.substack.com/p/your-attempt-to-refute-argument-maps)). The maps live in Debate Map, a DAG of claim and argument nodes with truth and relevance ratings and multiple *phrasings* per node so "nodes can be reused across multiple parent nodes" ([debate-map/app](https://github.com/debate-map/app)); the organisation's answer to maintenance is an LLM extraction pipeline ([Society Library Updates](https://www.societylibrary.org/society-library-updates)). The sceptical case is on record in full: "'Debate' almost never corresponds to mappable arguments," most disputes hinge on people who "weigh theoretical and empirical evidence differently," and the closest working format, r/changemyview, is "not any kind of mechanical change to the argument format" but a community norm ([Astral Codex Ten, April 2026](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/your-attempt-to-solve-debate-will)). Reader-side evidence is thin on all sides: 400 undergraduates who studied an argument map "scored higher than those who studied text, on tests of memory, though not comprehension" ([Dwyer et al. 2010](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ872591)); 87% of one EFL class agreed that "seeing people's ideas on two opposing sides" deepened their understanding ([Mahoney 2022](https://fler.rikkyo.ac.jp/journal/jc0e3e00000000a9-att/JFLER(2022_Vol.3)_13_Mahoney.pdf)); ConsiderIt's non-bounce visitors stayed 10 minutes 39 seconds over 6.1 pages and rated reading *opposing* points as significantly more helpful than reading points while undecided ([Kriplean et al.](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~borning/papers/kriplean-cscw2012.pdf)).

A last pair of mechanisms are viewpoint-aware without representing viewpoints as content. X's Community Notes fits r̂ = μ + iᵤ + iₙ + fᵤ·fₙ with a one-dimensional latent factor per rater and note, and shows a note only when its intercept reaches 0.40 — which, given heavier regularization on the intercept, "in practice requires that notes are rated by raters with diverse factors before a note gets a label" ([Community Notes ranking docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/twitter/communitynotes/main/documentation/under-the-hood/ranking-notes.md)); selected notes cut agreement with a misleading tweet by about 26% with no Democrat/Republican difference ([Wojcik et al., arXiv 2210.15723](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15723)). A September 2026 refit on 212.9M ratings found a second, near-independent institutional-trust axis, and among low-polarization notes publication fell "from 71.5% to 11.7%" as second-axis disagreement rose — a rank-1 model "loses the second coordinate" ([Andreou & Sirivianos](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.21496)). The Habermas Machine drafts one group statement from a per-person reward model and critique rounds; with up to 2,110 UK participants, more than 56% preferred AI statements to a human mediator's, but "after processing the critiques, the HM tended to over-weight minority viewpoints," and its authors flag "short, bland statements that say little of substance" as the scaling risk ([Knight Institute](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/can-ai-mediation-improve-democratic-deliberation); [Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adq2852)). Generative social choice instead outputs a *proportionally representative slate*: 84 of 100 U.S. respondents on abortion felt "excellently" or "exceptionally" represented by five statements ([Fish et al., EC 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.01291)). Neither bridging nor consensus drafting *presents* several positions; they choose one thing several factions accept, and the transferable idea is Community Notes' *test* — is approval of this text explained by which camp you are in? — rather than its output.

## Products label the outlet, reference works label the venue, scripture tools align the verse

The consumer products that advertise "all sides" label the *source*, not the argument. AllSides (2012) uses five buckets — Left, Lean Left, Center, Lean Right, Right — which it says "sacrifice precision in favor of simplicity" ([Wikipedia: AllSides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AllSides)); its own director conceded "we have to put somebody in a category when, in reality, it's kind of a spectrum" ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/fact-checking/media-literacy/2021/should-you-trust-media-bias-charts/)). Ground News uses seven buckets computed as the *average* of AllSides, Ad Fontes and Media Bias/Fact Check, defines a "blindspot" quantitatively (fewer than 10 left-leaning sources and right-leaning coverage ≥ 33%, for a left blindspot) ([Ground News rating system](https://ground.news/rating-system)), and reached roughly 8 million visits in July 2025; CJR's objection is that the product conflates distance from other coverage with truth — "truth is not a measure of how far a story strays from other coverage" ([CJR](https://www.cjr.org/analysis/the-business-of-balance-ground-news.php)). Ad Fontes rates on continuous coordinates (bias −42 to +42, reliability 0–64, three analysts of differing politics per item) ([Ad Fontes methodology](https://adfontesmedia.com/methodology-white-paper/)), and its founder still admits "some nuance has to go away when it's a graphic" ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/fact-checking/media-literacy/2021/should-you-trust-media-bias-charts/)). In every one of these, the perspective text is whatever the outlet happened to write, so the "sides" rarely answer the same question; the unit of comparison is the whole article.

Four designs make the perspectives parallel by construction. ProCon (2004; Britannica since May 2020, with a 25-year commitment) puts editor-labeled Pro and Con arguments on *one* question in two columns — the only news-adjacent product whose label attaches to the claim rather than the outlet ([Wikipedia: ProCon.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ProCon.org); [Hawley Troxell](https://hawleytroxell.com/2020/07/encyclopedia-britannica-acquires-procon-org-commits-to-running-for-25-years/)). Verity separates a **Facts** block ("where sources on opposite sides of the relevant controversy agree") from **Narratives** labeled per axis — left/right, pro-/anti-establishment, sometimes topic-specific such as Pro-/Anti-China — with outlets scored by ChatGPT 4.1 and every narrative human-edited ([Verity FAQ](https://www.improvethenews.org/faq)); its narratives are *written to be parallel*, each the strongest case on its side about the same story. Tangle's newsletter fixes per-perspective sections ("What the left/right are saying," "My take," "Staff dissent") for 500,000+ readers but states no criteria for what counts as left or right ([Tangle: About](https://www.readtangle.com/about/)); The Flip Side runs "adjacent columns" for 200,000+ subscribers with no disclosed classification method ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91162993/flip-side-newsletter-bipartisanship)). Zondervan's Counterpoints series is the only format found that combines the same question, separate *signed* essays, explicit view labels in the table of contents, and cross-responses: "three to five people make their case … the others make short critical responses, and the original author then has a chance to briefly respond," across 43 volumes ([Accordance](https://www.accordancebible.com/article/counterpoints-series/); [Logos](https://www.logos.com/product/259817/zondervan-counterpoints-series)). The perspectives there address *each other*, not just the reader — the one property no wiki in this survey has.

Reference works label by *venue*. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy mandates entries that are "objective/neutral analyses/surveys" presenting "the important arguments that have been put forward on both sides," bans first-person advocacy, and says nothing about competing entries on one topic ([SEP Guidelines](https://plato.stanford.edu/guidelines.html)); traditions get their own entries, as its comparative-philosophy entry's pointers to separate Buddhist, Confucian and Daoist entries show ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/comparphil-chiwes/)). The Catholic Encyclopedia (1907–1914) was written "from the Roman Catholic point of view" under *nihil obstat* and *imprimatur* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia)); Theopedia "locates itself within the Reformed theology tradition" ([Oxford ARCH](http://arch.oucs.ox.ac.uk/detail/20532/index.html)). Comparing "the Catholic view" and "the Jewish view" of a topic means two works in two tabs, with article boundaries that do not line up. The PhilPapers Survey (100 questions, ~2,000 respondents in 2020) is worth noting as the closest thing to a *canonical* taxonomy of named positions per question — compatibilism / libertarianism / no free will — with a fixed answer vocabulary ([PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/rec/BOUPOP-3); [Philosophers' Imprint](https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/phimp/article/2109/galley/2463/view/)); it is a ready-made column set for a rows-are-questions, columns-are-positions table.

Passage-level alignment exists only where a coordinate system pre-exists. BibleHub stacks roughly thirty commentaries under one verse, each headed by the *work* — Calvin, Haydock, Gill, Matthew Henry — with **no denominational label** ([BibleHub John 1:1](https://biblehub.com/commentaries/john/1-1.htm)); Bible Gateway shows up to five verse-aligned translation columns; Sefaria opens a side panel of related texts "organized by category" on click of any segment, for 775,000 monthly users ([Wikipedia: Sefaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sefaria)); Perseus's Scaife Viewer allows "up to seven windows side-by-side" with lineated commentary notes ([SCS review](https://classicalstudies.org/scs-blog/stephensansom/review-perseus-digital-library-scaife-viewer)). Comparison charts are the most parallel and least attributable format: ReligionFacts' three-column chart runs ~30 rows from founders to afterlife, authored by an editor with "no affiliation to any religion" ([ReligionFacts](https://religionfacts.com/charts/christianity-islam-judaism)); Rose's 12-denomination × 11-row fold-out ([Amazon listing](https://www.amazon.com/Denominations-Comparison-Rose-Publishing/dp/1890947350)); Patheos lets a reader pick up to three traditions and drill into a section ([Patheos Side by Side](https://www.patheos.com/library/lenses/side-by-side)). Wikipedia's "Theological differences between the Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church" is prose in topic sections with no table, so a reader cannot select the Filioque claim and see both sides' own wording ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theological_differences_between_the_Catholic_Church_and_the_Eastern_Orthodox_Church)). No site was found where each column of a chart is written by an adherent of that column's tradition.

The typographic precedents fix *who speaks where* on the page, which is precisely what an article-level design needs. Origen's Hexapla set six versions "in parallel vertical columns" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/biblical-literature/Origens-Hexapla)); Griesbach's 1776 synopsis, "instead of harmonizing them," displayed the Gospels side by side "making both similarities and divergences apparent" ([Synoptic Gospels Primer](https://virtualreligion.net/primer/griesb.html)). The Talmud page puts the shared text in the centre with Rashi always on the inner margin and Tosafot always on the outer ([Hadran](https://hadran.org.il/terms/the-layout-of-a-talmud-page/)), and the voices disagree by design — Tosafot "consist mainly of strictures on Rashi's commentary" ([Wikipedia: Tosafot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tosafot)). Diderot's *Encyclopédie* used **61,700 cross-references** that "intentionally and blatantly put contradictory articles into dialogue" ([DH abstract](https://dh-abstracts.library.virginia.edu/works/75)). Two layouts recur: *columns* (perspectives as peers, aligned passage by passage) and *centre-plus-margins* (one shared text with named voices around it that may attack each other), and every precedent gives each voice a stable slot rather than a reorderable list.

## Research shows neutrality converges only with crowds, and plurality lives beside the article

The empirical Wikipedia literature explains where a labeled design would earn its keep. Reagle reads NPOV as a collaborative *stance* — "assert facts, including facts about opinions—but do not assert the opinions themselves" — operationalized through "writing for the enemy," and concedes "neutral" cannot be defined except with equally problematic terms ([Reagle, ch. 3](https://reagle.org/joseph/2010/gfc/chapter-3.html)); Matei and Dobrescu find that "ambiguity is at the heart of the policy process on Wikipedia," "not extraneous, but a central ingredient," and that NPOV is "frequently used as a justification for personal biases" ([Matei](http://matei.org/ithink/ambiguity-conflict-wikipedia/)). Kittur et al. measured ~3.9 million reverts, found **more unique editors negatively correlated with conflict** ("having more points of view can defuse conflict"), and flagged protecting anonymous minority opinion as an open design question ([Kittur et al., CHI 2007](https://gwern.net/doc/wikipedia/2007-kittur.pdf)). Yasseri et al. found fewer than 1% of pages controversial and three trajectories — consensus, cycles of temporary consensus driven by "a constant influx of new editors," and "never-ending wars" with "a steady flow of replacement armies" ([Yasseri et al., PLoS ONE 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1202.3643)). Greenstein and Zhu found early U.S.-politics articles "leaned Democrat" with slant diminishing over time, but supported only a *narrow* Linus' Law because "the majority of articles receive little attention" ([ISR 2016](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/isre.2016.0643)); against Britannica, Wikipedia was "more slanted toward Democratic views," yet "the bias on a per word basis hardly differs" ([MISQ 2018](https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol42/iss3/14/)). NPOV tags reduce biased language in the article but produce "no significant change" in the tagged editors' later word usage — articles converge, editors do not ([Pavalanathan et al., CSCW 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06951)). Three literatures therefore agree on a shape: neutrality is a convergence process needing many editors, and it stalls on hot, high-conflict topics and on thin-attention ones — the residue a labeled design addresses, not the median article. Recasens et al.'s split between *framing* bias (perspective-specific words) and *epistemological* bias (presupposed propositions) ([ACL 2013](https://aclanthology.org/P13-1162/)) offers a usable editorial rule: perspective sections may carry framing bias by design, while the shared factual spine must not carry epistemological bias.

The tools that succeeded with users put plurality *beside* one article by treating language editions as de facto points of view. Hecht and Gergle showed 25 editions diverge in both which concepts they include and how they describe them ([CHI 2010](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/pubs/CHI2010-HechtAndGergle-TowerOfBabel.pdf)); Omnipedia aligned 25 editions' articles on one topic so users "strategically compared how language editions defined concepts" ([CHI 2012](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/pubs/CHI2012-BaoEtAl-Omnipedia.pdf)); Manypedia's side-by-side two-edition view coined the *Linguistic Point of View*, with Greek Wikipedia describing Northern Cyprus as "under Turkish occupation since 1974" and Turkish Wikipedia as "an independent state" ([First Monday 2013](https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/download/3939/3382)); Contropedia overlays *which topics within an article* were most controversial ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.08721)); and WikiGap (2025) surfaces facts present in other editions but absent from English, with **+32 usability points** over interlanguage links and users who "reconsidered the completeness of English Wikipedia" ([arXiv 2505.24195](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24195)). Dispute Finder (2010) highlighted text resembling known disputed claims and showed "a list of articles that support other points of view" ([WWW 2010](https://www.ra.ethz.ch/cdstore/www2010/www/p341.pdf)). Searches for "multiperspective wiki," "pluralistic encyclopedia," "polyvocal" and "multivocal" plus wiki returned no CSCW, CHI or Hypertext paper describing such a system; the "polyvocal" hits are all museum and heritage work.

Knowledge representation has three mature answers, each isolating contradiction at a different grain. Cyc's microtheories are each "internally consistent" while "different ones may include incompatible facts" ([Yuret, Cyc notes](https://www2.denizyuret.com/pub/cyc96/node17.html)), and the reported cost is the concrete risk of letting perspectives multiply freely: "as more contexts were created it became difficult and time consuming for the knowledge enterer to select the most adequate context for new assertions" ([Context in CYC](https://www.unav.es/gep/TesisDoctorales/MarkGTesis.pdf)). The ViewPoints framework holds that "maintaining global consistency at all times is an unnecessary burden … it is often desirable to tolerate and even encourage inconsistency," defining consistency only by explicit inter-ViewPoint rules and logging a work record for resolution ([Easterbrook et al. 1994](http://www.cs.toronto.edu/~sme/papers/1994/AID94_conflict_paper.pdf)). Wikidata is the only one running at wiki scale, and its rule is the sharpest precedent: "different sources may provide contradicting information, it's possible to represent a plurality of perspectives," disputes go to consensus, and "**Ranks are not a way of asserting your view for a disputed value, but instead are used for communicating the consensus opinion**" ([Help:Statements](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Statements/en); [Help:Ranking](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Ranking)) — separating *which views exist* (any, if sourced and qualified) from *which is mainstream* (a community judgment). In practice those mechanisms are rare and "often ambiguous" in use ([Di Pasquale et al., 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/SW-243686)); the polyvocal-web agenda notes that knowledge graphs "reflect the popular or majority vote … an inherent artifact of the frequency bias" ([van Erp & de Boer, ESWC 2021](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-77385-4_30)), and a 2026 framework sorts statements into doxastic, epistemic and conjectural "cognitive worlds" so "delusions" and disagreements do not collapse into inconsistency ([Vitali & Pasqual](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15246)).

The LLM-era work supplies the one automatable step and a warning. STORM's pre-writing stage "discover[s] diverse perspectives" by mining related Wikipedia articles, simulates per-perspective interviews with a grounded expert, and gains +25 points on organization — but the perspectives shape the outline and are *not* preserved as labels in the final article ([Shao et al., NAACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)). PERSPECTRUM's claim → perspective(stance) → evidence schema over 11,822 claims is the pre-LLM template for labeled multi-perspective output ([Chen et al., NAACL 2019](https://aclanthology.org/N19-1053/)); PerSphere's benchmark of paired opposing claims finds LLMs "struggle with long context and perspective extraction" ([arXiv 2412.12588](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12588)), echoed by the PerAnsSumm shared task ([PerAnsSumm](https://peranssumm.github.io/docs/)). The pluralistic-alignment roadmap distinguishes *Overton* ("a spectrum of reasonable responses"), *steerable* and *distributional* pluralism and reports that "standard alignment procedures might reduce distributional pluralism" ([Sorensen et al., ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05070)); Value Kaleidoscope's premise is that "AI systems fit to averages by default, washing out potentially irreducible value conflicts" ([arXiv 2309.00779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00779)). No 2022–2026 paper in the corpus generates a Wikipedia-style article with perspectives retained as labeled sections; STORM merges, and the summarizers summarize per perspective but not as encyclopedia articles. The perspective-enumeration step is proven automatable; the last step — emit per-perspective sections instead of one outline — is the change nobody has published.

## Conclusion

The record reframes the question. "Multiple labeled points of view per article" has not failed; it has barely been attempted, and the one production instance (Wikinfo) worked as a naming convention plus a linking rule, with its unknowns — how often the mechanism was used, whether anyone read the second page — never measured. What *has* failed, repeatedly and measurably, is every scheme that made plurality cheap to produce and expensive to read: Knol's dozens of unranked siblings, fedwiki's twins with no diff, the two-column debate wikis with no interlinking. And what has survived pairs a small number of named authors owning a position's text with editorial gatekeeping (ProCon, Counterpoints, Kialo, Verity) — never open-contribution trees. The evidence therefore points at a specific shape for a new attempt: label the *text* rather than the author's outlet, from a declared taxonomy the reader can see whole (PhilPapers' option lists, Rose's twelve denominations, Verity's per-story axes); give each perspective a fixed slot the way Rashi is always inner and Tosafot always outer; separate "who holds this position" from "who wrote this sentence," which is the reconciliation Wikidebate's unsigned norm and ProCon's paid proponent lists never achieved; and put structure in at authoring time by a few editors, since the Deliberatorium's numbers show up-front moderation quarters the total cost but is felt as a barrier by contributors.

The novel piece is the comparison layer, because nothing in the survey supplies it. Passage-level comparison of rival prose needs a shared coordinate system; scripture tools inherit verse numbers, Kialo has the thesis node, and free-form articles must manufacture one — a quote anchor into a shared factual spine is the analogue, letting a reader ask what the Reformed section says about *this sentence* of the Catholic one. That spine can be gated by Community Notes' test rather than its output — a sentence enters the common section only when readers from different labeled perspectives accept it, with everything else staying in a perspective section, and the 2026 "two fault lines" result warns that a single left/right label set is too coarse to make that gate honest. What the fedwiki community asked for in January 2015 and no one shipped — variants surfaced per paragraph, provenance carried in the artifact, and a side-by-side view that draws the shared spans between rival texts, which is Xanadu's picture of the reading side — remains the unbuilt combination, and the tools that came closest with users (Omnipedia, Manypedia, WikiGap) all did it by comparing *beside* the article rather than editing within it.
